Bechirah Chadashah: The Daily Renewal of Free Choice

It’s 9 PM and you just fell. The rest of the day feels finished - whatever I do now, today is a write-off. And there is truth in that. After a fall the spiral is real, and making a complete u-turn the same night is hard.


But watch what the write-off costs. One bad night becomes a lost day. A lost day becomes a lost week. Then it’s “the streak is broken anyway, I’ll start again Rosh Chodesh.” That is how one fall stretches into a month. The fall did the small damage. The days after it did the rest. We feel b’reshus libam - not masters of our own hearts but owned by them (Bereishis Rabbah 34:10).


Why does this spiraling happen? Explains the Sfas Emes (Re’eh, 5633) that once a person sins, his (full) bechira leaves him. There is a real negative momentum.


But does it really need to last days? Not at all. The first pasuk in this week’s parsha is “See, I set before you today a blessing and a curse” (Devarim 11:26). This means, says the Sfas Emes, that each morning you are gifted bechira chadasha - a fresh new ability to use your free choice again. You wake up fresh, and just as Hashem renews the world each day - המחדש בטובו בכל יום תמיד מעשה בראשית - He also restores your full power of bechira chofshis, no matter what happened yesterday.


And the Chiddushei HaRim explains that it’s because of this gift that each morning, we make the bracha of hanosein lasechvi binah lehavchin bein yom u’vein layla. We are thanking Hashem for the restored ability to know the difference between night and day, and to make the right choice - to choose bracha. (Sfas Emes 5631 and 5633; and see Mishnah Berurah 46:4, based on the Rosh, and Rashi to Iyov 38:36 that “sechvi” means a person’s heart.)


So the rule is short: don’t let a fall last longer than one day. There is no such thing as a lost week - the world is not renewed weekly. If you fell today, you may not be able to salvage today; don’t spend the night trying. Make a plan for the morning, and hold onto this even though you can’t feel it tonight - you will wake up with the koach to choose again.


You Gotta Taste the Sweetness

By @hashemisonmyside (Current streak: 530 days)


I want to take a moment to express my emotions.


It’s been a long journey, with plenty of bumps along the way, but baruch Hashem, I can’t thank Him enough to have reached this moment, to look back and see what it means to fight for Hashem.


Like the pasuk says this week, “Re’eh anochi nosein lifneichem bracha u’klala.” Living in the mud is klala. Living a pure life is true bracha. It takes tremendous kochos hanefesh to get to this point, but afterward, you see how sweet and beautiful it is.


It’s like someone who has never tasted sugar. You can’t explain to him what “sweet” means. You gotta taste it and live it.


So chevra, get your feet wet and jump into the race. It’s a tough journey, but it’s worth sweating it through.


WISDOM FROM THE FORUM

“Does It Ever Stop Feeling Like
Deprivation?”

It’s an honest question, and it fits Re’eh. The parsha sets bracha and klala in front of us, but in early recovery the “blessing” can feel like nothing more than giving something up. Does that ever change? The chevra’s answer is yes - though not quite in the way you’d expect.


Nobody there pretends the beginning is easy. @strike put the raw feeling into words: “It seems like stopping an addiction is a never-ending battle that never, ever, ever gets easier.” @onemomentatatime doesn’t argue with him: “That is truly what it feels like in the beginning. A grind of never-ending struggle.” If that is where you are right now, you’re not failing. You’re at the start.


But he finishes the thought: “While it never goes away completely, it gets considerably better and easier. You just gotta get past the rough start.” @dd adds the ingredient that speeds it up - it “will get much easier once you have a network of guys and the site to help you out.” And don’t expect a straight climb. @ozivezimraskah: “For me it’s definitely ups and downs.” A hard stretch after an easier one isn’t a collapse, it’s the shape of the road.


The deepest answer in the thread is a reframe. @tester613 described his turning point: he realized that “my addiction was preventing me from living life to its fullest, and not the opposite.” He had always assumed sobriety was the thing taking life away, when the addiction was the thief all along. Once that clicked, staying clean stopped being about giving something up and became about “rebuilding my life and learning how to live by connecting with the value system that I believe in.” @cordnoy says it plainer: the real work helps you “deal with life, which makes lust not necessary.”


Two things members lean on when it still feels raw. Memory - @afreshstart39 stays clean “because of the pain that I was in when I wasn’t,” and @tzvi credits “the newcomer who helps me remember the pain.” And connection - @dov calls out to the Ribono Shel Olam in the moment of nisayon, and @rashkebehag finds that simply coming to the forum and reading posts makes the Yetzer Hara “evaporate.”


So yes, it gets better. Not perfectly, and not by the urges vanishing overnight, but the grinding sense of deprivation really does lift. Or in the words of this week’s member: right now it may feel like giving something up, but “you gotta taste it and live it.” Get past the rough start, and the bracha starts to taste like bracha.

10 REALLY DUMB MISTAKES · #10

Criticizing Your Spouse

By Rabbi Benzion Shafier (TheShmuz.com)


We’ve reached the final mistake, and Rabbi Shafier calls it maybe the worst of them all: criticizing your spouse. This one goes deep, all the way down to why Hashem created us the way He did.


The Distant Husband


A woman once came to Rabbi Shafier, baffled and hurt. Her husband had grown cold and distant, and she couldn’t understand why. “I’m a wonderful balabusta,” she said. “The house is always clean, meals are always ready, I do his laundry, everyone says how nice I am. All I want is shalom bayis. Why won’t he spend time with me?”


As they spoke, something surfaced. She corrected him at the Shabbos table, in front of the married kids. And at the dry cleaners. And at the supermarket. And at the bank. Anywhere and everywhere. By nature she was intensely self-critical, she’d been hard on herself since she was a little girl, and when she married, she generously extended that same relentless correcting to her husband. No matter what he said or how he said it, she found something to fix.


She saw these as small, well-meaning “corrections.” He experienced them as being punched and kicked all day long. You can be the nicest person in the world, but if your spouse feels constantly under attack, they simply won’t want to be near you. She was committing the tenth and perhaps worst really dumb mistake: criticizing your spouse.


Why Yaakov Was Afraid


There’s a striking Rashi at the start of Devarim. Yaakov Avinu waited until the very end of his life to give his son Reuven rebuke, and explained: “If you’re wondering why I didn’t rebuke you all these years, it’s because I was afraid that if I did, you would leave me and cling to my brother Eisav.” (Rashi, Devarim 1:3)


This is hard to understand. Yaakov and Reuven had a relationship of enormous love and respect; Reuven accepted his father as his rebbi and guide. And Yaakov always spoke with love. So why would a little rebuke risk driving Reuven all the way to Eisav?


The answer reveals how criticism actually works on a human being, and it starts with why Hashem built us the way He did.


Why Criticism Cuts So Deep


Rabbi Shafier explains through a fascinating idea. Deep within every person is a neshama, so pure and lofty that it would never willingly choose to do wrong; it recognizes that every sin causes real, lasting damage, the way you’d never drink bleach or put your hand in fire. So how does a person with such a neshama have free will at all? Hashem gave us imagination, the power to make our own rationalizations feel true, to tell ourselves a story in which whatever we want to do is actually fine. That imaginative power is what levels the playing field and lets us genuinely choose.


But it has a side effect. Because deep down every person needs to be good and worthy, that’s the neshama, we are constantly, quietly narrating our own innocence. So when someone criticizes you, it collides head-on with that deep need. They’re saying you did something wrong. But a good person wouldn’t do wrong, so what they’re really saying, what you hear, is that you’re not good. That you’re not worthy. And that is so painful that even when the criticism is completely true, something inside you rejects it, defends, pulls away.


(Rabbi Shafier illustrates human self-justification with Dale Carnegie’s story of the 1930s cop-killer “Two-Gun” Crowley, who, bleeding from a shootout he started by murdering a policeman for no reason, wrote, “Under my coat is a weary heart, but a kind one, one that would do nobody any harm.” The warden of Sing Sing reported that virtually none of his inmates believed themselves guilty. If hardened killers can narrate their own innocence, imagine how instinctively the rest of us do it.)


This is why Yaakov was afraid. Not because Reuven was immature, but because criticism, even loving, even accurate, even from someone you deeply respect, creates a sense of rejection so hard to bear that the natural response is to pull away from the person delivering it. And the greater that person is in your eyes, the more the criticism wounds, and the wider the wedge it drives.


The Soup Is Salty


Because marriage is delicate and criticism is toxic, Rabbi Shafier is ruthless about his own words. His wife won’t even ask him if her cooking tastes right, “he’ll never tell me if he thinks it doesn’t.” And she’s correct. He learned long ago that when a husband says the soup is too salty, his wife doesn’t hear a comment about soup. She hears: I failed. I didn’t please him. I’m not good enough. He might as well have insulted her to her face. He didn’t mean anything hurtful, but that’s how it lands.


His rule: if you want a happy marriage, avoid any hint, suggestion, insinuation, or trace of criticism, in any shade or flavor. Treat it like something contagious. Wear a mask, wash your hands, and stay six feet away.


The Three Rules of Criticism


So before you criticize anyone, but especially your spouse, remember the three rules of criticism:


Rule 1: Don’t do it.
Rule 2: Don’t do it.
Rule 3: Don’t do it.


Don’t do it, because it hurts. Don’t do it, because it pushes people away. And most of all, don’t do it, because it doesn’t work. We all carry the fantasy that if we just point out what our spouse is doing wrong, they’ll stop. It never works. All it does is wound them, deepen the problem, and drive you apart. Even when your intentions are pure and the words are entirely for their good, criticism is a separator that accomplishes nothing and pulls people apart.


A Note for the Recovery Journey


There’s a reason this closes our series so fittingly. Everything Rabbi Shafier says about how criticism lands on a spouse is exactly how self-criticism lands on you. When you berate yourself after a fall, “I’m worthless, I’m disgusting, I’ll never change”, your neshama hears the same devastating message a criticized spouse hears, and the natural response is the same: to pull away, to give up, to run. The shame doesn’t inspire teshuva; it drives the next fall. So take the three rules and turn them inward too. Correct your actions when you slip, gently and honestly, but don’t attack your self. You’ll get much further being a friend to yourself than an enemy, exactly the way you would with a spouse.


Your Action Steps This Week


  1. Go one full day without correcting your spouse about anything, not the driving, not the story they told slightly wrong, not the soup. Just notice how the day feels.
  2. Catch one criticism before it leaves your mouth, and either say nothing or find the affirming thing to say instead.
  3. Turn the three rules on yourself. The next time you fall or slip, address the action without assaulting your worth. Speak to yourself the way Yaakov spoke to Reuven: with love, and with the awareness that harshness only pushes a person away, even when that person is you.
