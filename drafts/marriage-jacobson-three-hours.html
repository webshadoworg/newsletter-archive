MARRIAGE

Why do good marriages drift?

Based on a shiur by Rabbi YY Jacobson

Leave a marriage alone and in most cases it does not hold its position. Nothing has to go wrong for that to happen. The drift comes on its own, and it only runs in one direction, because couples do not naturally drift back together.

The Baal HaTanya writes in Likutei Torah that a man and a woman are fire and water. It is worth sitting with that for a second, because it is not a compliment to either of them. Fire and water do not seek each other out. Leave them alone in a room and they do not grow close, one of them wins. The only way they share a space at all is inside a vessel built to hold both of them, and a vessel does not appear on its own. Somebody has to put it there and keep it there.

So drifting is not evidence that you married the wrong person. It is the baseline. The marriage you have after ten years is not the marriage you built, it is the marriage you kept up.

How often does it need attention? There is research that puts it at roughly every three hours. Not a conversation every three hours. Just a signal that the other one is still in.

Three hours sounds ridiculous, until you notice that we already run our most important relationship on exactly that clock. Shacharis, Mincha, Maariv. Krias Shema in the morning and Krias Shema at night. We do not daven Mincha because Shacharis failed. We daven Mincha because a person who stood in the morning and said I am here is no longer standing in that place by the middle of the afternoon. Attention leaks. The Anshei Knesses HaGedolah understood that and built the shape of the day around it. Needing Mincha is not a weakness, it is the design. A marriage runs on the same leak, except that nobody hands you a schedule for it.

The timing is forgiving, which is the part worth hearing. Missing three hours costs almost nothing. Catch it by six, catch it by nine, and you are back where you were. The damage is never in the gap, it is in the gap nobody closes. Miss a day, then two, then three, and a week later two people who love each other are living on separate planets, and neither of them can point to the thing that did it.

That is what makes it so easy to miss. Nothing visible goes wrong. A plant that was watered last week still looks fine for a while. A lamp with no oil in it keeps burning for a while on yesterday’s oil. But you are not getting the Chanukah miracle every week. If you want the flame there tonight, you put the oil in today.

This week: attach one message to your wife to each of the three times you already stop to daven. Not what time you are coming home, not what is needed from the store. One sentence whose only job is to tell her that you are here and you are thinking about her. A text counts. And if you notice it has been since yesterday, that is not a failed week. Say something now. Closing the gap is the whole skill.
