The Twins Within Us

After almost twenty years of davening, Yitzchak and Rivka are finally expecting. But the twins struggle inside her, and the pain is so great that Rivka cries out, אִם כֵּן לָמָּה זֶּה אָנֹכִי - “If so, why am I like this?” Why did I daven for a child (Rashi)? What is the point of what I’m going through (Ramban)? She goes to the nevi’im of her generation (Rashbam), and they tell her she is carrying twins.


How did that calm her down?


Rav Shalom Schwadron (Lev Shalom, Toldos) has a sharp answer. Chazal say that when Rivka passed a beis medrash, Yaakov struggled to get out, and when she passed a house of idol worship, Eisav did. Rivka was afraid she had one son pulled both ways, half toward the beis medrash and half toward the street. Hearing there were two sons, each drawn to his own side, she was relieved. One tzaddik and one rasha was better than one child on the fence.


But even after a person chooses good, both sides still live inside him: a yetzer tov and a yetzer hara. The battle between them lasts a lifetime.


Deep down we want the good: רְצוֹנֵנוּ לַעֲשׂוֹת רְצוֹנְךָ, our will is to do Your will. So it’s hard to accept that part of us wants something else. We want calm, not constant conflict, so it’s only human to push the problem to the side and hope it goes away.


That’s why, after a long clean stretch, we feel this is behind us. Many have found that this very feeling is the warning light. When we deny the other side is still there, we stop standing guard, and a surprise urge finds us unready.


You can’t beat something you pretend isn’t there. First we have to admit it exists, including the parts of ourselves we’d rather push down. Do it calmly, not in a panic that only scares and weakens us.


Keep at it, step by step, and give the yetzer tov the bechorah.

Two Corners, One Tefillah

By [byline to come]


Yitzchak and Rivka waited twenty years for children. The Torah tells us how they davened: וַיֶּעְתַּר יִצְחָק לַה’ לְנֹכַח אִשְׁתּוֹ - Yitzchak davened to Hashem opposite his wife. Rashi explains that he stood in one corner of the room and davened, and she stood in another corner and davened.


Each one had a separate corner and their own words. But they were facing each other, and they were davening for the same thing.


The Yaavetz (Tzitzim uFrachim) points out that יצחק and רבקה together add up to 515, the same as תפלה. The two of them, he says, laid the foundation of tefillah together, as the pasuk says, Yitzchak davened opposite his wife.


A husband and wife are two different people. They carry different worries, they say things differently, and sometimes they really are standing in different corners of the same room. Being close doesn’t mean erasing that. It means turning to face each other and wanting the same thing.


Twenty years of waiting could have pushed them apart, each one alone with the pain. Instead it turned them toward each other. When a hard stretch comes, with money, health, or waiting for something that isn’t coming, it’s easy for each of you to go off into your own corner and stop talking. Yitzchak and Rivka show another way. Tell her what you’re davening for, and ask what she’s davening for. Then daven for the same thing.


Our relationship with Hashem works the same way. Some days we feel like we’re standing in a far corner, with a lot of distance between us. Tefillah doesn’t ask us to close that distance first. It only asks us to turn and face Him, from whatever corner we’re in.
