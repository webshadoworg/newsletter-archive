Option A

Vayeitzei: The Fall Is the First Rung

In the draft now, written by Chayim · 595 words

Yaakov Avinu leaves home and lies down for the night, and the Torah says he took from the stones of the place and put them under his head. The pasuk then describes his dream of the ladder: “mutzav artzah,” set on the ground, “v’rosho magia hashamayma,” its top reaching heaven.

The Baal HaTurim reads a hint into this. The ladder is a picture of a person’s life: planted on the ground, but reaching all the way up to shamayim. And the lesson is that if a person is thrown down to the ground for a moment, that fall itself can become the means by which he later climbs even higher. The descent is not the opposite of the ascent. It is often the start of it. After a person goes down, he rises even higher than before.

Watch what happens with those stones. When Yaakov first lies down, the pasuk calls them avanim, plain stones, and he gathers them for a hard night’s sleep on the bare ground, in the middle of his trouble. But later, when the brothers go to make the covenant, the pasuk says “vayikchu avanim,” and Rebbe Aharon of Belz explained the shift: at first they were just stones, but once the struggle had passed, Yaakov told his children to gather them up as something precious. The very same stones that had been nothing but a hard place to rest his head in his lowest moment were now collected like diamonds.

That is the whole secret of a nisayon. Each struggle, once you’ve come through it, turns out to have been a gem. The hardship you would never have chosen becomes, in hindsight, one of the most valuable things you own. The people who don’t understand this see only stones, a hard, miserable stretch they want to forget. The people who do, look back and see diamonds.

There’s a story that says it perfectly. A great man was being honored, and his students, who didn’t realize he was a hidden tzaddik, asked him about his past. He told them: “In one of my travels I stayed with a certain man for a long time. He gave me nothing but dry bread and water each day, and had me sleep in his barn. I suffered terribly there. But because of that suffering, I reached very great spiritual heights. So I have only hakaras hatov for him.”

He didn’t say “I grew despite the suffering.” He said the suffering itself was the ladder. The dry bread and the cold barn were his stones, and years later he could see they had been diamonds all along.

For anyone in this battle, this is everything. The struggle you’re in, the one you’d give anything to be free of, is not just an obstacle in the way of your growth. Chazal teach that we always have “stones” in our lives, and they are there for our benefit. We don’t ask Hashem to remove every stone. We ask for the strength to be lifted up by them rather than crushed under them. The fight itself, the falling and the getting back up, the long hard stretch with no end in sight, is quietly forging a closeness to Hashem and a depth of character that a person who never struggled could never reach.

You may be in the part of the story where they still look like plain stones, hard, cold, something you just want to get through. Hold on. The day will come when you gather them up and see what they really were. Not stones. Gems.

Option B

The Ladder Is Driven Into the Ground

New, built on the parsha piece of the Hebrew Vayeitzei issue · 502 words · draft rev 5

Yaakov leaves his parents’ house, where he sat in the tent and learned, and goes out to Lavan. There he spends twenty years minding sheep.

Much of avodah has that shape. The learning is one thing, and minding the nefesh habehamis is a job that runs all day and does not keep yeshiva hours.

Rashi, at the start of Vayeishev, has Yaakov asking for quiet: ביקש יעקב לישב בשלוה, he wanted to settle in peace. Then קפץ עליו רוגזו של יוסף, the unrest of Yosef jumped on him. Yosef is the one who stands for this struggle, and the unrest came in place of the quiet.

On the road Hashem steadies him with a dream: סֻלָּם מֻצָּב אַרְצָה וְרֹאשׁוֹ מַגִּיעַ הַשָּׁמָיְמָה, a ladder standing on the ground with its top reaching heaven. In Onkelos it is נְעִיץ בְּאַרְעָא, driven down into the earth.

Malachim have no body and behaimos have no da’as. Only a person is both ends at once, dug into the dirt and still tied to the top, and Hashem shows Himself through exactly that combination.

It was in those years at Lavan, in the middle of the sheep, that Yaakov raised the shevatim. The Zohar reads the מקלות he set in the water troughs as the sodos of tefillin.

Tefillin tie us to Hashem in every part of ourselves: the thoughts in the head, the feelings in the heart, and the actions we carry out with our hands. The shel yad sits opposite the heart, and the retzuah winds around the hand.

They go on the left arm. The pasuk writes וְהָיָה לְאוֹת עַל יָדְכָה with an extra hei, which Chazal in Menachos read as יָד כֵּהָה, the dim hand, the weak one. The connection is tied on the weak side.

At a Chabad house in Belgium, a man began taking on mitzvos. He put on tefillin, and in time kept the rest. When they invited him for the Shabbos seudos he politely said he could not, because every weekend he traveled to Italy, and he never said why.

Eventually he opened up to the shliach, Rabbi Shabtai Slavaticki. He was involved with a non-Jewish girl and they had decided to marry, and for all his growth he could not break it off.

Rabbi Slavaticki sent him to the Lubavitcher Rebbe. He went in afraid, and the Rebbe said, “I envy you.” Then, seeing his face, he explained:

“A nisayon is a ladder, and a man can climb it or go down it. The bigger the nisayon, the taller the ladder. I was never given a ladder like yours.”

He climbed. He wrote to her that it was finished, went up to Eretz Yisroel, married a Jewish woman and built a home.

There may be one thing you would rather not raise in front of Hashem. Tomorrow morning, as you wind the retzuah down the weaker arm, say that thing out loud in one sentence. Not a resolution, just a refusal to leave it outside.

עֲלֵה לְמַעְלָה, עֲלֵה, climb.
