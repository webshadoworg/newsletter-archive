SUKKOS

An Arava? No, I’m an Esrog. And so are you!

By @RefaelEY


According to a famous midrash, the four species hint at four kinds of people in Klal Yisrael. Naturally, the first thing I ask myself is where I sit in that table. After so many years of falling, it is fairly reasonable that I should feel a little like an aravah, no? True, I learn Torah, and I do chessed, but falling in something this serious, and this many times, makes me feel a little like a rasha. A sort of dry, beaten aravah.


But wait. The midrash goes on to say that the four species also hint at four central limbs of the body, limbs they resemble in their outward shape. So if the esrog hints at my heart, let us look inside for a moment: what do I really want in the depths of my heart? What would I do if there were a magic button here next to me that I could press and be free of these exhausting battles from now until forever?


What would I do if at that moment I were at my very best? At the full strength of body and soul, not tired, not under a pull, at my clearest, in the middle of an uplifted Neilah. Do I press it or not?... Obviously I press it! So there it is. That is my heart. That is my inner self. What I am struggling with must not be the thing that defines my “I”, my identity.


The aravah, on the other hand, hints at the mouth. It expresses my outward actions. What actually happens in practice, min hasafah ulachutz, from the lip outward. True, my actions still need fixing. And only Hashem and I know (and a few of my friends from GYE) how hard I keep trying to strike at this taavah and to beat this aravah. Again and again and again...


But me? I am an esrog! And you too, you are an esrog!


A MEMBER’S NE’ILAH

I love you, right where you are

By @bright, on the GYE forum


This Yom Kippur, I went into it knowing it was going to be a rough ride. I was feeling very judged, very down, depressed, and angry at my situation. And I was right. It was a really rough ride. I couldn’t stay through the entire Maariv.


Until Ne’ilah. This wasn’t even my regular shul. I was davening there because the minyan I normally davened with only had Shacharis, so I needed somewhere else to go for the rest of Yom Kippur. Then the Rav got up to give his Ne’ilah drasha and told a story.


The story was about a man who had lost his son in battle, shortly after October 7th. The man davened in this Rav’s shul. After Yom Kippur, he came over to the Rav and said, “I hope Hashem forgives me.” The Rav asked him what he meant.


The man explained that the previous year, during Ne’ilah, he had been standing there davening with his son. Now his son was gone. And he couldn’t bring himself to say the words. He listened. He tried his best. But he just couldn’t open his mouth. And he was worried that Hashem wouldn’t forgive him for that.


The Rav told him that if any of their tefillos were answered, it would be in the merit of his silence. His silence was worth more than everyone else’s tefillos in that Beis Midrash.


And I felt like Hashem was talking to me. I had spent the entire day feeling like I was failing Hashem. I was angry at Him. I was barely able to daven. And suddenly I heard: I value you. Your trying is precious to Me.


Not just the person I could become. Not the person I was supposed to be. Me. Where I was sitting right then. Hashem knew where I was. He knew how difficult it was for me to be there. He knew what was going on inside my head. And somehow, I felt that none of that made me less valuable to Him. If anything, he valued me more.


I felt accepted. I felt that Hashem appreciated me for what I was doing, even though I was nowhere near where I thought I was supposed to be. And I felt loved.


The questions hadn’t gone away. I was still confused. The things that had brought me to that point were still painful, and they still are. But something had changed. For the first time that Yom Kippur, I wasn’t trying to figure out how to make myself acceptable to Hashem. I felt that I already was okay. And somehow, that turned my entire Ne’ilah around.


The rest of the drasha had nothing to do with what I was hearing. But that one story was enough. And I walked out feeling that Hashem Himself had told me, in the only way I could hear it that night:


You matter to Me. I value you. I accept you. And I love you, right where you are.


WISDOM FROM THE FORUM

I can go clean for months, but how do you go for YEARS?

@MECHAZAIK ASKED

Hey Chevra,

Maybe the oilam can help me here. Bh the journey has been going pretty good recently but how is it I see some guys are at 2, 3, even 4 years straight clean?! How do you guys do it?! I can go 3-4 months ok, but I feel the fall is always in my mind potentially around the corner.

What happens after 3-4 months is (and I’m still figuring myself out, so I’m not sure) I think I start to get complacent. I feel like I’m pretty good and OK, and I’m not so “addicted” or “hooked in,” so “it won’t be so bad” if I just fall once. I’ll tell myself I deserve it, since it’s been so long. Obviously that’s all the yetzer hara and shtusim.


But I get to this place where I don’t feel that initial “omg, I must stop and run away from this stuff” feeling. Before that happens, I’m trying to figure out how to get into a “never again, this is not who I am” mindset, instead of “almost never, but only once every few months.” It’s almost as if I don’t want to completely let go.


Have any thoughts how I can change this mindset from almost never to never?

JOYFUL TESHUVA

Our Past 'Pesoles' Becomes Our Schach

Joyful Teshuva was meant to end at Yom Kippur. Then we read your replies. One reader wrote:


This email was incredible. The last few have literally been life changing. I started printing them and reading them over Shabbos/Yom Tov. I was very down and discouraged during Elul/RH/YK and there were certain gems in here that literally started making me cry (because I was so relieved to have answers to certain questions). Please continue! Yasher koach! THANK YOU!


So here is one more.


Chaza“l say that the Schach should be from ”Pesoles Goren Ve’Yekev“. Why do Chazal use the Lashon of ”Pesoles“? Couldn’t they have found a nicer way to describe the material we use for this holy Mitzvah?


Says the Alter Sadigerer Rebbe (son-in-law of the Beis Ahron of Karlin), that Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur are times of Teshuvah Me’Yirah-Repentance from Fear, and Chazal say that when we repent from fear our sins become like “mistakes” (zedonos na’asin ki’shgagos). However, Sukkos is a time of Teshuvah Me’Ahavah-Repentance through love and rejoicing. This is a much higher level of Teshuvah, and Chazal say that the sins become like merits!


This, says the Sadigerer, is the secret of the Schach. We davka take the “Pesoles”, the spoiled, the sins of our past, and through Teshuvah Me’Ahavah on Sukkos, we uplift them into merits. Hashem’s divine presence hovers over us in the Sukkah davka in the form of our past sins, which we have merited to uplift through our repentance with love!


7 KEYS TO A STRONGER MARRIAGE · KEY #6

How do I deal with never ending arguments?

Adapted from the research of Dr. John Gottman


We talked about the problems you can solve. This is about the ones you can’t, and how to stop them from tearing a marriage apart.


Here’s one of Gottman’s most important discoveries: most marital conflicts, roughly two-thirds of them, are never fully “solved.” They’re perpetual. They sit on top of deep, lasting differences between two people, in temperament, in how they were raised, in what they value, and they’ll come up again and again for the length of a marriage. One spouse is a saver, the other a spender. One wants family always around, the other needs quiet. These aren’t problems with a solution; they’re differences between two whole people.


Gottman found that the couples who thrive aren’t the ones who somehow resolve these, they’re the ones who learn to talk about them without gridlock. And the couples in trouble are the ones who dig in, each trying to win, until the same fight hardens into resentment and they stop even being able to discuss it.


Stop Trying to Win


Gottman’s breakthrough move is this: underneath each person’s stubborn position, there is almost always a dream, a value, a history, a need, that matters deeply to them. The spender isn’t reckless; maybe he grew up with nothing and money means freedom to him. The saver isn’t cheap; maybe money means security she never had. When couples fight about the money, they’re really fighting about two dreams they’ve never spoken out loud. And the way out of gridlock isn’t to win the argument, it’s to get curious: why does this matter so much to you? what does it mean to you?


Honor the Dream, Even When You Disagree


Once you accept that the differences are permanent, the question changes from “how do I win this?” to “how do I honor what my spouse cares about, even though I see it differently?” And that’s the move that actually ends gridlock. When your spouse feels that you understand the dream underneath their position, and that you respect it, the conflict stops being a war, even if the difference itself never goes away. You don’t have to agree with the dream. You have to let it be real, and treat it as something worth protecting because it belongs to the person you love.


This is the everyday avodah of two people building one home out of two different worlds. The tension may remain. The gridlock lifts.


Your Action Steps This Week


  1. Name one perpetual conflict. Identify the disagreement that keeps coming back in your marriage, the one you’ve never actually solved. Just naming it as “perpetual, not solvable” takes pressure off both of you.
  2. Ask about the dream. Instead of restating your side, ask your spouse: “Help me understand why this matters so much to you. What does it mean to you?” Then just listen, no rebutting, no fixing.
  3. Honor it, even without agreeing. Find one small way to show your spouse you respect the dream behind their position, even if you still see it differently. Understanding, not victory, is what ends the gridlock.
