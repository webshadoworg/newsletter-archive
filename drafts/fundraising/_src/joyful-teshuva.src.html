Teshuva is the main Mitzvah of Yom Kippur. It's an incredible gift Hashem gives us to have a complete reset and turn a new page.

Over the past few weeks, our weekly newsletter has been running a series called Joyful Teshuva.

Here are 6 short ideas from it, each with its source, to help you have a powerful Teshuva this Yom Kippur.

1. Stopping comes first. The guilt can wait.
For a behavior that keeps repeating, Rabbeinu Yonah changes the order: "ראשית תשובת האיש הזה לעזוב דרכו," first leave the path, and only "אחרי כן יתחרט." If you are actively working on breaking free, trying, adjusting, fixing your plan, you are doing teshuva right now.
(Shaarei Teshuva 1:11)

2. The heaviness is not humility. It's the second attack.
Every night we ask: והסר שטן מלפנינו ומאחרינו. The satan in front pulls you toward the fall. The satan behind uses last night to cost you the whole week. On אם תיטיב שאת, R' Yeruchom says: get out of the mud, lift yourself up, keep going.
(Leket Reshimos p. 109)

3. Your wins weigh more than you think.
The Steipler zt"l writes that the seforim described the damage at length and were brief about the other side. A man who sometimes falls and sometimes wins, at the moment he overcomes a burning tayva, is "בבחינת יוסף הצדיק," and those wins are what repair the past.
(Kereina D'igrasa vol. 1, 105-106)

4. The decision alone changes where you stand.
The Pri Megadim states that the moment a person sincerely commits to leave his old ways, he is no longer a rasha in Hashem's eyes, even before he has fixed a thing. Similarly, Chazal say, “Open an opening like the eye of a needle, and Hashem opens one wide enough for wagons.”
(Pri Megadim, Pesicha Kolleles 3:10, on Kiddushin 49b; Midrash Shir Hashirim 5:3)

5. A fall doesn't cancel what came before it.
"I meant it last Yom Kippur, and then I fell. So was it ever real?" Yes, says the Mabit: teshuva that was sincere at the time is not erased by a later fall. Rav Shalom Schwadron zt"l compares it to digging in soft ground. Every shovelful, some dirt slides back in. But keep digging, and the hole still gets deeper. You are not back at square one.
(Bais Elokim, in Alei Shur vol. 1 pp. 45-6; Lekach Tov, Yamim Noraim vol. 2 pp. 108-9)

6. Even your sigh is written down.
Sometimes a person gives in, but almost always, it hurts him as he does it: "Oy, I wish I weren't doing this." Rav Itzele Peterburger zt"l says that a sigh is recorded in Shamayim alongside the aveira, and it affects how the act is judged. The Torah records even the embarrassment of a thief who carries off a sheep. How much more the pain of a Yid who never wanted to fall.
(Alei Shur vol. 2 p. 440, on Bava Kama 79b and Shemos 21:37)
