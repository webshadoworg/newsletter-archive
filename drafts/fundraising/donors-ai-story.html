The research sentence, checked against the right papers this time

First, a correction to what I sent you at 7:15. I told you the “review of 35 studies” was Li et al. in npj Digital Medicine, and that your durability line was unsupported. I had the wrong paper. Your research doc names the real one, and it says exactly what you wrote. The sentence I told you to cut is the sentence that is best sourced. The half I told you to keep is the half that overstates.

Here is each clause against the paper it actually comes from.

  • “A 2026 randomized trial found that a human coach produced broader gains in motivation and resilience than a chatbot.” Confirmed, unchanged.
    de Haan, Terblanche & Nowack, A randomised controlled comparison of the effectiveness of human and AI chatbot coaching with goal attainment, wellbeing and self-efficacy, Human Resource Development International, 2026, 29(4) 754-783. N = 114, accredited human coaches against automated AI coaches on goals, motivation, resilience and wellbeing. Its own words: “substantial effectiveness in diverse coaching outcomes but only for human coaching, with effect sizes in the mid-to-high range.”
    https://doi.org/10.1080/13678868.2026.2633990
  • “Some of the gains vanished once the human support stopped.” Confirmed, and almost word for word.
    Loughnane, Laiti, O’Donovan & Dunne, Systematic review exploring human, AI, and hybrid health coaching in digital health interventions, Frontiers in Digital Health, April 2025. This is your 35-study review: 35 studies, four of them hybrid. Verbatim: “While psychological wellbeing improved from baselines, this was not sustained after the human-coaching element of the intervention ended.” That is your sentence, sourced. Keep it.
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12058678/
  • “Found the best results when AI worked between sessions and a person stayed in the picture.” This is the clause to change.
    The same review does not report hybrid as the best performer. Its conclusion is “hybrid approaches need further refinement to harness AI’s scalability and the depth of human coaching”, and on the numbers hybrid retention was the lowest of the three models, 55 to 56.5%, against 80 to 100% for human coaching. Your own research doc says the same thing in its opening line: “there is not yet strong evidence from large randomized trials showing that hybrid coaching consistently outperforms both human-only and AI-only coaching.”
    What the review does support, and it is close to your point: “engagement and satisfaction were typically higher with human-delivered coaching”, adding a human component improved adherence to the whole intervention, and in the hybrid studies the AI handled personalised content, monitoring progress and adherence, and motivational nudges, while the human did the coaching. One study kept a coach in the loop to monitor, modify and validate the AI’s recommendations. So the division of labour is real and documented. The ranking is not.

Optional, if you want a second citation for the human half. Werntz et al., JMIR, Feb 2023, a meta-review of 31 meta-analyses covering 505 primary studies: “almost half (n=22, 48%) of them showed that human-supported DMHIs were significantly more effective than unsupported DMHIs” against 9% the other way, and the effect is strongest for the people with the most severe symptoms. That last part is the best line for us in any of these papers.
https://doi.org/10.2196/42864

My recommendation, one clause only. Keep the trial, keep the durability sentence, and replace the middle clause with what the review measured. Suggested wording:

The research is now saying the same thing. A 2026 randomized trial comparing accredited human coaches with AI coaches found real gains in motivation and resilience only in the human arm, and a review of 35 digital health studies found engagement and satisfaction consistently highest where a human coach was involved. When the human coaching ended, the gains did not hold.
