Dear Chaveirim,


Sometimes the journey seems overwhelming, we wonder if we really have the Koach within us and the game plan to break free for good. Sometimes it might not even seem possible.


But look at the order Avraham was given: his land, then his birthplace, then his father’s house. Easiest first, hardest last. Rav Leib Chasman, the Mashgiach of Chevron, writes in Ohr Yahel that this is the lesson. Take the one step you actually can, and that step is what brings down the siyata dishmaya for the next one. Plan the next step, and you move along, new doors will open up.

Your Lech Lecha

The parsha opens with Hashem telling Avraham, לֶךְ לְךָ מֵאַרְצְךָ וּמִמּוֹלַדְתְּךָ וּמִבֵּית אָבִיךָ, “Go for yourself from your land, from your birthplace and from your father’s house” (Bereishis 12:1). Avraham leaves Terach’s avodah zarah behind and sets out on a road nobody had walked before.


Interestingly, Yitzchak made a lech lecha of his own, even though his father’s house was the best there was. Avraham’s middah was chesed; Yitzchak’s was gevurah, its opposite. He didn’t reject anything Avraham taught him. He found his own way of serving the same Hashem.


Why did Yitzchak need his own derech? Rav Dessler answers that otherwise his avodah would have been “a mere repetition of his father’s avodah.” Hashem wanted each of the Avos to bring a new dimension of avodas Hashem into the world (Michtav MeEliyahu vol. 2 p. 164).


A tzaddik the son of a tzaddik, Rav Dessler explains, faces a danger of his own: what he was handed can remain a habit. His maalah comes when he works to turn it into an inner kinyan, “like a man who discovers it all afresh” (ibid. vol. 2 p. 205, vol. 3 p. 124).


Most of us at GYE are Yidden who keep Torah and mitzvos. We grew up in good homes, and we are still here. Why does Hashem make it so easy for frum Yidden to fall into this struggle?


Perhaps this is why. The struggle forces us to go deeper than anything we were given. Breaking free from lust requires us to deepen our connection to Hashem, His Torah and His mitzvos beyond what we grew up with, and to make it real in a way it never was before.


This is our lech lecha. Not a journey away from our father’s house, chas v’shalom, but beyond it: a new dimension of avodas Hashem that only we can bring into the world.


FROM A MEMBER · DAY 29

“This Didn’t Work Out Too Well”

by @shevayipolvkam11, on the forum


Starting to detail my journey. Hoping this will be of benefit to others, and a way for me to get chizzuk from myself and others.


To make a very long story short: I have been struggling with inappropriate content for over 25 years. I was first exposed when I was around 11 and have been battling since. I’ve had various successes over the years, but every few months or so, I inevitably stumbled.


I don’t think I ever really believed that I had it within myself to stop. For so many years, I thought the solution was simply making sure I didn’t have access, and that as long as I had the best filters, I would be fine.


This didn’t work out too well.


I tried every filter out there, and while I was fully on board with filtering everything, I never really tried to stop testing the filters. I’m pretty computer savvy, and I was eventually able to skirt most filters in some form, only to then go out and get a different filter or multiple filters at the same time. I would go months without access, but I also spent a lot of that time trying to get access.


Even when I couldn’t get access through filters to images, etc., it wasn’t enough. I discovered all kinds of ways to get stimulating content. I always felt like I was chasing something to numb myself when I was feeling overwhelmed, down, or triggered.


This all changed recently. There was a glitch in my filter, I got some access, and I was discovered by my wife. I never came so close to losing everything. It is still an extremely excruciating process of rebuilding trust and connection with her.


But I’m done with my old ways of just filtering. I am doing something different.


I am being radically open with her (as incredibly painful as that has been). I have a mentor I check in with every day before I go to sleep. I am admitting to myself that I CANNOT DO THIS ALONE.


This has been the most painful and strangely relieving realization over the past few weeks.


I’ve made a list of boundaries, green circles, red circles, a plan, etc. I’m fully invested and hope to post my progress here, mostly for myself.


It’s been 29 days so far, and I’m proud to say I haven’t crossed a single boundary. No filter poking. No [acting out]. No using my computer to distract or numb myself in any form.


I’m feeling my strength build day by day. B”H.


Thank you for reading.

MARRIAGE

My Sister or My Wife?

Based on an essay by Rabbi YY Jacobson


You come home, say hello, and she barely looks up. Dinner is quiet. You scroll, she folds laundry, and the thought creeps in: when did we stop being us? Nothing dramatic happened. The feeling just left, and you’re not sure you have the energy to get it back.


Most men hit this stretch, and most draw the wrong conclusion from it. This week’s parsha offers a different one.


On the way down to Mitzrayim, Avraham asks Sarah to say she is his sister. The Chassidic masters read this as something every married couple eventually needs to know.


There are two kinds of love in a family. The love between siblings is innate. You didn’t choose your brother and can’t un-choose him; whether you feel close this year or not, he remains your brother. It’s steady, unshakable, and rarely exciting. The love between husband and wife is the opposite. Two different people came together and created it, and that difference is what makes it passionate. It’s also what makes it fragile. Passion rises and passion fades.


Mitzrayim comes from meitzarim, constraints. Every marriage has its “Mitzrayim”: the feelings go quiet, the walls go up, and you look at your wife and don’t feel much. Avraham’s instruction is for exactly that moment. When you can’t be husband and wife, become brother and sister. Fall back on the bond that doesn’t depend on mood. The Zohar calls husband and wife two halves of one neshama. The connection isn’t only the one you built under the chuppah; it’s as innate as blood. You are linked whether you feel it or not.


So in the dry season, stay loyal the way healthy siblings do. Show up. Keep her confidence. Don’t let trust erode just because the romance has. And here is the quiet promise in the story: a man who stays present for his wife when he isn’t in the mood finds the warmth comes back, often stronger. The sibling bond isn’t the ceiling of the marriage. It’s the floor that lets you climb back up.


The same secret works with Hashem. Shir Hashirim calls the Jew “my sister, my beloved.” In your “Eretz Yisroel,” davening with feeling, you’re the spouse. Then comes the inner “Mitzrayim”: numbed by taavah, caught in old patterns or an addiction, and the relationship feels dead. On that day, fall back on the sibling bond. You are not holy because you feel holy. You are holy because that’s what you essentially are, and nothing you did last night changes it. Choosing right when you feel nothing isn’t performing. It’s loyalty to who you really are.


Takeaway: When you’re not feeling it with your wife, don’t ask whether you’re in love. Ask whether you’re being a loyal brother. Do one thing today that a good brother would do for his sister, and expect nothing back. That loyalty is what carries a marriage out of “Mitzrayim.”
