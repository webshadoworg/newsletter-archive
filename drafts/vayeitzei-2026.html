The Fall Is the First Rung

Yaakov Avinu leaves home and lies down for the night, and the Torah says he took from the stones of the place and put them under his head. The pasuk then describes his dream of the ladder: “mutzav artzah,” set on the ground, “v’rosho magia hashamayma,” its top reaching heaven.


The Baal HaTurim reads a hint into this. The ladder is a picture of a person’s life: planted on the ground, but reaching all the way up to shamayim. And the lesson is that if a person is thrown down to the ground for a moment, that fall itself can become the means by which he later climbs even higher. The descent is not the opposite of the ascent. It is often the start of it. After a person goes down, he rises even higher than before.


Watch what happens with those stones. When Yaakov first lies down, the pasuk calls them avanim, plain stones, and he gathers them for a hard night’s sleep on the bare ground, in the middle of his trouble. Rashi says the stones began quarreling with one another, each one wanting the tzaddik to rest his head on it, and Hashem made them into a single stone. By morning the pasuk no longer says stones at all. It says he took the stone he had put under his head, stood it up as a matzeivah, and poured oil over it. The very same stone that had been nothing but a hard place to rest his head in his lowest moment was now standing as a monument, anointed with oil.


That is the whole secret of a nisayon. Each struggle, once you’ve come through it, turns out to have been a gem. The hardship you would never have chosen becomes, in hindsight, one of the most valuable things you own. The people who don’t understand this see only stones, a hard, miserable stretch they want to forget. The people who do, look back and see diamonds.


There’s a story that says it perfectly. A great man was being honored, and his students, who didn’t realize he was a hidden tzaddik, asked him about his past. He told them: “In one of my travels I stayed with a certain man for a long time. He gave me nothing but dry bread and water each day, and had me sleep in his barn. I suffered terribly there. But because of that suffering, I reached very great spiritual heights. So I have only hakaras hatov for him.”


He didn’t say “I grew despite the suffering.” He said the suffering itself was the ladder. The dry bread and the cold barn were his stones, and years later he could see they had been diamonds all along.


For anyone in this battle, this is everything. The struggle you’re in, the one you’d give anything to be free of, is not just an obstacle in the way of your growth. Chazal teach that we always have “stones” in our lives, and they are there for our benefit. We don’t ask Hashem to remove every stone. We ask for the strength to be lifted up by them rather than crushed under them. The fight itself, the falling and the getting back up, the long hard stretch with no end in sight, is quietly forging a closeness to Hashem and a depth of character that a person who never struggled could never reach.


You may be in the part of the story where they still look like plain stones, hard, cold, something you just want to get through. Hold on. The day will come when you gather them up and see what they really were. Not stones. Gems.


FROM A MEMBER

“I was pulled so hard in both directions”

Based on a forum post by @abieham


Hi everyone. Baruch Hashem, I’ve been clean for over 800 days, but this past Motzei Shabbos I almost fell.


I wasn’t even feeling pulled toward it. I had just upgraded one of my devices, and late that night I took it into the bathroom and sat there poking around on it. I told myself it was nothing. Within a few minutes I was coming across things I shouldn’t have been looking at. One thing led to the next, and I ended up on a page with content I hadn’t seen in over 800 days.


The moment I found it I moved off the page. I didn’t want to break my streak. But a minute or so later I found myself back on it, standing there deciding what to do.


It was late. My wife was sleeping. I was alone. And it was right there in front of me. I was pulled so hard in both directions. One side of me said, why not, you haven’t seen it in so long, you can look for a minute and close it. The other side knew exactly how it would make me feel afterward, the way it always used to. Dirty and depressed. Having a cold shoulder to my amazing wife. Feeling guilty.


But the pull was so strong. My heart was pounding. And the voice that was loudest said, who cares, these feelings aren’t going away, just do it.


So I gave in partway. I looked a moment longer. But then I hit something so ugly that I couldn’t bring myself to keep looking. I blocked the screen, got off the page, and jumped into the shower, which I left running for almost an hour. It was around 1 a.m. by the time I got to bed.


I wasn’t sure whether to count this as a fall for my GYE count. But because I ended up stopping and didn’t go all the way, and because having over 800 days never goes away, and I don’t want to start spiraling downward, I’m keeping the count.


Baruch Hashem, I’ve now strengthened the filter and made a new geder: no iPad in the bathroom. I hope to stay strong, and im”h to be clean and celebrate 1,000 days soon.


MARRIAGE

The Part of Her You Don’t Understand

Based on an essay by Rabbi YY Jacobson


The Torah tells the story of how Yaakov came to marry both Rachel and Leah. He worked seven years for Rachel, the wife he chose and loved, but on the wedding night Lavan switched Leah in her place, and only in the morning did Yaakov discover it. He stayed married to Leah, but the Torah says something startling about her: “And Hashem saw that Leah was senuah,” that Leah was hated.


It’s a hard word. Yaakov Avinu, the third of the Avos, the father of all of Klal Yisrael, “hated” his own wife? And if that were really so, why stay married at all?


The Alter Rebbe gives a breathtaking answer, and it unlocks something about every marriage.


In the Zohar, Rachel and Leah represent two worlds: the “revealed world” and the “hidden world.” Rachel is the part of a person you can see, grasp, and make sense of. Leah is the part that runs deeper than words, the hidden layers you can’t quite wrap your mind around. And the Alter Rebbe makes a piercing observation about human nature: people hate what they don’t understand. We love what fits neatly into our box, what we “get,” what feels in our control. But when we meet something in another person that we can’t make sense of, something that unsettles us and defies our expectations, our instinct is to recoil from it, to push it away, even to resent it. The resentment is really just fear of what we can’t control.


That, says the Alter Rebbe, is the deeper meaning of Leah being “hated.” It isn’t that Yaakov despised his wife, chas v’shalom. It’s that Leah represented the dimension of the relationship that challenged him, the parts that didn’t fit his plan, that he never would have chosen, that forced him to grow in ways he hadn’t signed up for. Notice the Torah’s language: Yaakov chose Rachel. He never chose Leah; she came to him “by mistake,” unexpectedly, unasked for. And that’s exactly the point. The parts of a marriage that stretch us most are never the parts we picked. They arrive on their own, and they’re the ones that grow us the most.


Here is what every husband needs to hear in this. Your wife, like every person, has her “Rachel” and her “Leah.” There are things about her you understand and love easily, and there are things that genuinely challenge you, that irk you, that you can’t quite figure out. The temptation is to treat that second category as the problem, the part to resent or wish away. The Alter Rebbe is teaching the opposite. Those very things, the ones that unsettle you, that force you to question yourself, often hold the deepest growth and healing a marriage has to offer. Look again at the pasuk: “Hashem saw that Leah was hated, so He opened her womb.” It was specifically Leah, the hated one, the challenging one, who became the mother of most of the shevatim. The dimension Yaakov resisted was the one that gave birth to the future of Klal Yisrael.


If you surround yourself only with what feels comfortable and in your control, you stay, in the Torah’s word, barren. It’s by opening yourself to the parts of your spouse you don’t understand, instead of fleeing them, that a marriage gives birth to something far greater than either of you could have planned.


And the Alter Rebbe adds one last line worth carrying for a lifetime: the path to Rachel always runs through Leah. You can never fully love the parts of your wife you understand until you’ve made peace with the parts you don’t.
