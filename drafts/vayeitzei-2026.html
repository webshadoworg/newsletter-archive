What Yaakov Knew About Facing Challenges

Yaakov Avinu runs for his life. His own brother wants to kill him, and he leaves his parents, his familiar surroundings and everything he knows, not knowing when he will be able to return. And things didn’t exactly get easier from there.


He went to stay with his uncle Lavan, expecting it to be for a short time. Instead, he ended up spending twenty years there. Lavan deceived him repeatedly, changed his wages ten times, and made even the simplest things in life difficult. He was surrounded by people whose values were completely different from his own.


Just imagine the stress, disappointment, and uncertainty Yaakov had to deal with. So many things were outside his control. It would have been easy to become bitter, discouraged, or influenced by the people around him.


Yet when he finally left Lavan, Yaakov was able to tell Esav, Im Lavan garti, I lived with Lavan. As Rashi explains, the word garti has the numerical value of 613. Yaakov was saying that despite living with Lavan, he had kept the 613 mitzvos and had not adopted Lavan’s ways.


How did he manage to stay so strong through all those years?


Perhaps we can find a hint at the very beginning of the parsha. When Yaakov stopped for the night on his way to Charan, he placed stones around his head to protect himself from wild animals.


But why did Yaakov place the stones only around his head? Why didn’t he protect the rest of his body?


Perhaps there’s a deeper message here. We can’t always control what happens to us or what’s going on around us. But we can learn to control what’s going on inside our heads, how we think about things, what we focus on, and how we respond to challenges.


And perhaps that was one of the secrets to how Yaakov managed to stay strong through everything he went through. He couldn’t control Lavan’s behavior or make all his problems disappear. But he could choose how to respond, what to focus on, and what kind of attitude to maintain.


This is something we can all relate to. We don’t always get to choose how our day goes. We face stressful situations, disappointments, difficult people, and sometimes things we’re exposed to that we’d rather avoid.


Of course, we should do our best to create a healthy environment and avoid unnecessary challenges. Filters, boundaries, and other practical precautions are important. But no matter how careful we are, we can’t eliminate every difficulty or control everything around us.


What we can do is learn how to handle those situations better.


Think about how easily a frustrating experience can affect the rest of our day. Something goes wrong at work, someone says something upsetting, or we find ourselves in a situation we didn’t choose. We can spend the next few hours dwelling on what happened, getting more frustrated, and feeling helpless. Or we can recognize what’s outside our control and focus on what we can actually do next.


The same applies when dealing with urges. We may not be able to prevent every trigger or unwanted thought, but we can learn how to respond without getting carried away by it.


There are practical skills for managing stress, handling uncomfortable feelings, changing unhelpful thought patterns, and riding out urges until they pass. These skills take practice, but they can make an enormous difference.


The shift is inward. Not waiting for the things around you to get easier so that you can finally succeed, but asking what you can change about your mindset, your attitudes, your skills, the way you cope with uncomfortable feelings and urges, even if nothing around you changes at all.


And you don’t have to work it out alone. Post the question on the GYE forum and you’ll get back a lot of good ideas, from people who have been exactly there.


Yaakov’s life is a powerful reminder that our circumstances don’t have to determine who we become. Even when things around us are far from ideal, we can work on what happens inside our own minds.


We can’t always choose what happens to us. But we can learn to choose how we respond.


FROM A MEMBER

“I was pulled so hard in both directions”

Based on a forum post by @abieham


Hi everyone. Baruch Hashem, I’ve been clean for over 800 days, but this past Motzei Shabbos I almost fell.


I wasn’t even feeling pulled toward it. I had just upgraded one of my devices, and late that night I took it into the bathroom and sat there poking around on it. I told myself it was nothing. Within a few minutes I was coming across things I shouldn’t have been looking at. One thing led to the next, and I ended up on a page with content I hadn’t seen in over 800 days.


The moment I found it I moved off the page. I didn’t want to break my streak. But a minute or so later I found myself back on it, standing there deciding what to do.


It was late. My wife was sleeping. I was alone. And it was right there in front of me. I was pulled so hard in both directions. One side of me said, why not, you haven’t seen it in so long, you can look for a minute and close it. The other side knew exactly how it would make me feel afterward, the way it always used to. Dirty and depressed. Having a cold shoulder to my amazing wife. Feeling guilty.


But the pull was so strong. My heart was pounding. And the voice that was loudest said, who cares, these feelings aren’t going away, just do it.


So I gave in partway. I looked a moment longer. But then I hit something so ugly that I couldn’t bring myself to keep looking. I blocked the screen, got off the page, and jumped into the shower, which I left running for almost an hour. It was around 1 a.m. by the time I got to bed.


I wasn’t sure whether to count this as a fall for my GYE count. But because I ended up stopping and didn’t go all the way, and because having over 800 days never goes away, and I don’t want to start spiraling downward, I’m keeping the count.


Baruch Hashem, I’ve now strengthened the filter and made a new geder: no iPad in the bathroom. I hope to stay strong, and im”h to be clean and celebrate 1,000 days soon.

The Part of Her You Don’t Understand

Based on an essay by Rabbi YY Jacobson


The Torah tells the story of how Yaakov came to marry both Rachel and Leah. He worked seven years for Rachel, the wife he chose and loved, but on the wedding night Lavan switched Leah in her place, and only in the morning did Yaakov discover it. He stayed married to Leah, but the Torah says something startling about her: “And Hashem saw that Leah was senuah,” that Leah was hated.


It’s a hard word. Yaakov Avinu, the third of the Avos, the father of all of Klal Yisrael, “hated” his own wife? And if that were really so, why stay married at all?


The Baal HaTanya gives a breathtaking answer, and it unlocks something about every marriage.


In the Zohar, Rachel and Leah represent two worlds: the “revealed world” and the “hidden world.” Rachel is the part of a person you can see, grasp, and make sense of. Leah is the part that runs deeper than words, the hidden layers you can’t quite wrap your mind around. And there is a piercing observation about human nature here: people hate what they don’t understand. We love what fits neatly into our box, what we “get,” what feels in our control. But when we meet something in another person that we can’t make sense of, something that unsettles us and defies our expectations, our instinct is to recoil from it, to push it away, even to resent it. The resentment is really just fear of what we can’t control.


That is the deeper meaning of Leah being “hated.” It isn’t that Yaakov despised his wife, chas v’shalom. It’s that Leah represented the dimension of the relationship that challenged him, the parts that didn’t fit his plan, that he never would have chosen, that forced him to grow in ways he hadn’t signed up for. Notice the Torah’s language: Yaakov chose Rachel. He never chose Leah; she came to him “by mistake,” unexpectedly, unasked for. And that’s exactly the point. The parts of a marriage that stretch us most are never the parts we picked. They arrive on their own, and they’re the ones that grow us the most.


Here is what every husband needs to hear in this. Your wife, like every person, has her “Rachel” and her “Leah.” There are things about her you understand and love easily, and there are things that genuinely challenge you, that irk you, that you can’t quite figure out. The temptation is to treat that second category as the problem, the part to resent or wish away. The opposite is true. Those very things, the ones that unsettle you, that force you to question yourself, often hold the deepest growth and healing a marriage has to offer. Look again at the pasuk: “Hashem saw that Leah was hated, so He opened her womb.” It was specifically Leah, the hated one, the challenging one, who became the mother of most of the shevatim. The dimension Yaakov resisted was the one that gave birth to the future of Klal Yisrael.


If you surround yourself only with what feels comfortable and in your control, you stay, in the Torah’s word, barren. It’s by opening yourself to the parts of your spouse you don’t understand, instead of fleeing them, that a marriage gives birth to something far greater than either of you could have planned.


And one last line is worth carrying for a lifetime: the path to Rachel always runs through Leah. You can never fully love the parts of your wife you understand until you’ve made peace with the parts you don’t.
