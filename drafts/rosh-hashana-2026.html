ROSH HASHANA

Rosh Hashana: The Day You Can Walk Out of Prison

The Mishna (Rosh Hashana 27b) says that someone who passes behind a shul, or whose house is next to a shul, and hears the shofar, has fulfilled the mitzvah, but only if he intends to. Two people can hear the identical sound: one is yotzei, the other isn’t. The difference is kavanah, intent of the heart.


The Tolner Rebbe asks two questions. First, why does the Mishna give two cases, the man passing by and the man whose house is next door? The principle is identical; one should have been enough. Second, the Gemara explains that “intent” here means the listener has to know he’s hearing a shofar and not, say, a donkey braying. But which Jew stands outside a shul on Rosh Hashana, hears Tekiah-Teruah-Tekiah, and thinks he’s listening to a donkey?


To answer, the Tolner Rebbe brings the Gemara (Rosh Hashana 11a) that Yosef left prison on Rosh Hashana. Why does that matter to us, is it just a historical detail?


Picture what a prison was in those days. Not a modern cell, however harsh, but a pit dug in the ground, no ventilation, no sanitation, minimal food and water, thrown in among the dregs of society, and Yosef abused there as a Jew accused of a crime. And then, in a flash, they pull him out, cut his hair, change his clothes, and stand him before the most powerful man in the world for his advice. He offers his plan, and within days he goes from the pit to being the second most powerful person on earth. Overnight.


That is the message. A person can go from the lowest of the low to the highest heights almost instantly. As Shlomo HaMelech says, “from prison he went out to rule” (Koheles 4:14). And people can feel imprisoned in all sorts of ways, by their troubles, their tormentors, above all by their lusts and their yetzer hara, walking around as if with a ball and chain. The lesson of Yosef leaving prison on Rosh Hashana is that this is the day a person can walk out of that prison in one stroke.


Now the two questions answer themselves. The second case, “his house is next to the shul,” raises an obvious problem: if he lives right next door, why isn’t he inside on Rosh Hashana? Because the Mishna is describing someone so far removed that when he hears the shofar, he genuinely wonders whether it’s just a donkey. What kind of Jew is that? One who has drifted that far from the day. And yet, the Mishna teaches, if in one minute he catches himself and says, “Wait, that’s a shofar!”, that single flash of recognition can lift him out of the dungeon. In that one minute he becomes transformed - מאשפות ירים אביון.


So if you feel imprisoned in some sense, remember that Rosh Hashana is the day Yosef went from prisoner to ruler in a single moment, and it’s the day each of us can do the same. It just takes one right move.


WISDOM FROM THE FORUM

“Why Do I Crash
Right After Rosh Hashana?”

Every year this question turns up on the forum a few days after Yom Tov, when it is too late to do anything about it. So we are running it a week early instead.


@poiuytrewq asked:


I just came off of a beautiful Rosh Hashanah. I felt like I was on a spiritual high and then as soon as Rosh Hashanah ended I cracked. Does anyone have any insight?


@HashemHelpMe responded:


This is a very common occurrence. Many of us here do not have the skill of regulating our emotions in a healthy manner. We trained ourselves to take all that built up high/euphoria/dveikus and release it all by masturbating. We have to give ourselves other tools. Secondly, when a yom tov leaves, we find people are generally more anxious (see Aruch Hashulchan who says on Motzai Shabbos, the body senses the loss of the neshomo yeseira and becomes uneasy), and spiritually vulnerable. Prepare yourself, say a tfilah to Hashem and iyh you will be fine.


@grateful4life answered from a 12-step perspective, with a good הוראת שעה if your struggle is especially intense:


Your experience is very common among serious strugglers, and it was especially challenging for me too... better yet it was a demoralizing and hopeless phenomenon for me... feeling that I was so high and so close to Hashem and then BOOM - only to spiral completely out of control as if Elul and Tishrei occurred 10 months ago, not 10 days ago or 10 hours (minutes) ago. This happened to me year after year.

Now being part of a mostly frum live meeting in SA, I’ve noticed how prevalent it is that people relapse after Yomim Tovim.

So today I avoid getting too high during the Yomim Noraim because I just don’t have a good parachute to land safely back down. Your problem is that you are like the cartoons with people attached to a propelled rocket that runs out of fuel mid-air and suddenly everyone is crashing down from the sky. In cartoons they usually end up ok but in real life it hurts! And what do we do when we feel we’re missing something or something hurts us - we turn to our drug to numb out the pain, hence the vicious cycle continues...

In a 12 step program we are seriously doing teshuva and growing spiritually on a daily basis so Elul/Tishrei is no different but regardless try to treat these days as “regular” days and don’t get too high on the avodas hayom. Hashem wants you to stay clean above anything else so try this method and see how it goes. Maybe in a few years down the road you’ll get back to that intensity when you can handle it but for now just keep it even keeled.


Those are two answers out of many, and the same question is being asked on the forum right now.


This week, on GYE’s new forum, a member wrote that intense stress had set off the old pattern, that the Yomim Noraim were piling guilt and shame on top of it, and that he was closer to acting out than he had ever been. He was, he wrote, “typing in furiously here at midnight trying to grab a lifeline”. Members answered him, and one of them asked whether he had a plan. He came back with this:


Step One is to truly believe that Hashem Loves to forgive... like holyari said כל הגדול! Now for the Plan:

1. Understand that Yamin Noraim is about Micro Moments and Micro Victories NO ONE has TRUE AND REAL, hours long spiritual highs, its not applicable to almost anyone today thats not the goal

2. chart your good moments - your victories - your aha moments at the end of every day of these days You wont believe how impressive it will look once Simchas Torah comes around

3. Remember אין הקבה בא בטרוניא עם בריותיו youre not going to get extra whacked because you slipped up during this time of year - Relax!

4. know that there is a tremendous battery pack of fresh spiritual powers that come down to every yid this time of year and youre about to get a reload! all you need to do is to show up and tap in!!

5. YOU GOT THIS!!


That thread is open on the new forum, and someone reading it four days before Rosh Hashana needs your answer in it: Join the discussion.

JOYFUL TESHUVA · PART 5

Get Out of the Mud

You know the hour after a fall better than you know the fall itself. You can get up and do what the day needs. You do not move. You sit there going back over what just happened, or what happened yesterday, filled with a gloomy kind of sadness. Why did that happen. Why did I do that again. What does this say about me. What is going to be.


And it does not stop when the hour does. Something is left inside you and it stays. You do not have the strength to get back up. You are upset that you are still struggling with this at all. You wonder whether you are ever going to break free. You are frustrated that it is taking so long to get anywhere. And while all of that is running in the background, it is very hard to function at your best and do what you have to do.


Earlier parts of this series dealt with the past itself: that going back over it waits until you are sure you can do it without getting triggered, and that even then it belongs to Yom Kippur or another set occasion. The teshuva for right now is the work of breaking free - stopping, planning how to stop, and positive action, above all Torah.


This week is not about the past. It is about that feeling that is left behind afterwards - what it actually is, and what it is coming to tell you about moving on.


1. The satan in front and the satan behind


Every night we ask for something oddly specific: והסר שטן מלפנינו ומאחרינו, remove the satan from before us and from behind us. The Dubno Maggid explains the two directions with a mashal about two men, each walking with an escort.


יש הולך עם שומר והשומר הוא לכבודו, ויש הולך עם שומר והשומר הוא לרעתו... המשרת המוליך את האורח אל בית העשיר, משפט המשרת ללכת תחלה עם הפנס להאיר לפניו למען הכין הדרך לפני האורח שלא יכשל, ובהיפך אותו אשר מוליך את אחד להסגירו בבית אסורים, הוא מוליך את האיש הזה לפניו והוא ילך אחריו להביט בו ולשמרו שלא יברח.


There is a man who walks with an escort and the escort is for his honor, and there is a man who walks with an escort and the escort is to his harm. The servant bringing an invited guest to the rich man’s house walks ahead of him with the lantern, to light the way and prepare the road so the guest does not stumble. The opposite is the officer marching a man off to prison: he puts the man in front of him and walks behind him, watching him, guarding him so he does not run.


כן ענין השטן כי קודם שחטא האדם, השטן הולך לפניו להראות לו שילך למקום פלוני ושם ישיג תאוה זאת, ולמקום פלוני ושם ישיג תאוה אחרת, אמנם אחר המעשה אחר שכבר חטא, אז ילך אחורי האדם עם חרבו... וזהו והסר שטן מלפנינו שלא יביאנו לחטא, ומאחרינו שלא יביאנו אל העונש.


So it is with the satan. Before a man has sinned, the satan walks in front of him, showing him to go to this place and there he will get this taava, and to that place and there he will get another one. But after the act, once he has already sinned, then he walks behind the man with his sword. And this is what we ask: remove the satan from before us, that he not bring us to sin, and from behind us, that he not bring us to punishment.


Ohel Yaakov, the Dubno Maggid, on Bechukosai. The mashal is the second of two explanations he gives for the phrase.



2. Get out of the mud


R’ Yeruchom Levovitz, as recorded by the Lakewood Mashgiach R’ Nosson Meir Wachtfogel, on Hashem’s words to Kayin, אם תיטיב שאת:


אך יש חולשה גדולה לבני אדם להשאר בתוך הבוץ, וזה סכנה עצומה, וזו אזהרת ה’ לקין ”אם תיטיב”, אם תרצה לצאת מהבוץ של חטא, ”שאת” תתרומם ותתקדם הלאה, אל תחשוב על העבר, תשכח ממנו ותצא מהבוץ ותמשיך הלאה, ואם לאו, הסכנה עצומה ”לפתח חטאת רובץ” ח”ו.


But there is a great weakness in people, to stay inside the mud, and that is an enormous danger. And that is Hashem’s warning to Kayin: אם תיטיב, if you want to get out of the mud of sin, שאת - lift yourself up and move forward. Do not think about the past, forget it, get out of the mud and keep going. And if not, the danger is enormous, לפתח חטאת רובץ.


R’ Yeruchom Levovitz zt”l, quoted by Rav Nosson Meir Wachtfogel zt”l, Leket Reshimos, p. 109.



3. What went wrong, not what is wrong with me


The heaviness feels like it is doing something. It is not. Doom and gloom after a fall does not leave you with more motivation to get back up, it leaves you with less. It is not the price of taking this seriously; it is the thing that stops you from acting on it.


So ask the other question. What went wrong. Which mistake led to this fall. How could I revise my plan so that it is less likely to happen again. Those have answers, and the answers are things you can do something about this week, which is the essence of teshuva.


That is not the same as sitting in the mud. A question pointed at your plan moves you forward; a question pointed at yourself does not. What is wrong with me never has a good answer, and there is nothing at the end of it but sadness and staying exactly where you are.




This Rosh Hashana, don’t focus on what you did wrong (leave that for Yom Kippur), focus on the positive, on your readiness for kabbalas ol malchus shamayim for the coming year. And that comes with Simcha, as the Yerushalmi says (Rosh Hashana 1:3), that most people get anxious from Days of Judgment, but Yidden are not like that, we get dressed in white and celebrate, confident that Hashem will bless us with a miraculous year.


אבל ישראל אינו כן, אלא לובשים לבנים ומתעטפין לבנים ... ואוכלין ושותין ושמחים, יודעין שהקדוש ברוך הוא עושה להן ניסים (ירושלמי ראש השנה פ”א ה”ג).

A ROSH HASHANA ESSAY

A Freilechen Yom HaDin

Based on an essay by Rav Shalom Arush shlita


Rosh Hashana is the Yom HaDin, the Day of Judgment. There is a deep seriousness to the day, which is why we spend the month of Elul preparing for it with Selichos and teshuva.


But Hashem’s judgment is not a trial meant to punish you.


The awe of Rosh Hashana is not fear or panic. It is the reverence of standing before the King.


Even if you are still struggling.


Because the moment you truly want to change, something has already changed. Even if you have not succeeded yet. Even if it takes years.


If you truly want to move in the right direction, then you’ve already begun.


Everyone here wants to break free. And if you at least want to break free, then that already counts, and you can come into Rosh Hashana with joy.


We are happy on Rosh Hashana, very happy. Not frivolous, not distracted by idle talk, but happy and confident that we are standing before a King Who wants us close.


The Baal HaTanya gives the famous parable of the King in the field.


Normally, the King is in His palace. Getting close to Him is difficult. Who can simply walk in and get an audience with the King?


But in Elul, the King comes out to the field.


He comes to us.


Moshe Rabbeinu went up to Heaven on Rosh Chodesh Elul and came down forty days later, on Yom Kippur. Those forty days became a special time of teshuva, forgiveness, and atonement.


And during those days, the King does not wait for us in the palace.


He comes down into the field, even into the lowest places, so that every person can approach Him.


Even if you feel far away.


Even if you have fallen very low.


The King comes close so that you can come close.


And then comes Rosh Hashana.


Rosh Hashana is the day we proclaim Hashem’s kingship. The day we crown Him as King.


And this King wants every one of His people close to Him.


That is why Elul is a time of such joy. The King has come out of His palace for us.


And that is how to think about the judgment of Rosh Hashana:


The King is judging me because He wants good for me. He wants to give me what I need, and arrange my life in the way that will help me come closer to Him.


That should not lead to anxiety.


It should lead to hope, closeness, and joy.


MEMBER STORY

I Already Failed, So Why Stop Now?

By @curedsoon


I’m a bachur who, b’ezras Hashem, is getting married in about two months.


My main nisayon, besides the other two poisons, is AI.


I was on Day 52 when I had a very rough day. First seder was rough, second seder was rough, and I was also dealing with stress about my future shver. I came home that night to unwind and eat supper. Then I remembered that I needed to prepare a Waze device. I have a Gentech filter that can turn a smartphone into a Waze-only device, so I took out two old phones to see if either one could handle it.


The first phone was way too slow. My annoyance with life started building up even more. The second phone looked promising, but it turned out to be too old to run Waze.


At that point, all the buildup reached its peak.


The little voice in my head piped up:


“It was such a rough day. You might as well. It’s only text—not images. Don’t do anything really wrong. Just play around with the AI a little. It will be fun and relaxing. Let’s see what has changed on those sites.”


So I did.


At first, I told myself I was only playing around with the AI and trying to confuse it. The little voice kept egging me on:


“See how much fun this is? This is your nekamah for everything AI has done to you.”


Of course, that makes no sense. The AI just resets after you leave.


After a little while, the voice pushed further:


“You’re already here. Why not have a small conversation? Why not see what’s new?”


And there was something new. I won’t get into the details because I don’t want to give anyone ideas. But almost immediately, I could see where it was trying to pull me.


I nearly got pulled in, but after the briefest look, I turned my eyes away.


When that didn’t work, the Satan tried something he knew might work better: getting me more involved in the conversation.


One minute passed. Then two. Then three.


It felt almost like I was being held at gunpoint.


Finally, I told myself:


“Reb Yid, you are getting married in two months. On top of that, it’s Elul. What are you doing? You know that if you continue, it will only lead you further.”


I shut off the phone and put it down.


But the Satan wasn’t finished. All my koach was drained, and he kept pushing. My first thought was:


“Let’s go back and get the phone. I already failed, so why stop now?”


With the dejection and the dopamine rush, I came close.


But then a more powerful thought came:


ENOUGH OF THIS!


I ran, got my hat and jacket, and went straight to the beis medrash.


It wasn’t my best Maariv ever, but I davened. Then I managed to put in a few hours of night seder and learn through a halachic question.


Afterward, I spoke to one of my mentors. He gave me his psak:


“Not only is this not a fall—it’s a victory!”


And I guess he is right.


The Satan wanted me to say, “I already failed, so there is no reason to stop.” He wanted to turn one bad moment into a complete collapse.


But I did stop.


I looked away. I shut off the phone. I went to the beis medrash. I davened, learned, and reached out to a mentor.


Maybe victory doesn’t always look like having no struggle at all.


Sometimes victory looks like being deep inside the battle—and finally shouting:


“Enough of this!”


7 KEYS TO A STRONGER MARRIAGE · KEY #5

Solve the Problems You Can Solve

Adapted from the research of Dr. John Gottman


The last four keys were about the foundation of a marriage, knowing, admiring, turning toward, and yielding to your spouse. This week’s key is about the thing couples usually think marriage help is only about: handling conflict. But Gottman’s findings here are surprising, and they turn out to match the Torah’s wisdom about speech almost exactly.


First, a crucial distinction Gottman discovered. Most marital conflicts, about two-thirds of them, are perpetual, rooted in deep, lasting differences between two people, and will never be fully “solved.” (We’ll deal with those in a later key.) But the rest are solvable, situational problems with a real answer: the schedule, a specific chore, how to handle a particular expense. The mistake couples make is treating a solvable problem like a war. Gottman found that solvable problems don’t need deep techniques; they need a few simple skills, and the most important one is how you start.


A soft answer, or a hard one


Gottman’s single biggest finding about conflict is this: a conversation almost always ends on the same note it began. He calls it the “harsh startup” versus the “soft startup.” Open with criticism or contempt, “You never help, what’s wrong with you?”, and the whole conversation is already lost, no matter how it’s dressed up afterward. Open gently, “I’m exhausted, could you take the garbage tonight?”, and the same request lands completely differently. His research could predict the outcome of an entire discussion from its first three minutes.


Shlomo HaMelech said this three thousand years ago, in five words: “Ma’aneh rach meishiv cheimah”, a soft answer turns away wrath. (Mishlei 15:1) It’s the identical principle. The gentleness of the opening decides where the whole exchange goes. The Torah has always known that in the home, how you say a thing matters as much as what you say, and that a soft word defuses what a hard one would ignite.


Repair, and step back before you boil over


Gottman’s second finding: happy couples aren’t the ones who never argue, they’re the ones who know how to repair an argument before it spirals. A small gesture mid-fight, a bit of humor, a hand on the shoulder, “let me try that again,” pulls the conversation back from the edge. And when things get too heated, they take a break. Gottman found that once a person is truly flooded with anger, physiologically, heart pounding, they literally can’t hear their spouse anymore, and nothing productive can happen until they’ve calmed down.


The Torah prizes exactly this restraint. Chazal praise the one who is ma’avir al midosav, who can let things pass, and warn again and again about the destructive power of anger in the home. Stepping back to cool down isn’t weakness or avoidance; it’s the wisdom to not say the thing you can’t take back. “A soft answer” is often only possible after you’ve given yourself a moment to soften.


Then compromise, and accept what won’t change


Finally, on the genuinely solvable problems, Gottman found that lasting solutions come from real compromise, and from accepting that your spouse has faults you’re not going to fix. You look for the common ground, the part you both agree on, and build the solution outward from there, rather than demanding total victory. This is the everyday work of two reyim ahuvim, partners, not opponents, solving a shared problem together.


Your Action Steps This Week


  1. Soften your startup. Before raising something that’s bothering you, rewrite your opening line in your head. Swap “you always/you never” for “I feel…” or “could you help me with…”. The first ten words decide the next ten minutes.
  2. Take the break. The next time you feel yourself getting truly heated, say so, “I need a few minutes,” and step away to cool down before you continue. You’ll say something wiser in twenty minutes than you would right now.
  3. Look for the yes. On your next solvable disagreement, start by naming what you both already agree on. Build the compromise out from that shared ground instead of from the fight.
