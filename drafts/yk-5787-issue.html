SHABBOS SHUVA

Why Teshuvah Works

By Rabbi Dr. Abraham J. Twerski zt”l


“It shall be that when many evils and distresses come upon it, then this song shall speak up before it as a witness for it shall not be forgotten from the mouth of its offspring, for I know its inclination” (Deut. 31:21).


Malbim interprets this verse as a Divine promise to be forgiving when the Children of Israel will do teshuvah, repentance.


Malbim cites the Talmud which states that the prophet Elijah pleaded for the Israelites who had become idolatrous, saying to G-d, “It was You Who made it possible for them to go astray,” and that G-d admitted, “Yes, I created the yetzer hara, the evil inclination” (Berachos 32b). G-d accepts part of the responsibility for our sins, having created so powerful a yetzer hara.


Forgiveness requires teshuvah: regret for having sinned and a sincere resolution not to repeat the sinful act. But why is teshuvah effective? If a person commits a crime and pleads before the judge, “I’m sorry I did it and I promise I will never do it again,” this will hardly stop the judge from imposing a penalty.


Teshuvah is effective because G-d understands how vulnerable we are to the cunning and temptations of the yetzer hara. Therefore, if we realize that we have been duped by the yetzer hara, G-d takes this into consideration.


The chassidic master, the Shpoler Zeide, used to plead for his people, “Master of the universe! You have placed temptations before people’s eyes, but the punishments of Gehinnom (hell) are described in the books. If You had placed Gehinnom right before people’s eyes and the temptations in the books, I assure You, no one would sin.”


There is no justification for sin, but if a person who has sinned does teshuvah, G-d assumes part of the responsibility and forgives the sin.


This, Malbim says, is the promise in the above verse. “It shall be that when many evils and distresses come upon it, then this song shall speak before it as a witness . . . for I know its inclination.” When the troubles that befall Israel will cause us to do teshuvah, G-d promises to forgive, because He knows the power of the evil inclination. This song, the Torah, “shall not be forgotten from the mouths of its offspring,” and it will be a witness to plead in our behalf.


A person may be discouraged from doing teshuvah, thinking, “What’s the use? I cannot expect G-d to forgive me for having disobeyed Him for so long.” G-d promises that if a person does teshuvah, He will enter a plea in his behalf, assuming part of the responsibility for the person’s behavior.


It is never too late for teshuvah.

“Who is Hacking Me
During Davening?”

For context, my standing in regard to this amazing journey of Lust currently includes this that my mind is usually under control, meaning although I can have the odd fantasy or somethin’ like that, it’s not one of my usual things I deal with.

I was shocked then, when as soon as I started davening (praying) on Rosh Hashana, my mind was inundated with CRAZY thoughts. Lusty things I never think of. It was crazy, and totally outta control. Almost like your rebbi teaches you, how the yetzer harah tries to foil you from doing mitzvos n’ stuff, but what threw me off, was how unsophisticated its tactics were. It happened exactly how my 9 year old self would’ve envisioned an attack from the yetzer hara.

I would love feedback if anyperson has ideas what the heck happened there, and/or how to keep that from recurring.


@livingandlearning replied:


You tried all the techniques. THOSE are the hegyon libis that you are putting before Him. He loves them and treasures them.


And, as I told @guywithalife privately, I know exactly what he’s talking about. This takes up much of my RH avoda too. Pretty much every year.



@BenHashemBH replied:


As I did it yesterday, realized that I could add a bit more to the focus shifting in technique. With shemiras einayim, I use the image of the fire on the Mizbeach (as suggested by one of the distributed SE books I read - maybe the Chofetz Chaim Heritage Foundation one.).


The other one I use often for thoughts, esp during davening, is imagining the thought as a silver strand and gently pulling it out of my head with my hand and then letting it go to dissipate on its way to the floor. @mendygye replied:


This is a real life example of what’s described in Tanya Ch. 28:


כשנפש האלהית מתאמצת ומתגברת להתפלל, אזי גם הקליפה מתגברת כנגדה לבלבלה ולהפילה במחשבה זרה שלה. ולא כטעות העולם, שטועים להוכיח מנפילת המחשבה זרה מכלל שאין תפלתם כלום, שאילו התפלל כראוי ונכון לא היו נופלים לו מחשבות זרות. והאמת היה כדבריהם אם היתה נפש אחת לבדה היא המתפללת והיא המחשבת ומהרהרת המחשבות זרות. אבל באמת לאמיתו הן שתי נפשות הנלחמות זו עם זו במוחו של אדם, כל אחת חפצה ורצונה למשול בו ולהיות המוח ממולא ממנה לבדה..


When the Divine soul pushes itself and grows stronger in prayer, the kelipah pushes back, trying to confuse it and bring it down with מחשבות זרות of its own.


People often mistake these distracting thoughts for proof that their prayer is worthless. They think, “If I were really praying properly, these thoughts wouldn’t be popping into my head.”


That would be true if we had only one soul, i.e., if the same soul that was praying was also producing those distracting thoughts. But the truth is that we have two souls fighting each other inside our mind. Each one wants to take control and fill the mind entirely with itself.


Tanya, Likkutei Amarim, ch. 28.

7 KEYS TO A STRONGER MARRIAGE · KEY #6

Understand the Dream Behind the Conflict

Adapted from the research of Dr. John Gottman


Last week we talked about the problems you can solve. This week is about the ones you can’t, and how to stop them from tearing a marriage apart.


Here’s one of Gottman’s most important discoveries: most marital conflicts, roughly two-thirds of them, are never fully “solved.” They’re perpetual. They sit on top of deep, lasting differences between two people, in temperament, in how they were raised, in what they value, and they’ll come up again and again for the length of a marriage. One spouse is a saver, the other a spender. One wants family always around, the other needs quiet. These aren’t problems with a solution; they’re differences between two whole people.


Gottman found that the couples who thrive aren’t the ones who somehow resolve these, they’re the ones who learn to talk about them without gridlock. And the couples in trouble are the ones who dig in, each trying to win, until the same fight hardens into resentment and they stop even being able to discuss it.


Stop trying to win. Start trying to understand.


Gottman’s breakthrough move is this: underneath each person’s stubborn position, there is almost always a dream, a value, a history, a need, that matters deeply to them. The spender isn’t reckless; maybe he grew up with nothing and money means freedom to him. The saver isn’t cheap; maybe money means security she never had. When couples fight about the money, they’re really fighting about two dreams they’ve never spoken out loud. And the way out of gridlock isn’t to win the argument, it’s to get curious: why does this matter so much to you? what does it mean to you?


Honor the dream, even without agreeing


Once you accept that the differences are permanent, the question changes from “how do I win this?” to “how do I honor what my spouse cares about, even though I see it differently?” And that’s the move that actually ends gridlock. When your spouse feels that you understand the dream underneath their position, and that you respect it, the conflict stops being a war, even if the difference itself never goes away. You don’t have to agree with the dream. You have to let it be real, and treat it as something worth protecting because it belongs to the person you love.


This is the everyday avodah of two people building one home out of two different worlds. The tension may remain. The gridlock lifts.


Your Action Steps This Week


  1. Name one perpetual conflict. Identify the disagreement that keeps coming back in your marriage, the one you’ve never actually solved. Just naming it as “perpetual, not solvable” takes pressure off both of you.
  2. Ask about the dream. Instead of restating your side, ask your spouse: “Help me understand why this matters so much to you. What does it mean to you?” Then just listen, no rebutting, no fixing.
  3. Honor it, even without agreeing. Find one small way to show your spouse you respect the dream behind their position, even if you still see it differently. Understanding, not victory, is what ends the gridlock.
