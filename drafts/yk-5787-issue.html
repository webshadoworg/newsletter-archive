SHABBOS SHUVA

Why Teshuvah Works

By Rabbi Dr. Abraham J. Twerski zt”l


“It shall be that when many evils and distresses come upon it, then this song shall speak up before it as a witness for it shall not be forgotten from the mouth of its offspring, for I know its inclination” (Deut. 31:21).


Malbim interprets this verse as a Divine promise to be forgiving when the Children of Israel will do teshuvah, repentance.


Malbim cites the Talmud which states that the prophet Elijah pleaded for the Israelites who had become idolatrous, saying to G-d, “It was You Who made it possible for them to go astray,” and that G-d admitted, “Yes, I created the yetzer hara, the evil inclination” (Berachos 32b). G-d accepts part of the responsibility for our sins, having created so powerful a yetzer hara.


Forgiveness requires teshuvah: regret for having sinned and a sincere resolution not to repeat the sinful act. But why is teshuvah effective? If a person commits a crime and pleads before the judge, “I’m sorry I did it and I promise I will never do it again,” this will hardly stop the judge from imposing a penalty.


Teshuvah is effective because G-d understands how vulnerable we are to the cunning and temptations of the yetzer hara. Therefore, if we realize that we have been duped by the yetzer hara, G-d takes this into consideration.


The chassidic master, the Shpoler Zeide, used to plead for his people, “Master of the universe! You have placed temptations before people’s eyes, but the punishments of Gehinnom (hell) are described in the books. If You had placed Gehinnom right before people’s eyes and the temptations in the books, I assure You, no one would sin.”


There is no justification for sin, but if a person who has sinned does teshuvah, G-d assumes part of the responsibility and forgives the sin.


This, Malbim says, is the promise in the above verse. “It shall be that when many evils and distresses come upon it, then this song shall speak before it as a witness . . . for I know its inclination.” When the troubles that befall Israel will cause us to do teshuvah, G-d promises to forgive, because He knows the power of the evil inclination. This song, the Torah, “shall not be forgotten from the mouths of its offspring,” and it will be a witness to plead in our behalf.


A person may be discouraged from doing teshuvah, thinking, “What’s the use? I cannot expect G-d to forgive me for having disobeyed Him for so long.” G-d promises that if a person does teshuvah, He will enter a plea in his behalf, assuming part of the responsibility for the person’s behavior.


JOYFUL TESHUVA

5 Powerful Perspectives for Joyful Teshuva

The five perspectives are adapted from “Strategies for Successful Teshuva” by Rabbi Chaim Morgenstern.


1. You don’t have to fix everything to begin


Imagine a businessman who owes far more than he can repay. He can’t clear his debts today. But he contacts the people he owes, asks for more time, and starts paying what he can.


Even a small payment says: “I haven’t walked away. I want to make this right.”


Rav Pam explains that we can approach teshuvah in the same way.


You may look at everything you want to change and feel overwhelmed. Where do you even start? You’ve tried before. You’re still struggling. Fixing everything feels out of reach.


But if you can’t do everything today, you can still do something.


Choose one practical step: put a stronger boundary in place, reach out for help, or make time for something that helps you stay on track. Take that step because you want to keep going.


Hashem sees your desire to return, and He helps you move forward. The Midrash describes it this way: make an opening as small as the eye of a needle, and Hashem will open a passage wide enough for wagons.


Your first step may be small. It still matters.


Rav Pam, The Pleasant Way, pp. 239-40. The Midrash is Midrash Shir Hashirim 5:3.


EDITOR’S NOTE

Editor’s note: A teaching in the name of the Baal Shem Tov takes this idea a step further. In Tishrei, Hashem gives us the coming year’s blessings “on credit.” Throughout the year, we’re meant to repay that debt through our avodas Hashem. When Elul arrives, we take stock and begin paying it back through teshuvah.

When Hashem sees that we sincerely want to repay the debt, and have at least started doing so, He forgives the rest. Then, on Rosh Hashanah, He gives us another good year on credit.

Our willingness to begin means so much to Him that He gives us a fresh start.

Adapted from a teaching attributed to the Baal Shem Tov, Kesser Shem Tov, Hosafos, §191.


2. You don’t have to wait until you’ve fixed everything


The Pri Megadim teaches something remarkable: when a person sincerely commits to doing teshuvah and leaving his old ways, he is no longer considered a rasha in Hashem’s eyes.


How can that happen before he has done teshuvah for everything?


Because there is a difference between receiving forgiveness for the past and changing where you stand today. Full teshuvah still requires regret, confession, and a commitment to change. But the sincere decision to begin already changes your standing before Hashem.


Think about what that means as Yom Kippur approaches. Whatever happened this past year, you don’t have to wait until you’ve built up months of success for this change to happen.


You can sincerely commit now: “Hashem, I want to leave this behind. I’m ready to begin doing teshuvah.”


Then put that commitment into practice with one real step. The work ahead is real, but so is the change that has already begun. And Hashem will give you siyata dishmaya to help you continue.


Pri Megadim, Pesicha Kollelet part 3 §10, on the gemara in Kiddushin 49b.


3. A later fall doesn’t erase sincere teshuvah


“I meant it last Yom Kippur. I really wanted to change. But then I fell again. Does that mean my teshuvah wasn’t real?”


The Mabit teaches that if your teshuvah was sincere at the time, a later fall does not erase it. Falling afterward does not prove that you never meant what you said.


You need to get up and return again. But you don’t need to dismiss your earlier teshuvah as a lie.


The Mabit also teaches that regret itself has value, even when you haven’t yet managed to stop. That feeling, “I wish I hadn’t done this; I want things to be different”, can help bring you toward fuller teshuvah.


And a change you manage to keep for a limited time also counts. Rav Yisrael Salanter teaches that overcoming an aveira for part of the time is a real achievement in teshuvah.


Mabit, Bais Elokim, quoted in Alei Shur vol. 1, pp. 45-6. Rav Yisrael Salanter, Lekach Tov, Yamim Nora’im, vol. 2 p. 110.


4. You’re not always back at the beginning


Rav Shalom Schwadron compares repeated attempts at teshuvah to digging a hole in soft ground.


You lift out a shovelful of earth, and more dirt slides back in. You dig again. More falls back. It can feel as though you’re getting nowhere.


But each time you dig, you remove more earth. Even with the setbacks, the hole is taking shape.


That is how teshuvah can feel. You make a sincere effort, move forward, and then fall again. Looking at the setback, you may think you’ve lost everything you worked for.


The mashal reminds you that your efforts have value even when the results are hard to see.


Get up. Renew your commitment. Learn from what happened and take the next step.


The dirt that fell back in isn’t the whole story. You’ve also been digging.


Rav Shalom Schwadron, Lekach Tov, vol. 2 pp. 108-9.


5. Hashem sees the pain, too


Does it matter that a fall hurts you? That you wish you hadn’t done it, even though you did?


Rav Itzele Peterburger, a student of Rav Yisrael Salanter, teaches that it matters a great deal.


He points to a Gemara about someone who steals a sheep. The Torah takes into account even the embarrassment the thief experienced while carrying it away. Even in judging a wrongdoing, Hashem notices what the person went through.


Rav Itzele applies this to an aveira. A person may give in and still feel pain over what he is doing. That pain may come out as a sigh: “Oy, I wish I weren’t doing this.”


That is the sigh he means: an expression of real regret, even while the person is falling.


Rav Itzele explains that in Shamayim, the aveira is recorded together with that sigh. The fact that it hurt him to do it becomes part of how the act is judged. The difference between an aveira done with that regret and one done without it, he says, is enormous.


The person still needs to do teshuvah. But even the regret he felt in the middle of the fall carries real weight before Hashem.


Rav Itzele Peterburger, quoted in Alei Shur vol. 2, p. 440, on Bava Kama 79b and Shemos 21:37.

WISDOM FROM THE FORUM

“Who is Hacking Me During Davening?”

@guywithalife asked:


For context, my standing in regard to this amazing journey of Lust currently includes this that my mind is usually under control, meaning although I can have the odd fantasy or somethin’ like that, it’s not one of my usual things I deal with.

I was shocked then, when as soon as I started davening (praying) on Rosh Hashana, my mind was inundated with CRAZY thoughts. Lusty things I never think of. It was crazy, and totally outta control. As soon as I finish the prayers tho, aside from some insignificant vestiges, it was gone. Then it returned for the next tefilla. It blew my mind!!!!

Almost like your rebbi teaches you, how the yetzer harah tries to foil you from doing mitzvos n’ stuff, but what threw me off, was how unsophisticated its tactics were. It happened exactly how my 9 year old self would’ve envisioned an attack from the yetzer hara.

I would love feedback if anyperson has ideas what the heck happened there, and/or how to keep that from recurring.


@livingandlearning replied:


You tried all the techniques. THOSE are the hegyon libis that you are putting before Him. He loves them and treasures them.


And, as I told @guywithalife privately, I know exactly what he’s talking about. This takes up much of my RH avoda too. Pretty much every year.



@BenHashemBH replied:


As I did it yesterday, realized that I could add a bit more to the focus shifting in technique. With shemiras einayim, I use the image of the fire on the Mizbeach (as suggested by one of the distributed SE books I read - maybe the Chofetz Chaim Heritage Foundation one.).


The other one I use often for thoughts, esp during davening, is imagining the thought as a silver strand and gently pulling it out of my head with my hand and then letting it go to dissipate on its way to the floor. That clears my mind a bit to then fill the space with something else. Whatever works. It should be smooth and calm, not forceful.



@mendygye replied:


This is a real life example of what’s described in Tanya Ch. 28:


כשנפש האלהית מתאמצת ומתגברת להתפלל, אזי גם הקליפה מתגברת כנגדה לבלבלה ולהפילה במחשבה זרה שלה. ולא כטעות העולם, שטועים להוכיח מנפילת המחשבה זרה מכלל שאין תפלתם כלום, שאילו התפלל כראוי ונכון לא היו נופלים לו מחשבות זרות. והאמת היה כדבריהם אם היתה נפש אחת לבדה היא המתפללת והיא המחשבת ומהרהרת המחשבות זרות. אבל באמת לאמיתו הן שתי נפשות הנלחמות זו עם זו במוחו של אדם, כל אחת חפצה ורצונה למשול בו ולהיות המוח ממולא ממנה לבדה..


When the Divine soul pushes itself and grows stronger in prayer, the kelipah pushes back, trying to confuse it and bring it down with מחשבות זרות of its own.


People often mistake these distracting thoughts for proof that their prayer is worthless. They think, “If I were really praying properly, these thoughts wouldn’t be popping into my head.”


That would be true if we had only one soul, i.e., if the same soul that was praying was also producing those distracting thoughts. But the truth is that we have two souls fighting each other inside our mind. Each one wants to take control and fill the mind entirely with itself.
