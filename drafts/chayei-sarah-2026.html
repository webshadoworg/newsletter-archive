The Philosophizing Trap

Avraham comes to Chevron to buy a burial plot for Sarah. Efron stands up in front of the whole town and makes a grand offer: “A field worth four hundred shekel, what is that between me and you? Take it.” It sounds generous. But when the money is weighed out, Efron takes every shekel, and in the largest coins around.


In the very pasuk where Avraham weighs out the silver, the Torah spells Efron’s name without a vav. Rashi explains: חָסֵר וָי”ו, לְפִי שֶׁאָמַר הַרְבֵּה וַאֲפִלּוּ מְעַט לֹא עָשָׂה - it’s missing a vav, because he said a lot and didn’t do even a little.


Shammai teaches the opposite: אֱמֹר מְעַט וַעֲשֵׂה הַרְבֵּה - say a little and do a lot.


There’s an Efron trap in recovery too: the philosophizing trap. You read about recovery, talk about it, debate the different approaches and form opinions on all of them, with no real intention of putting any of it into practice anytime soon. It’s tiring and it’s about the right subject, so it feels like work. But the week ahead looks exactly the same.


Planning is different. Planning is practical: what exactly am I going to do? Which filter, who do I call, what happens at 11 at night when the house goes quiet? That kind of clarity is worth a lot. It’s the step right before doing.


How do you tell them apart? Ask yourself one question: will this change what I do tonight or tomorrow? If it will, you’re planning. If it won’t, you’re philosophizing.


That’s the “little” Shammai wants from us: just enough clarity to know your next move. Then comes the “lot”: using the same simple tools again and again, on an ordinary Tuesday, when you’re not in the mood. Say a little. Do a lot.


FROM A MEMBER

“Aibishter, 85!!!”

By @musicmentch
Editor’s Note: He wrote after a previous fall, now he is 49 days clean. His attitude towards the fall definitely helped him get back up.


Those 85 days were cut off by a fall just now.


I have never posted before because I had enough chizuk from GYE, but with a fall just now I feel it would help to keep my own record in a place like this.


Boruch Hashem, I have never struggled outright with “Mr P,” although I am not fully innocent. For me, as a bochur, it was inappropriate images and videos that are termed “kosher” enough in some circles but have certain parts that have to be skipped. This would ultimately lead to “Mr M.” I never owned a smartphone, and we didn’t even have internet at home until Corona. From age 17, already a couple of years in yeshiva, I opened up to someone about it who helped me a lot. I had nearly a full clean year before I got married, and the first three years of my marriage were blissfully and totally clean.


Then I once stumbled on something on my strongly filtered laptop (yes, things slip through), and I slowly spiralled into monthly falls. It was worse than as a bochur, because as a married man I was no longer naive. BH I am blessed with an amazing marriage. But that yetzer hara is clever.


Still, with the help of my mentor I managed 30 days, then 60 days, then another couple of 30 days, and now finally 85 days. It’s been great, but you are never free. The low is still a low and the battle is still real. Sometimes thinking “I’m not even really struggling” is itself the reason to fall. That’s when the YH gets you.


When I adjusted my counter on the GYE website, I took a long look at that “85,” well aware it was about to be replaced by a “0,” and I started to break up. But then I stopped and said to the heavens, “Aibishter, 85!!! Aibishter, 85!!!” How blessed I am to have achieved 85, which still remains an untouched 85. So why should the one fall today get in the way of the next 85, BeH?


Thank you, Hashem, for making me come so far.


One last point. What would you tell someone else in this situation? I would say, “You’re doing great. Believe in yourself. C’mon, you can do this. Pick yourself up NOW and move onwards.” So that’s what you’ve got to tell yourself.


Let’s keep up the fight!


MARRIAGE

The Relationship Beneath the Ground

Based on an essay by Rabbi YY Jacobson, theyeshiva.net


This week’s parsha opens with Sarah’s passing and Avraham buying the Me’aras HaMachpeila to bury her. Then, right away, the Torah tells the long love story of Yitzchak and Rivka. Death and burial sit right next to the start of a new marriage.


The Gemara goes further. The Torah never spells out how a marriage is made, so Chazal learn it from Avraham’s purchase of the cave, through a gzeirah shava, a comparison of two places where the Torah uses the same word. Just as Avraham acquired the field with money, a chosson betroths his kallah with money, the ring under the chuppah. Every Jewish marriage is built on a law we learn from buying a burial plot.


Why learn how to begin a marriage from a story that ended one? Because every relationship has two layers: one above the ground, and one buried beneath it.


The first layer is built from feelings. It’s real, and it’s wonderful when the passion runs high. But feelings rise and fall, and a marriage built only on them can leave a couple married on paper while feeling divorced inside.


The second layer doesn’t depend on what you feel at the moment. Your bond with your mother isn’t created by your warm feelings toward her. Your feelings grow out of a bond that exists no matter what. The Zohar says a husband and wife are “two halves of one soul.” Their connection began before they were born and can’t be undone, even by death.


That is what Avraham was saying when he bought a cave for Sarah. His bond with her didn’t end at her death, because it never began at her life. And that is the message of the gzeirah shava: under the chuppah, a chosson isn’t only marrying his kallah on the surface. He is entering the cave with her, the bond in the depths of their souls.


This is why Sarah’s burial comes right before Yitzchak and Rivka’s marriage. Yitzchak and Rivka had real disagreements, some with historic consequences, yet they never lost their loyalty to each other, because they never forgot the connection beneath the ground.


The same is true of our relationship with Hashem. Some days it feels alive and close. Other days it feels distant, and you’re numb and not even sure He’s there. In those moments, remember the cave. Beneath the surface there is a bond with Hashem that is hidden but never gone. It may be quieter than the relationship above ground, but it’s the one that lasts forever.


Based on the discourses of the Tzemach Tzedek.
