Dear Chaveirim,


You get to a number. Ninety days, six months, two years. And somewhere in you is the quiet hope that at some point the fight is behind you and you can stop watching yourself.


Chayei Sarah says otherwise, twice. Avraham’s last test came after the Akeida, and the Torah learns the laws of marriage from the day he buried his wife.

Living Each Day

I once heard a powerful idea from R’ Mattisyahu Salomon zt”l.


Most Rishonim say that Avraham Avinu’s final nisayon was the Akeida. But Rabbeinu Yonah says the last nisayon was burying Sarah.


The question is obvious. After the Akeida, what was so hard about having to haggle with Efron over a burial plot?


The answer is that sometimes, after a person has been through a very hard nisayon, he feels, “Hashem, I’ve done my part, leave me alone already.” And Avraham had every reason to feel that way. He had just come through nine difficult tests. Surely he had proven his loyalty. But in this world there is no let-up, and that was the final, hardest test of all: to pick himself up and keep going, right after the greatest moment of his life, as if there were no “I’ve earned a rest.”


This is sharpened by a Mishna in Avos. “Al ta’amin b’atzmecha ad yom mosecha”, do not trust yourself until the day you die (Avos 2:4). The Mishna brings a startling proof: Yochanan Kohen Gadol served as Kohen Gadol for eighty years, and in the end became a tzedoki. Eighty years of avodah in the Beis Hamikdash, and the battle was still not “won.” The test was still live. As long as a person is alive, there is no finish line past which the yetzer hara packs up and goes home.


That is both the sobering truth and, strangely, the freeing one. There is no point at which you’ve “made it” and the fight is behind you forever. But that also means a fall after long success is not some bizarre failure that unravels everything, it’s simply the nature of a battle that lasts a lifetime. Avraham didn’t get a pass after nine nisyonos, and he didn’t need one. He just took the next step.


So for all those who have reached 90 days, the battle has not ended. It has just begun. And that’s not discouraging. It’s exactly what it means to be alive and still in the fight. One more day, and then the next.


FROM A MEMBER

I Have Survived the Feeling Before

by member @ultimatesuccess (636 days clean)


I find myself once again fighting battles I’m unlikely to win. Not because I choose to fight them, but because I have to. They are handed to me on a silver platter, one after another, even as I sit here writing this very journal.


I think about all the times I’ve wished I could do something without being consumed by guilt. And now, here I am, caught between pent-up desire and what feels like the greatest sin in the world.


It’s not that I wish I didn’t have these desires. It’s that I wish I were permitted to act on them.


That’s what desire does to you. It takes hold of your mind and makes you crave things you believe you shouldn’t have. It holds your thoughts hostage while your conscience struggles to break free. You become both the prisoner and the jailer, trapped in an endless game of tug-of-war with yourself. Back and forth. Forward and backward. Never quite arriving anywhere.


It begins to feel as though the only decisions left for you to make are these: Do I give in, or do I resist?


There is no room for the ordinary. No room for the mediocre, the mundane, or the simple pleasures of being alive. Everything becomes a question of indulgence or restraint, sin or abstinence, surrender or resistance.


Life feels empty without the thing you crave. Yet you fear that if you give in, you will be the one left feeling empty.


And so you remain suspended between two opposing forces, convinced that there is no middle ground. Once you start peeking through the door, it’s over. There are no second chances, no harmless glances, no room for error. One small step, and the entire battle is lost.


But perhaps there is another way to fight.


Sometimes, you let yourself be distracted. You watch a Disney movie. You eat until you’re satisfied. You allow yourself a few hours of ordinary, uncomplicated enjoyment. You give your mind something else to hold on to, something harmless to occupy it while the storm passes.


It reminds me of a scene in the 2013 film After Earth, when Jaden Smith’s character encounters a dangerous organism while exploring the wilderness. His body begins to shut down, and he finds himself helpless, unable to do much more than endure what is happening to him. He has to wait for the treatment to take effect. He has to trust that, despite how powerless he feels in the moment, the feeling will eventually pass.


Perhaps that’s what these battles require of me, too.


Not every urge needs to be answered. Not every feeling demands a decision. Sometimes, the only thing I need to do is endure the discomfort without allowing it to dictate my actions. I can let myself be distracted for a few hours rather than surrender to something that might leave me wrestling with guilt for days.


I don’t have to win the battle in a single, decisive moment. I only have to make it through the moment I’m in.


Because this, too, shall pass.


The test may seem impossible while I’m standing in the middle of it. The desire may feel all-consuming, the temptation unbearable, the choice impossibly heavy. But time has a way of changing the scale of things. What feels like an insurmountable obstacle tonight may seem like nothing more than a small prick in the road tomorrow.


And by then, I’ll be past it.


Not because the desire never existed. Not because the battle was easy. But because I endured it, and the feeling that once seemed capable of consuming me no longer holds the same power.


I have survived the feeling before. I can survive it again.


MARRIAGE

The Relationship Beneath the Ground

Based on the discourses of the Tzemach Tzedek
After an essay by Rabbi YY Jacobson, theyeshiva.net


This week’s parsha opens with Sarah’s passing and Avraham working hard to buy the Me’aras HaMachpeila, the “double cave,” to bury her. And then, immediately, the Torah launches into the long love story of Yitzchak and Rivka. One parsha, death and burial side by side with the dawn of a new marriage.


There’s something even more striking in the Gemara. The Torah never spells out how a marriage is legally made. Chazal fill the gap with a gzeirah shava, a comparison of two places where the Torah uses the same word, and the case they compare marriage to is Avraham’s purchase of the cave. Just as Avraham acquired the field with money, so a chosson betroths his kallah with money, the ring under the chuppah. To this day, every Jewish marriage is built on a law we learn from Avraham buying a burial plot.


It seems almost tasteless. Why would we learn how to begin a marriage from a story that ended one? Why compare taking a wife to buying a grave? The strangeness is the point. It pushes us to look deeper, into the hidden “caves” of our own relationships.


The Torah describes two dimensions to every relationship: one above the ground, visible and conscious, and one buried beneath it, hidden.


The first layer is built from feelings. “I love you because I feel close to you.” It’s real, and it’s wonderful when the passion runs high. But feelings rise and fall. When the warmth fades, a marriage built only on that layer falters, and a couple can end up married on paper while feeling divorced inside.


But there’s a second layer, buried underground, in the caves of the soul. This is the bond that doesn’t depend on what you’re feeling at the moment. The clearest example is a parent and child: your bond with your mother isn’t created by your warm feelings toward her; your feelings grow out of a bond that exists no matter what. She’s your mother. It’s part of your essence, and nothing erases it. The Zohar says a husband and wife are “two halves of one soul.” Their connection is intrinsic and eternal, it began before they were born and can’t be undone, even by death.


That is what Avraham was declaring when he bought a cave for Sarah, the underground place. He was saying that his bond with her did not end at her death, because it never began at her life. It lives beneath the ground, where it can’t be destroyed.


And that is the message hidden in the gzeirah shava. When a chosson places the ring on his kallah’s finger, the Torah is telling him he isn’t only marrying her on the surface, in the realm of feeling. He is entering the “cave” with her, the timeless bond in the depths of their souls. The chuppah isn’t only the union of two people. It’s the reunion of two halves that were always one.


This is why the Torah places Sarah’s burial right before Yitzchak and Rivka’s marriage. Before you build a marriage, know what you’re building: a bond with your eternal partner, one that no argument or crisis can tear apart, because underneath it all your core and your spouse’s core are one. Yitzchak and Rivka had real disagreements, some with historic consequences, and yet they never lost their loyalty to each other, because they never forgot the connection beneath the ground.


And the same is true of our relationship with Hashem, which marriage has always mirrored. There are times it feels alive and close, above the ground. And there are times it feels dead, distant, when you’re numb and not even sure He’s there. In those moments, remember the cave. Beneath the surface there is a bond with Hashem that is hidden but never gone, a spark etched into the deepest chamber of your soul that can never be extinguished. It may be quieter than the relationship above ground. But it is the one that lasts forever.
