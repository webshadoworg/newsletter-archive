The Bad Teiva

Tell me, is a teiva a good thing or a bad thing?


Like almost everything in the world, it depends on the context. In one context it’s good, and in another it can be bad. At the beginning of our parsha, Hashem commands Noach to build a teiva to be saved from the Mabul. At that stage the teiva is a good thing. It protects Noach, his family and the animals. But once the Mabul is over, Hashem commands Noach, צֵא מִן הַתֵּבָה, “Go out of the teiva,” to settle the world again. Staying in the teiva at that point would already be bad, and against Hashem’s will.


We face a kind of mabul too. And on that note, something interesting: R’ Chaim Vital writes (Shaarei Kedusha 1:2) that the root of all the middos lies in the four elements that every creation is made of (Rambam, Yesodei HaTorah 4:1): fire, air, water and earth. And the root of taavah? Water, of course! To protect ourselves from the flood of taavah, we need a teiva too. But not every teiva helps. There is a good teiva that shields us from the current, and a bad teiva that actually makes us sink. Let’s start with the good ones.


Many gedolei Yisrael in recent generations called the beis medrash a “teivas Noach” that protects us from the flood raging in the streets. Perhaps we can extend that to any positive activity we are busy with. When we are immersed in something good and connected to it, it puts some safe distance between us and the flood.


A filter is a good teiva as well. It may not be watertight, and there may be leaks and drips here and there, but that is a world away from giving the flood an official permit to wash over our minds every day at full force. The filter also keeps reminding us what we really want. Deep down, we don’t want to live soaked to the bone. We chose to build this teiva in a clear-headed moment, when we were at our best.


But there is also a bad teiva: when we struggle with this alone. We stay locked in with the taavah, in a dark teiva of gloomy despair, without letting in any light from another person. That teiva is a bad one. It only strengthens the inner flood, the flood of guilt and shame that makes us sink. We may think it’s comfortable to shut ourselves in and not tell anyone what we are going through. But it only looks like Noach’s ark. In reality, it isn’t a teiva at all. It’s a sinking ship.


GYE’s long experience has taught us that in every kind of struggle, connection with others is critical to our progress. If we want to stay clean for the long run, we need supportive partners on this journey.


צֵא מִן הַתֵּבָה! Come out of the teiva!
