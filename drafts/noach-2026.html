The Ark That Saves You and the Ark That Drowns You

Tell me, is a teiva a good thing or a bad thing?


Like almost everything in the world, it depends on the context. In one context it’s good, and in another it can be bad. At the beginning of our parsha, Hashem commands Noach to build a teiva to be saved from the Mabul. At that stage the teiva is a good thing. It protects Noach, his family and the animals. But once the Mabul is over, Hashem commands Noach, צֵא מִן הַתֵּבָה, “Go out of the teiva,” to settle the world again. Staying in the teiva at that point would already be bad, and against Hashem’s will.


We face a kind of mabul too. And on that note, something interesting: R’ Chaim Vital writes (Shaarei Kedusha 1:2) that the root of all the middos lies in the four elements that every creation is made of (Rambam, Yesodei HaTorah 4:1): fire, air, water and earth. And the root of taavah? Water, of course! To protect ourselves from the flood of taavah, we need a teiva too. But not every teiva helps. There is a good teiva that shields us from the current, and a bad teiva that actually makes us sink. Let’s start with the good ones.


Many gedolei Yisrael in recent generations called the beis medrash a “teivas Noach” that protects us from the flood raging in the streets. Perhaps we can extend that to any positive activity we are busy with. When we are immersed in something good and connected to it, it puts some safe distance between us and the flood.


A filter is a good teiva as well. It may not be watertight, and there may be leaks and drips here and there, but that is a world away from giving the flood an official permit to wash over our minds every day at full force. The filter also keeps reminding us what we really want. Deep down, we don’t want to live soaked to the bone. We chose to build this teiva in a clear-headed moment, when we were at our best.


But there is also a bad teiva: when we struggle with this alone. We stay locked in with the taavah, in a dark teiva of gloomy despair, without letting in any light from another person. That teiva is a bad one. It only strengthens the inner flood, the flood of guilt and shame that makes us sink. We may think it’s comfortable to shut ourselves in and not tell anyone what we are going through. But it only looks like Noach’s ark. In reality, it isn’t a teiva at all. It’s a sinking ship.


GYE’s long experience has taught us that in every kind of struggle, connection with others is critical to our progress. If we want to stay clean for the long run, we need supportive partners on this journey.


צֵא מִן הַתֵּבָה! Come out of the teiva!


WISDOM FROM THE FORUM

“I Need Some Help”

@iwanttochange1140 asked:


Baruch Hashem I finally got my computer filtered but I have a question that I could use some insight on.

But now baruch Hashem I won’t be able to look at things but am still tempted to look and fantasize. Without a filter it used to be on the screen and now it will be in my head.

My question is what type of strategies have any of you used to rid your mind of the thoughts and memories of things you have seen. I don’t want to just not look at this shmutz I want to not even think about thinking about it. I don’t want in a few years when I start shidduchim to be thinking about these things as I look at my potential wife.



@theboywholived replied:


I don’t have the answers for you. There is no strategy that will completely eliminate these thoughts or urges. It’s a day by day process of consciously deciding to turn away or stop thinking about what you are drawn towards.

I’m currently struggling immensely with this right now. I wish I could use my own advice as easily as I can give it. But the hard part isn’t getting a filter on your computer. It is filtering my own brain.



@mechazaik replied, quoting @chosemyshem:


Fantasy is much easier to stop than you probably think it is. The kuntz is to stop fighting thoughts. Have a prepared other engaging thing to think about. Accept that you’re having a fantasy, acknowledge that it is there, gently choose to think about something else. Then move on to your other engaging thought. Be persistent and you’ll see that it works amazingly well.

Learning how to value our less tangible wins is both difficult and important. One thing you might find helpful is cultivating a good feeling from being clean. Find something you enjoy about being clean and pay attention to that feeling. Feelings are like kids - they stick around when you pay attention to them. Find that positive feeling and grow it.

You don’t need to be “fed up” to stop. You can stop now.



@BenHashemBH replied:


Ya’asher Koach on the filter, Brother!

In preparation it helps to find something motivating and associate that with an image and/or thought. Review it many times and deeply until it carries meaning for you. This part is done when things are calm. Then, when an undesired image or thought arises, you conjure your response to replace it. Once your mind is cleared, then fill it with some other engaging topic to prevent space for the thought to return. It takes a little practice and finesse though, as you don’t want to make too big a deal out of the bad thoughts - that makes them stick around.

Don’t let the thoughts linger and play out. Over time, they should lessen, and moving on when they do come should get easier too.



@noilad replied:


A big nisayon now is not to test the filter for loopholes. It’s easy to self-justify that you are doing a good thing, as you will report anything that is found, etc...and in a way is a bigger nisayon as you are moreh heter.

It’s not your job - it’s your job not to test. Don’t consider yourself an “ethical hacker”.

In short, don’t worry, the YH won’t run out of cards to play, even on the device battlefront. Congrats on a very wise and admirable move!

Why Do Good Marriages Drift?

Based on a shiur by Rabbi YY Jacobson


Think about the last three messages you sent your wife.

“Do we need anything from the store?”
“What time are you coming home?”
“Can you pick up the kids?”

Now try to remember the last message you sent simply because you were thinking about her.

It is possible to spend the whole day in touch and still barely connect. You coordinate schedules, solve problems, keep a household running. Everything seems to be working. Yet somewhere in the middle of managing your life together, you begin to lose sight of each other.

That is how a good marriage can drift. Nothing has to break. Sometimes, all it takes is for two people to become so busy with everything that needs their attention that they forget their marriage needs it too.

The Baal HaTanya writes in Likutei Torah that a man and a woman are like fire and water. Sit with that image for a moment. Fire and water do not naturally settle into a comfortable partnership. Without something to hold them together, one overwhelms the other. But put water in a pot over a flame, and those same opposing forces can prepare a meal. The vessel gives their differences a shared purpose.

A marriage needs that vessel too, and building it is an ongoing responsibility.

So if you notice some distance, it does not necessarily mean you married the wrong person. It may mean that something precious needs attention. The marriage you have after ten years reflects thousands of small moments when you turned toward each other - or let the opportunity pass.

How often does it need that attention? Probably more often than we think. A few small moments of connection throughout the day can give each person the reassurance that they are still on the other’s mind.

We already understand this rhythm in our relationship with Hashem. Shacharis, Mincha, Maariv. Krias Shema in the morning and again at night. By the time Mincha comes around, Shacharis may feel far away. We have answered messages, dealt with problems, and become absorbed in the day. We need another moment to stop and remember before Whom we stand.

Attention slips. The rhythm of tefillah gives us a way to return.

A marriage needs those moments of return too. It is easy to spend an entire day caring for the life you share without actually connecting with the person you share it with.

And when you miss a moment, you can still reach out at the next one. A busy afternoon does not undo a marriage. But an afternoon can become a day, and a day can become a week. Eventually, two people who love each other can feel far apart without being able to name a single thing that went wrong.

That is what makes drift so easy to miss. A plant does not wilt the moment you forget to water it. For a while, it looks much as it did yesterday. By the time the neglect becomes visible, it has usually been going on for some time.

A flame needs a steady supply of oil. We cannot count on a Chanukah miracle every week. If we want the flame burning tonight, we need to put in the oil today.

This week, try attaching one small gesture of connection to each of the three times you stop to daven. Alongside the messages about groceries and schedules, send your wife a sentence simply because you are thinking of her. Thank her for something. Mention a moment you appreciated. A short text counts.

And if you realize you have barely connected since yesterday, reach out now.

You do not have to wait for the distance to become a problem before you close it.
