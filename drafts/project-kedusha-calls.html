The Tircha of Doing the Right Thing

Every family knows it should have better protection at home. Almost nobody knows where to start, and the gap between those two facts is where the damage happens.

“A fifteen-year-old called us sobbing. He had found things online he cannot unsee. I asked him where he was when it happened. He was in his father’s office. There was no filter on the computer.”

Mendy: DRAFT WORDING, for you to review

His father is a serious man, well known in his community, the kind of person other people come to for advice. He had not decided that an unfiltered computer in his office was acceptable. He had never decided anything about it at all. It was on a list, somewhere behind a hundred other things, and it stayed on the list.

That is not negligence. It is the most ordinary thing in the world, and it is how most of these stories actually begin.

Everyone Knows. Almost Nobody Knows Where to Start.

Ask a parent whether the devices in the house should be better protected and the answer is yes, immediately, without argument. Ask what they are going to do about it and the answer gets quiet.

Which filter? There are many, and they are not the same. Is the cheap one good enough, or is this the wrong place to save? What happens to the work laptop, the one running software that breaks the moment you lock it down? What about the office. The smart TV, the car. Who installs it. What do you say to a twelve-year-old who will be around it by the afternoon anyway.

By the third question it has stopped being a decision and become a project, and a project needs an evening nobody has. So it moves to next week, and next week it moves again.

Protecting a Jewish home should not be this much of a tircha. Right now it is.

The Answers Keep Changing

It would be easy to read all of that as a failure of will. It is not. These are the same people who will drive an hour for a shiur and take a second job to pay tuition. Effort is not what is missing.

What is missing is a target that holds still. New products appear that genuinely solve problems families have had for years. New technology arrives carrying risks nobody had heard of last winter. A father who sat down and worked this out properly two years ago may be running the wrong answer today, and have no way of knowing it.

What makes it worse is that the information already exists. Organizations filtering devices in dozens of cities. Seforim for a nine-year-old and for a chosson. Curriculums in Bais Yaakovs. Kosher phone standards worked out community by community. Hotlines, mentoring, sponsorship funds for families who cannot pay.

The problem is that it is scattered.

A filter that is the right recommendation in Adar quietly stops being the right one after an update nobody announced. A phone a kehilla accepted last year is not the one it accepts now. AI arrived and changed the question in a single season. Working out which of them is actually good, on which device, at what price, in which community and in which language, is close to full-time work.

“A printed page about technology is out of date by the time it is bound. Somebody has to be watching every week, or you are handing people last year’s advice.”

R Lazerson, Mishor: DRAFT WORDING, for him to review

The Half We Were Never Experts In

Guard Your Eyes has become the foremost address in the Jewish world for helping a Yid who is already struggling. Nineteen years of it, and a three-pillar program: motivation, planning and connection. Tens of thousands have used it, and behind that number is a quieter list of marriages rebuilt and fathers present again.

Where GYE was never the expert, until now, is the other half of the same problem. Helping a family that is not struggling at all protect a home and an office. Knowing which solution is genuinely the best one this month, for this situation, on this device.

That half is not a matter of chizuk or willpower. It is a research problem, and research is somebody else’s expertise.

“We were never only in the business of helping people who fell. The ambition was always that a family should not have to call us at all.”

Yaakov Nadel: DRAFT WORDING, for him to review

He started Guard Your Eyes nineteen years ago with Yechezkel Stelzer, under the guidance of Rabbi Dr. Abraham Twerski zt”l and, yibadel lechaim, Rav Aharon Feldman shlita. It was a daily email and a handful of mentors then. Prevention was in the plan from the beginning and kept losing to the emergencies, because a man on the phone at two in the morning comes before a father who does not have a problem yet.

So GYE went and found the people who do know. Project Kedusha is a partnership with Mishor in Lakewood, whose business is exactly this: which kosher phone a kehilla will accept this year, what is coming in six months, and what only looks like it works. GYE brings nineteen years of resources for those already struggling, teens and adults. Mishor keeps the technology current.

One Address, Three Ways In

The Kedusha Guide, in print, close to a hundred full-color pages: 230 vetted resources from dozens of organizations, across six sections. Safeguards is the largest, comparing every filter in America and in Eretz Yisroel, kosher phones by region, routers, accountability software for a man who must keep open internet, all seventy-plus TAG offices, and the devices people forget, the game console, the smart TV, the Tesla. Then Education, by age, from a book for a four-year-old to pre-marriage material. Chizuk. Struggles. Kosher Isle, which is what you put on instead. And Screen Addiction, which is not about content at all. It can also be printed at home.

Kedusha.com, the same library alive online. A father types one sentence in his own words. What answers him asks his son’s age, the device, what the family can spend, which community and which language, and only then narrows 230 resources down to the few that fit him. So the mother in Yerushalayim gets Israeli filters rather than American ones.

The Kedusha Hotline, 866-KEDUSHA, which answers the same way for a family with no internet at all. It is answering now.

None of the three is a snapshot. The Guide is a yearly edition, rebuilt each year against what actually changed. The site and the hotline are corrected continuously, with Mishor watching the technology week by week, so a recommendation that stops being right stops being given. That is what makes this a service rather than a publication.

“People start by apologizing for calling. They think their question is too small for us. It is never too small.”

Chaim, GYE hotline: DRAFT WORDING, for him to review

He is the one who sees the shape of it. Different countries, different kehillos, a bochur at two in the morning and a zeide asking on behalf of a grandson. A great many of the calls are not about getting out of trouble at all. They are about what to buy, what to install, and what to say at what age, and those were the ones that could not be answered on the spot. one call he remembers, in his words

Wherever the GYE badge appears, that resource is ours. Everything else is somebody else’s work, printed with their name on it. Rav Yosef Elefent of the Mir called it a game changer. Rav Elya Brudny shlita his own words and exact title: need from you.

An Evening You Do Not Have to Spend

Go back to the office with no filter on the computer.

Nothing in that story needed a program, a mentor or a crisis line. It needed somebody to know, on an ordinary Tuesday, which product to put on one machine, and to be able to find that out in ten minutes instead of losing a night to it.

That is the whole ambition. Every family in Klal Yisroel that wants to be protected should be able to find out how, quickly, and then get on with the rest of their life. Information should not be the barrier standing between a Yid and the solution he needs.

The Guide belongs on a shelf in the house, next to the benchers, where a child can see it and a guest can pick it up. Not because something went wrong. Because every family is already making these decisions, and until this year most of us made them alone, at eleven at night, guessing.
