The Calls We Were Never Built to Answer

Guard Your Eyes spent nineteen years helping Yidden who were already struggling. Then the phone started ringing with a different kind of question.

The call that changed things was not a crisis.

A father, somewhere in America, wanted to know whether there was an app that could limit how long his ten-year-old sat with a tablet. Not a filter. Not therapy. A time limit. He had been given the Guard Your Eyes number by somebody in shul, and he opened by apologizing, because he suspected he had the wrong place.

He did have the wrong place. And he had nowhere else to call.

That is the story of the last few years at GYE, told over and over in different accents. A rebbi in Flatbush with one talmid he cannot reach. A mother in Yerushalayim who has priced filtering four devices and cannot do it. A father whose fifteen-year-old left a search history open, who does not want a program, he wants to know what to say at breakfast. A chassidishe family with no internet in the house at all, who still has to pay a water bill and order a ride to a wedding. A man with a perfectly kosher phone who reaches for it every free minute and has begun to be frightened of himself.

Not one of those is what GYE was built for. GYE was built for the man who is already in trouble and has decided to climb out. These callers were not in trouble. They were making a decision, alone, at night, with no way to know whether the answer they landed on was any good.

Everything They Needed Already Existed

Here is the part that should be said plainly, because it is the whole point.

Klal Yisroel is not short of answers in this area. It is extraordinary how much has been built. There are organizations filtering devices in dozens of cities, seforim written for a nine-year-old and for a chosson, curriculums running in Bais Yaakovs, kosher phone standards worked out community by community, hotlines, mentoring programs, sponsorship funds for families who cannot pay. Almost every question that reaches GYE has already been answered properly by somebody, somewhere.

The father with the tablet did not need anything invented for him. He needed a person who knew where to look.

One Address, Three Ways In

What came out of it is Project Kedusha, and this Elul it reaches Klal Yisroel in three forms.

The Kedusha Guide, in print, close to a hundred full-color pages: 230 vetted resources from dozens of organizations, across six sections. Safeguards, the largest of them, comparing every filter in America and in Eretz Yisroel, kosher phones by region, routers, accountability software for a man who must keep open internet, all seventy-plus TAG offices, and the devices people forget, the Xbox, the smart TV, the Tesla. Then Education, by age, from a book for a four-year-old to pre-marriage material. Chizuk. Struggles. Kosher Isle, which is what you put on instead. And Screen Addiction, which is not about content at all. It is a yearly edition, revised each year, and it can also be printed at home.

Kedusha.com, the same library alive online. A father types one sentence in his own words. What answers him asks his son’s age, the device, what the family can spend, which community, which language, and only then narrows 230 resources to the few that actually fit him, and tells him how the conversation in the morning might sound. So the father with the work laptop is not handed the same answer as the father with a family iPad, and the mother in Yerushalayim gets Israeli filters, not American ones.

A phone number, 866-KEDUSHA, answered the same way, for a family with no internet to type into. It is answering now.

Wherever the GYE badge appears in the Guide, that resource is ours. Everything else is somebody else’s work, printed with their name on it. Rav Yosef Elefent of the Mir called it a game changer. Rav Elya Brudny shlita his own words and exact title: need from you.

The Barrier Was Never the Answers

There is a version of this article that lists what is in the Guide, and it would be an accurate article and nobody would finish it. The reason to read this one is the father who apologized before he asked his question.

He was not weak, or negligent, or in crisis. He was a normal father making a normal decision about a machine in his house, and he could not find out what the right decision was, so he guessed. Multiply him by every family you know.

Information should not be the thing standing between a Yid and the solution he needs. Not in this, of all subjects.

The Guide belongs on a shelf in the house, next to the benchers, where a child can see it and a guest can pick it up. Not because something went wrong. Because every family is already making these decisions, and until this year most of us made them alone.
