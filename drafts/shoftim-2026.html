Stop Him at the Next Gate

This week’s parsha opens: “Shoftim v’shotrim titen lecha b’chol she’arecha” - “Appoint judges and officers for yourself in all your gates” (Devarim 16:18). The seforei chassidus and mussar explain that a person has to guard every gate of his own body, every opening through which the outside world reaches him.


Guarding the eyes from what they shouldn’t see. The ears from kol isha and coarse talk. Even the nose, from the scent of a perfume (Shulchan Aruch, Even HaEzer 21:1).


All true, and all l’chatchila. But what do you do once the enemy is already through the gate?


Very often it feels like the thing is already lost. It is the logic of a blown diet: you slipped on one cookie, so you might as well finish the box. But finishing the box was never the consequence of the first cookie. It was a second decision, made separately.


Starting to fall doesn’t decide where you land.


That voice saying “here we go again, I know exactly how this ends” is not information. It’s a weakening thought, and left unchallenged, it tends to create the very outcome it predicts. It is not over until it’s over. Wherever you’re standing, there’s still a way out.


“וְדַע שֶׁיֵּשׁ פְּתָחִים הַרְבֵּה בַּחשֶׁךְ הַזֶּה לָצֵאת מִשָּׁם” - know that there are many openings in this darkness through which to leave it (Likutey Moharan 9).


Maybe that’s why the pasuk says b’chol she’arecha, in all your gates, and not just bishe’arecha, in your gates. Even once the enemy has breached one gate, there are still gates deeper in that can be held. One line has fallen. The next one is still yours to hold.


All of it with siyata dishmaya - “asher Hashem Elokecha nosen lach”, which Hashem your God is giving you. And from that comes “v’shaftu es ha’am mishpat tzedek” - they shall judge the people with righteous judgment: every force inside a person, brought back into line.


Elul zman starts in the yeshivos this week. Anyone who’s fought this fight for years knows the tiredness, the voice that says: “I know myself. Same story every year. I hold on through Elul, and it’s only a matter of time until the fall after Yom Kippur.”


That voice is the enemy at the outer gate. It doesn’t get to decide what happens at the next one.


This year can still be different.


WISDOM FROM THE FORUM

“How Do I Use the Elul Season to
Strengthen My Recovery?”

Fittingly for Shoftim, which always lands at the opening of Elul, this week’s question is about the season itself. For some, Elul is a tailwind. For others it is dread. The chevra has honest words for both.


Start with the hard part, because the forum doesn’t pretend it away. @hashemhelpme18 and @gettingthere9 both admit Elul can be “brutal,” bringing a kind of “Elul anxiety” where all the focus on cleaning up aveiros makes the guilt heavier and recovery harder. @elephant18 described “a panicky thing in the heart,” weighed down by past falls and broken promises. And @sturggle noticed that so many members hit a rough patch in Elul - “down and depressed, falling, tripping and slipping” - that it looks like a season of intensified attack. If Elul feels heavier to you rather than lighter, you are in very good company.


But the chevra’s answer to that heaviness is firm and consistent: the guilt is not the point of the season. @captain reminds everyone that even the days you fought and lost are “priceless gems” - you are spiritually wealthy after a fall, not bankrupt. The Day of Judgment is coming, but the dominant note of the month is closeness, not fear.


Inspiration alone doesn’t hold, though, and the forum is united on what turns it into something real: small action. @BenHashemBH puts it sharply - tefillah is essential, but we also have to “do our part” and “put in the work” to show we’re sincere. His advice is “small though significantly meaningful commitments” rather than big unrealistic promises, and his own is to reach out to a chaver before walking into a situation he knows will be hard. @once-innocent says the same from her side: do one small positive thing, or hold back from one passive slip, and bring that to Hashem as proof you are trying to turn things around. Small and real beats big and broken.


And @ezrac90876 named the worry underneath all of it: Elul makes overcoming desire feel easier, but the progress fades after Sukkos - so how do you turn a good Elul into a changed year? @ur-a-jew answered him plainly: without real internal change, the pattern of temporary sobriety just repeats itself. The seasonal boost is a gift, but it is scaffolding. The building is the inner work underneath it.


So: Elul isn’t a courtroom where you stand accused. If the season feels heavy, you are normal, and the guilt is not the goal. Lean into how close Hashem is right now, make one small commitment you will actually keep, and remember that the point isn’t a good few weeks - it’s the change that lets them last.

NEW SERIES · JOYFUL TESHUVA

The best way to use the month of Elul to reach freedom.

Elul is here, and for a lot of us it arrives as bad news - a month whose entire subject is everything we have not managed to fix. This new series is going to work through that, one idea at a time.


In honor of the month, we want to bring a few very inspiring ideas about Elul from the Lakewood Mashgiach, Rav Nosson Meir Wachtfogel zt’l. They are four separate ideas, not one argument. Each one is worth sitting with on its own.


  1. Elul, the way most of us hear it, is the month for going after what is wrong with us. The Mashgiach says, it’s the opposite: “עיקר העבודה באלול צריך להיות בעשה טוב דוקא, ולא בסור מרע!” - the main avoda of Elul has to be in aseh tov specifically, and not in sur me’ra. In these days of רצון ורחמים, he explained, נפתח המוח ומתרחב הלב - the mind opens and the heart widens. Elul is the month a person can carry more than he usually can. So the way you use it is by adding.
  2. He put one condition on the adding, and it is the reason the whole approach either works or does not: “מתיקות בלימוד, אחרת האדם נהפך למתאוננים! ומחפש הנאות במקום אחר חלילה” - there has to be sweetness in the learning. Otherwise a person turns into a complainer, and goes looking for pleasure somewhere else, chas v’shalom.

    [Rav Michel Yehudah Lefkowitz zt’l heard the same thing in a single word of the Gemara. Hashem created the yetzer hara and created the Torah as a תבלין for it - a spice. Not a רפואה, not a cure. A cure works whether or not you enjoy swallowing it; a spice only does its job if there is taste in it. Learning without sweetness, he said, does not protect a person from the yetzer hara at all. And Chazal’s advice for enjoying your learning is to learn what your heart desires, in the place your heart desires.]
  3. What we are up against is not appetite. It is humiliation. “עיקר כח היצר להשפיל את האדם, ולשכח ממנו את מעלתו שהוא בן מלך” - the yetzer’s main power is to bring a person low and make him forget his own madreiga, that he is a ben melech. That is how a person comes to do things that do not belong to a ben melech at all. He brings Yosef HaTzaddik at the hardest nisayon of his life, whose answer was “לי יש שייכות לאבות” - I am connected to the Avos; what would I have to do with what you are describing. And then the conclusion: “וכשנזכר ומרגיש שהוא בן מלך, ממילא מתנהג אחרת!” - when a person remembers and feels that he is a ben melech, he behaves differently on his own. That, said the Mashgiach, is the avoda of אני לדודי ודודי לי.
  4. When people asked him outright what the derech of avoda for Elul should be, he said he had no derech to hand them: “אין דרך מסוימת ברורה לכולם, אלא כל אחד צריך לעמול” - there is no one clear derech for everybody; each person has to work his own out. Only a kabbala from his rebbeim: whoever works and exerts himself and sweats, and does not stop, will get somewhere in the end. Nobody arrives on yichus. A person’s whole madreiga is set by how much he toils. He would say it in Yiddish - “א טראפען שוייס ווערט ניט פארלאָרן”, a drop of sweat is never lost.

We think all four are especially relevant to GYE members - more than they may look at first. Here is how each one lands for a man who is still in the middle of this fight.


The first is close to our whole approach. Adding good, rather than going after what is wrong, is what we call the green circle: deliberately filling your day with productive, positive things. That is not a pleasant extra alongside the real work, it is one of the strongest defenses there is. A day with structure in it, and something good in the gaps, is a day that is much harder to fall in. And since there is no one derech for everybody, the circle is yours to build. For one man it is the morning: up on time, a seder that starts when it says it starts. For another it is the night, where the trouble usually is, and what he needs is a fixed routine and an hour when the phone is somewhere else. In yeshiva it is mostly the sedarim. Outside yeshiva it can be a set learning time, work you are moving forward in, exercise, a hobby you genuinely enjoy. That last one is not a compromise. A man with good things in his life is a much harder target.


The second is not a refinement for us, it is the diagnosis. A person going to look for pleasure somewhere else is our own story, told in advance. A life with nothing enjoyable in it will go looking for enjoyment, and it will find the worst version available. Which makes sweetness an instruction rather than a mood: pick the sefer you actually want to open, and ask for it - והערב נא, every morning, is a request that the Torah be sweet in our mouths.


The third is the one we would underline hardest. Anyone who has been in this fight a while knows how low it brings him, and knows that most of the falling happens after he has already written himself off. The Mashgiach is saying that the lowering is not the damage afterwards - it is the weapon itself. Which turns remembering who you are into avoda rather than comfort.


And it helps to know where the King is standing this month. All year He is in the palace, and getting in takes permission, protocol, and a reason to be there. In Elul, in the Baal HaTanya’s mashal, the King is out in the field - among ordinary people, on their ground, where anyone who wants to come over can come over, in his work clothes, with no appointment, and He receives every one of them with a smile. You do not have to be cleaned up first in order to come over. That is why אני לדודי is not a slogan for the season. It is a reminder of who you are, in the month the King came out to the field to be with you.


The fourth is for a bad week. A man who has fallen many times can be very sure that all the trying added up to nothing, and there is usually no result on the day itself to argue with him. A drop of sweat is never lost. The toil is the madreiga.


ONE THING TO ADD THIS WEEK

Pick one thing to increase, and only one. Size it so a bad day cannot break it: five minutes of learning you actually enjoy, one seder you do not miss, one hour at night that has a plan in it. Not something to stop. Something to add.


The Mashgiach’s words are from Likut Reshimos b’Inyanei Elul v’Yamim Noraim (5766). Rav Michel Yehudah Lefkowitz on tavlin is cited in Yechezkel Leiman, The Heart of a Nation (Feldheim, 2018), p. 77, on Bava Basra 16a. The King in the field is from the Baal HaTanya, Likutei Torah, Parshas Re’eh.

7 KEYS TO A STRONGER MARRIAGE · KEY #1

Know Your Spouse

Last series we went through ten “really dumb mistakes” that smart couples make - the things to stop doing. This new series is the flip side: seven positive, buildable habits that the strongest marriages share. They come from Dr. John Gottman, who spent decades in his lab studying thousands of couples, watching minute by minute what separated the ones who lasted from the ones who came apart. What is striking, for us, is how much of what he found the Torah said first, and said better. We will take his practical tools and set them on their proper foundation.


He calls the first one building a “love map.” We’ll call it what it really is: knowing your spouse.


Knowing is the Torah’s word for closeness


When the Torah describes the deepest bond between husband and wife, it reaches for a surprising word. Not “loved.” Not “was close with.” “V’ha’adam yada es Chava ishto” - “and the man knew his wife” (Bereishis 4:1). In lashon hakodesh, daas, knowing, is the very word for intimacy and connection. That is not an accident of language. Real closeness is a form of knowing, and to truly love someone is to truly know them.


And knowing, in the Torah’s sense, is not a collection of external facts. There is a kind of knowledge that gives you only the surface - image and data. Real daas is inner: a person’s heart, what moves them, what worries them, what they hope for. You can live in the same house as someone for years, know a thousand facts about them, and barely know them at all.


What Gottman found


This is exactly what Gottman saw in his lab, phrased in the language of a scientist. The couples with the strongest marriages carried around a detailed “map” of each other’s inner world. They knew the small things: their spouse’s favorite way to unwind, the name of the coworker who has been driving them crazy, the dream they have half given up on, the worry sitting on them this week. When life got hard, they weren’t navigating a stranger. They were navigating someone they knew.


The couples who struggled had let their maps go blank. They had stopped updating - they still knew who their spouse was five years ago, but not who they had become. And Gottman found that this quiet drifting-apart-through-not-knowing showed up early, and hurt most when a big change arrived, like a new baby: the couples who no longer really knew each other lost their closeness the fastest.


Knowing takes renewal


Here is the part that matters most, and it is a Torah idea to its core: knowing your spouse is not a one-time achievement. You did not “get to know” your wife before the wedding and complete the task. A person is not a static thing. Your wife today carries worries, hopes, small victories and small hurts that did not exist a year ago. Real daas is a continuous pursuit, an ongoing turning-toward the other to keep learning who she is becoming. The moment you assume you already know everything about your spouse is the moment your knowledge starts going stale.


Which is a deeply hopeful thing. It means that however long you have been married, there is always more of your spouse to discover, and the discovering itself is a form of love. Some of the warmest moments in a long marriage come from a husband learning something about his wife of thirty years that genuinely surprises him - and letting her see how much it meant to him that he wanted to know.


Your Action Steps This Week


  1. Take an honest inventory. Without asking, see if you can name right now: your wife’s biggest current stress, something she is looking forward to, and one hope she has mentioned lately. Notice where the map has gone blank. That isn’t a failure - it is just where this week’s work is.
  2. Ask, and actually listen. Pick one real question this week that isn’t about logistics. Not “did you call the plumber,” but “what’s been on your mind lately?” Then listen to learn, not to reply. You are updating the map.
  3. Keep the map current. Choose one small ongoing way to stay in touch with her inner world - a few minutes at the end of the day to hear how it really went, or a standing question you ask each week. The goal isn’t a grand conversation. It is the steady, quiet habit of continuing to know the person you married.
