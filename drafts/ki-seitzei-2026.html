PARSHAS KI SEITZEI

The Ongoing Battle Is Why You’re Here

You go out to war against the yetzer hara, and you win. You hold the line, you string together a real stretch. And then a few days later it is back, and you have to fight the whole war over again. The Tiferes Shlomo calls this a great wonder. We already fought this war. We already beat it. That should have been enough. So why does the yetzer come storming back, and where does it end?


The answer is in the first word. “Ki seitzei lamilchama al oyvecha,” when you go out to war against your enemy (Devarim 21:10). The Tiferes Shlomo reads seitzei, “you go out,” as your going out into this world, and says the whole purpose of that going out is the war itself. You did not come down to win once and retire. You came down to fight, and that is what you were created for. The ongoing battle is not evidence that something went wrong. It is the reason you are here.


His proof is Yaakov Avinu, who wished to settle in peace and was answered: is it not enough for the tzaddikim that the World to Come is prepared for them, that they seek quiet in this world too? Then came the ordeal of Yosef. Yaakov, in seeking peace, was preparing to receive his portion in the next world, but his time to leave had not come. Had the battle ended, his work here would have ended with it. The renewed struggle was not a punishment. It was what kept him here.


He finds the same in the last days of Dovid HaMelech. Dovid had conquered his yetzer completely, “v’libi chalal b’kirbi,” my heart is empty within me, which Chazal read as having killed it outright. Then the pasuk says they covered him with garments and he could not get warm. His servants understood what that meant, and arranged a fresh battle for him, so that he would have a new war to wage and stay here longer.


Hear what that does to the most demoralizing sentence in this struggle: it came back, so clearly I am getting nowhere. A returning nisayon does not mean you lost the last one. It means you are still in the middle of the exact avodah your neshama came down to do. Read him carefully, though. He is promising a lifetime of battle, not a lifetime of falling. The war staying open is your purpose. The falls are what you are working your way out of.


This is also why the parshiyos sit next to each other. Shoftim ends with “you shall do what is just and good in the eyes of Hashem,” and this one opens, “when you go out to war.” The Tiferes Shlomo reads them as one sentence: the straight path in Hashem’s eyes is to keep going out to war against the yetzer, all the days of your life. So when the battle comes back and a voice tells you it should have been over by now, answer it with the parsha. It was never supposed to be over.



Tiferes Shlomo, Parshas Ki Seitzei.

MEMBER STORY

A Total 360

By @puppy (Current streak: 418 days)


Since I’ve been clean of P&M I feel so much more pleasant at home, with my wife and my kids. I’ve realized that my behavior used to make me depressed, and I didn’t even know it. I couldn’t have told you whether I was happy or not.


When I started my clean streak, I saw that the depression, the lack of happiness, was just gone. I’m so grateful to Hashem that He brought me to GYE, a place where people care about me, where I get inspired by others, where I’ve learned so much about life in general.


It’s amazing to look back at where I came from and where I’m standing now. That person from a year ago feels far away. I made a complete 360, from running after P&M to someone who is here, in the moment, smiling, connecting to friends, wanting to be around people. When I was in the mud, I didn’t even like going out with friends the way I do now. No words can really describe the transformation. I could write all day and all night about how different I am.


I never dreamed there were so many people out there like me, until I joined this amazing family, and now I don’t feel alone anymore. When you know there are others out there struggling too, everything shifts.


And now, when I have a temptation to go looking for content that isn’t the best (not porn, but the kind of thing that can lead there), I remind myself of everything I just wrote. So please, puppy, do yourself a favor: relax, take a deep breath. Before you go anywhere that could lead somewhere worse, stop. Take a minute and think. What am I about to do, and where can it lead? What do I really want? Where have I been heading this whole time? Who am I really? I think that after that, I’ll choose the right thing.


So this is a reminder for me. Enjoy, my dear friends, and have a great day!


WISDOM FROM THE FORUM

“How Do I Actually Win When
the Urge Hits Hardest?”

Fittingly for Ki Seitzei, “when you go out to war against your enemy,” this week’s question is about the battle at its fiercest: the moment the urge crashes over you and your willpower is running on empty. The chevra has been in that exact moment countless times, and they’ve brought back real, in-the-trenches tactics. Here’s what works.


One of the most powerful approaches members describe is “urge surfing.” Instead of wrestling the urge head-on, you acknowledge it and let it pass like a wave. The key insight is understanding what the urge actually is: your mind is looking for an escape from some pain, stress, loneliness, boredom, and it’s offering the urge as a false promise of relief. Once you see that clearly, you don’t have to fight it so much as outlast it. The idea is to sit back, be aware of what’s going on, and then just let the wave decrease on its own. That means not fantasizing, not touching, just observing the urge without feeding it, and watching it crest and fall.


When surfing isn’t enough, members turn to distraction and what @musicman calls “guerilla tactics.” When the urge is strong and you’re alone, simply getting up and redirecting yourself works, “distract myself, it’s working for the most part,” he wrote. This is especially valuable exactly when willpower is lowest, because it doesn’t rely on winning a staring contest with the urge; it just removes you from the battlefield.


Self-talk is another crucial weapon, and @shteeble offers a whole script to run through when the urge hits. A few of his lines: “You don’t need it.” “If you give in, you may feel better now, but you won’t improve your life situation in the slightest way.” “Think about how much you love your wife, and how you don’t want to damage that love.” “Love yourself. Have pity on yourself. Don’t let yourself sink.” And: “Think of a high-priority task you could accomplish by not wasting your time on lust.” Having words ready before the moment means you’re not trying to compose them mid-battle. A simpler version comes from @sunnycheetah84, who just tells himself he’ll do it “tomorrow”, a gentle delay that lets the wave pass without a head-on refusal.


Several members stress that winning the moment starts before the moment, by knowing your triggers. @doingbetter realized that financial stress was a huge driver for him, with the urge showing up as a distraction from the pressure, and @hashem help me suggested finding other de-stressors, like exercise, to meet the real need instead of acting out. If you know why the urge tends to come, you can head it off before it arrives at full strength.


And when the moment feels too big to conquer, shrink it. The ODAAT principle, one day at a time, is a lifeline here; @musicman describes taking it “one day at a time, like I should.” But @bardichev offers an even smaller unit when a whole day feels impossible, asking a struggling member, “Can you go fifteen minutes at a time?” You don’t have to win forever. You have to win the next fifteen minutes, and then the next.


Finally, two reminders the chevra returns to. First, be kind to yourself in the fight. As @some-guy was told by his Rabbi, “it is natural to have these urges, and I am better for just trying to stop”, and getting depressed after a slip only fuels the next one, so don’t pile self-hatred onto the battle. Second, you’re not fighting alone. @i-man feels that “chizuk from my brothers in arms gives me additional ammo in this battle of good vs. evil,” and @bardichev reassured a member in a hard moment: “You won’t fall. I am here for you, and we have a huuuuge family that is here for you.” Sometimes the winning move is simply reaching out before you act.


The bottom line? “Ki seitzei lamilchama”, you’re going to war, and the pasuk promises “Hashem will deliver them into your hand.” Your job is to bring the weapons: surf the wave instead of fighting it, distract and redirect when willpower is low, keep a self-talk script ready, know your triggers, shrink the battle down to fifteen minutes if you have to, stay gentle with yourself, and never fight alone. Do your part, and trust that the victory comes with Hashem’s help.

JOYFUL TESHUVA · PART 2

What Teshuva Is When You Haven’t Stopped Yet

We are in Elul, with Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur ahead. For a GYE member the hard question is not whether he should do teshuva. It is whether he can, on something he has not managed to stop yet.


Start with the situation as it actually is. You want to stop. You have wanted to stop for a long time. You have tried things, some of them worked for a while, and you are still going up and down with no clear view yet of the road that gets you out of this for good. You are in the middle of figuring it out. So when Elul arrives and brings the word teshuva with it, there is a fair question underneath: how does a person do teshuva on something he is still in the middle of? You are meant to regret it and resolve never to return to it. You genuinely wish you never would. You also know you are not out of the woods. So what are you actually doing when you say you are doing teshuva?


Rabbeinu Yona takes up this exact case, and that by itself is worth knowing. He has just written about a man who fell in a moment when the yetzer overpowered him. Then he turns to somebody else: “האיש המתיצב על דרך לא טוב תמיד”, the man who is standing on a bad road all the time, who returns to it day after day and has repeated it many times over. For him, Rabbeinu Yona does not begin where he began before. “ראשית תשובת האיש הזה, לעזוב דרכו ומחשבתו הרעה” - the beginning of this man’s teshuva is to leave his way and his thinking. Leaving is his first step.


That is not a lowered standard, and it is worth being precise about why. The other parts of teshuva are not cancelled for you. They are real, they are coming, and they should be done. What changes, for a pattern this ingrained, is the order. You are not ready for those layers yet, and nothing is gained by forcing them. You start on the ground floor, on the leaving itself, and the rest gets built on top of it later.


Which makes this month practical rather than emotional, and here is the part worth being blunt about: working on stopping is not something that happens in your head. It is not deciding again, more sincerely this time. It is not feeling worse about it in shul on Rosh Hashanah. It is what you do differently on an ordinary Tuesday night. Which hour of the day the trouble lives in, and what is different about that hour. Which device, in which room, with what on it. Who you call before instead of after. What goes into the gap when the boredom shows up. You try one thing, watch it fail, try the next one, and treat what went wrong as information rather than as a verdict on yourself.


That work is not preparation for teshuva. That is the teshuva. The line that matters is not between a man who has stopped and a man who has not. It is between a man actively working on this, trying things, adjusting, learning what works for him, and a man who has quit working on it, who is coasting, or resigned, or waiting to become a different person. If you are in the first group, you are doing the thing being asked of you. Not a smaller version of it.


And notice what is not on that list. Producing the guilt is not the first step, and it is not the measure of whether you are serious. For this struggle in particular, leaning too hard on the past tends to backfire, and we will come back to that later in this series.


So there are two people reading this. If you are actively working on this in reality, then you are already doing it, and Elul is for sharpening what you have. If you have not been doing anything about it, this is the month to start. Not with a resolution about the rest of your life. With one thing you are going to actually do this week.


ONE STEP TO TAKE THIS WEEK

Pick the hour of the week that is hardest for you. You already know which one it is.

Then decide now, in advance, what happens in that hour instead. Something real: where the phone is, who you are with, what you are doing. Small enough that a bad day cannot cancel it.

If it does not work, you have not failed the month. You have found out which thing to try next. That is the work, and that is the teshuva.


Rabbeinu Yona, Shaarei Teshuva, Shaar Rishon, os 11.

7 KEYS TO A STRONGER MARRIAGE · KEY #2

Nurture Fondness and Admiration

Adapted from the research of Dr. John Gottman


Last week we built the foundation: knowing your spouse. This week we add the thing that keeps a marriage warm, actively holding on to what you admire about the person you married.


Gottman found that in the strongest marriages, husbands and wives kept a living sense of fondness and admiration for each other, a genuine, felt appreciation of their spouse’s good qualities. And he found that this fondness was the single greatest protector against contempt, the most corrosive force in a marriage. You cannot sneer at someone and cherish them at the same time. Where admiration is alive, contempt can’t take root.


Here’s the striking part of his research: he could gauge the health of a marriage just by how a couple remembered their past. Couples who spoke warmly about how they met, the early days, what drew them together, tended to be solid, even if the present was rocky. Couples who had rewritten their history in a sour key, who could barely recall what they ever saw in each other, were usually in trouble. The fondness, or its absence, colored everything.


The whole thrust of the mitzvah to judge others favorably, dan l’kaf zechus, and to cultivate an ayin tova, a good eye, is that we largely see what we train ourselves to look for. Two people can look at the very same spouse: one sees a list of faults, the other sees a partner doing their best under real pressure. Same spouse, different eye. The Torah asks us to build the good eye deliberately, because the eye we use becomes the marriage we live in.


And this connects directly to where our last marriage series ended, on the tenth mistake, criticism. Criticism and admiration are two lenses pointed at the same person. A mind trained to catch what’s wrong will always find it, and will miss everything being done right. Nurturing fondness is simply choosing, on purpose, to aim the lens the other way.


It’s a practice, not a feeling


The key word in Gottman’s finding is nurture. Fondness isn’t something you either have or don’t; it’s something you feed or starve. In a long marriage, the warm feelings don’t evaporate because the spouse became unlovable. They fade because nobody watered them. The repair is not to wait for the feeling to return, but to actively rehearse the good: to call to mind, and say out loud, what you appreciate. The feeling follows the practice, not the other way around.


This is deeply hopeful for a couple in a hard stretch. It means you are never as far from warmth as you feel. The affection isn’t gone; it’s just under-watered. And the moment you start naming what’s good, out loud, to your spouse, you begin to feel it again.


Your Action Steps This Week


  1. Name three things you admire. Write down three real qualities you genuinely respect in your spouse, and for each one, recall a specific moment they showed it. Specifics turn a vague compliment into something you actually feel.
  2. Say one out loud. Once this week, tell your spouse something you appreciate about who they are, not what they did for you. “I really admire how patient you are with the kids” lands differently than “thanks for watching the kids.”
  3. Guard your inner monologue. Catch yourself the next time your mind starts building its “case” against your spouse. That’s the contempt lens. Deliberately swap it: name one thing they got right today. You’re training the good eye.
