PARSHAS NITZAVIM-VAYEILECH

You Don’t Stand There Alone

“Atem nitzavim hayom kulchem lifnei Hashem Elokeichem”, “You are standing today, all of you, before Hashem your G-d.” (Devarim 29:9)


Nitzavim is read right before Rosh Hashana, and the Nesivos Shalom notices that the pasuk stresses kulchem, all of you together. Why does it matter that we stand before Hashem as one?


He explains that there are two ways to approach Hashem: through din, strict judgment, or through rachamim, mercy. Rosh Hashana is the day of judgment, so how do we awaken mercy? Through the koach harabbim, the power of the group. A father’s mercy is aroused most when his children come to him together.


An individual Jew is called Hashem’s child as part of klal Yisrael. When a person binds himself to other Jews, the words “banim atem laHashem,” you are children to Hashem, rest fully on him, and a father’s mercy follows. This, the Nesivos Shalom says, is why Rabbi Akiva called “v’ahavta l’re’acha kamocha” the great principle of the Torah: loving your fellow Jew is how each person joins the group and draws on its strength.


Then he brings the line that reaches the hardest case of all. Quoting the Rebbe of Karlin, he teaches that even a Jew who has fallen in the worst way, who afterward feels he cannot stand and daven, feels he has no face to turn to Hashem, can still reach Him through the koach harabbim. By joining himself to the tzibbur, he stands before Hashem carried by the whole. A Jew is never completely pushed away, and that truth becomes available through the group, because when Jews join together, they are as one.


Before we apply that, it is worth being clear about who we are talking to. The men reading this learn, daven, raise families, give tzedaka, and are careful with mitzvos that plenty of people never think twice about. They are struggling in one specific area, and it happens to be one of the hardest there is. A person is not defined by his record in his hardest battle. And even inside this battle there are real wins: the day you got up after falling, the hour you held out, the call you made instead of giving in. Those count before Hashem, and they may be the most valuable thing any of us brings to Rosh Hashana.


So the koach harabbim is not a fallback for people with nothing of their own. It is there for the days when shame insists you have nothing of your own. On those days, there is something underneath you that doesn’t depend on how the week went. Atem nitzavim hayom kulchem: you stand as part of all of us, and as part of the whole you are a child, and a father’s mercy reaches you regardless.


This is why the connection at the heart of GYE matters so much. Someone once pointed out that there may be no other place where Jews from so many different communities engage with each other this closely, and about something this private. Men who would otherwise never meet are holding each other up and asking nothing in return. That is ahavas Yisrael in its plainest form, kulchem.


So as Rosh Hashana comes, bring what you have, and bring it without apology, your Torah, your mitzvos, your wins in the hardest place. And bring the strength of the whole along with it: Atem. You, standing with klal Yisrael as a child before a Father whose mercy was never in question. No Jew is ever pushed away entirely.


May the caring between members, the volunteering, and the chizuk given by people with nothing to gain awaken that mercy on this whole community and on klal Yisrael, and may we all be written and sealed for a good and sweet year. Kesiva vechasima tova.

7 KEYS TO A STRONGER MARRIAGE · KEY #4

Let Your Spouse Influence You

Adapted from the research of Dr. John Gottman


The last three keys were about knowing your spouse, admiring them, and turning toward them in the small moments. This week’s key is about something harder for many of us, especially for husbands: actually letting your spouse’s opinion move you.


Gottman found one of the most striking predictors of a marriage’s future in a single question: does the husband accept his wife’s influence? In his research, marriages where the husband was unwilling to share power, to let his wife’s views genuinely affect his decisions, were vastly more likely to fail. And the ones where he did let her influence him were far more likely to thrive. Note the direction: Gottman found that wives generally already accept their husbands’ influence; the piece that’s often missing, and that makes the biggest difference, is the husband learning to yield.


Now, “accepting influence” doesn’t mean losing yourself or agreeing with everything. It means treating your spouse as a genuine partner whose perspective has real weight, so that when they raise a concern or want something, your instinct is to consider it and look for the yes, rather than to defend, dismiss, or dig in. It’s the difference between “here’s why you’re wrong” and “help me understand, you might be seeing something I’m not.”


The Torah’s picture of the husband who listens


This is old wisdom. The Gemara teaches, “if your wife is short, bend down and take counsel with her”, lower yourself to hear her perspective, because her insight is worth having. Chazal held up the man who honors his wife’s opinion, not the one who overrides it. In fact, the Gemara famously says a man should be careful with his wife’s honor, because blessing, in wealth and in the home, comes to a household on her account. The Torah’s vision of a strong husband is not the one who wins every argument. It’s the one secure enough to be moved.


And notice the Gemara’s balance, because Gottman found the same thing. The same passage that says “bend down and seek her counsel” also cautions against following blindly and thoughtlessly. Accepting influence isn’t surrendering your judgment; it’s genuinely weighing your spouse’s, so the decision becomes ours rather than a contest of wills. Two partners looking for common ground, not a winner and a loser.


Yielding is strength, not weakness


There’s a reason Chazal praised the one who is ma’avir al midosav, who can let go, bend, and not need to be right. The person who insists on winning every disagreement doesn’t actually have a strong marriage; he has a scoreboard. The one who can say “you make a good point, let’s do it your way” has something far stronger: a partner who feels genuinely heard, and who becomes, in turn, far more eager to hear him. Influence flows both ways once someone is brave enough to let it flow at all.


Your Action Steps This Week


  1. Find the yes. The next time your spouse raises something you’d instinctively push back on, pause and look for the part you can agree with. Start your response with that: “You’re right that…” It changes the whole conversation.
  2. Ask instead of defend. When you feel yourself getting defensive, replace the defense with a question: “Help me understand how you’re seeing this.” Then actually listen for the thing they might be right about.
  3. Yield on one real thing. Pick one genuine disagreement this week and let your spouse’s view carry the day, not as a concession you resent, but as a partner choosing “us.” Notice what it does to the temperature between you.

JOYFUL TESHUVA · PART 4

Who Told You There Is No Tikkun?

So far in this series we have been talking to a person who is trying. This week is about the one who stopped, and about the sentence that stopped him.


Somewhere along the way he heard that for this particular aveira there is no tikkun. Maybe he read it in a footnote. More often he heard it from someone frum, someone he had every reason to trust.


The seforim take that claim up directly, and they are not gentle about it.


1. The people who say it are the ones causing it


The Midrash Talpiyos, from R’ Eliyahu HaKohen, the baal Shevet Mussar, writes about the frum people who go around saying there is no tikkun for this. His verdict is that they are the ones producing the aveira, that they are not chassidim but chaseirim, men who are missing something, and that their punishment is heavier than the punishment of the people they are talking about.


אלו החסידים המחמירים לומר אין תיקון לזרע לבטלה, כאשר שמעתי מפיהם - הם המרבים פושעים בישראל, ומרחקים לבני אדם מתחת כנפי השכינה. שכל מי שיש בו עון זה ושומע מפיהם שאין לו תקנה, הולך ועושה מה שלבו חפץ, באומרו וכאשר אבדתי אבדתי. ואלו הגורמים כך - אינן חסידים כי אם חסרים, וענשם כבד משלהם.


These chassidim who are strict to say that there is no tikkun for zera levatala, as I have heard from their own mouths - they are the ones who multiply sinners in Israel, and they push people away from under the wings of the Shechina. Because anyone who has this sin, and hears from them that he has no remedy, goes and does whatever his heart desires, saying, I am lost anyway. And those who cause this are not chassidim but chaseirim, and their punishment is heavier than theirs.


Read the order of his words. The man hears there is no remedy, and then he goes and does whatever he wants. The despair came first and the behavior followed it. That is a description, written hundreds of years ago, of the person who writes to us.


2. It is not one opinion


The Shelah HaKadosh takes it up at length in Shaar HaOsiyos and shows that nothing stands in the way of teshuva even for this. The Keser Rosh, from R’ Chaim Volozhiner, arrives at the identical ruling through a completely separate chain, its note quoting the Shnei Luchos HaBris on the Zohar in almost the same words: they are the ones who multiply sinners in Israel, and they are called not chassidim but chaseirim. Two chains that do not depend on each other, one verdict.


R’ Tzadok HaKohen of Lublin is blunter still. אין יאוש כלל אצל איש יהודי - there is no despair at all for a Jew - and he says it specifically about a sin that Chazal described as one teshuva does not help for. Even the harshest reading of the aveira does not produce a heter to give up.


R’ Nachman says the whole thing in one line. אם אתה מאמין שיכולין לקלקל, תאמין שיכולין לתקן - if you believe a person can ruin, believe a person can repair. A man who is completely certain about the damage he has done is being asked to be equally certain about the other half of the same sentence.


Midrash Talpiyos, anaf zera levatala, os 7. Shelah HaKadosh, Shaar HaOsiyos, Kedushas HaZivug. Keser Rosh 133 with the note of Ohalei Chaim 58. Divrei Sofrim, os 16. Likutey Moharan II:112.



3. What the tikkun actually looks like


A person who has been told there is no tikkun is usually picturing the tikkun as one enormous act he will never manage. The Steipler describes something much closer to the ground. Every single time a person stands up to this and does not fall, he draws back a large part of what was taken from him, and over time the damage can be undone. Not at the end of the process. Then, that time.


So the tikkun is not waiting somewhere for a version of him that does not exist yet. It is the ordinary Tuesday afternoon where nothing happened. That is the whole thing, arriving in installments.


Kereina D’igrasa vol. 1, 106-107.



4. The danger is not the fall


R’ Yeruchom Levovitz, quoted by the Lakewood Mashgiach on the same page we quoted last week, reads this into Hashem’s warning to Kayin:


...אך יש חולשה גדולה לבני אדם להשאר בתוך הבוץ - בלוטע, וזה סכנה עצומה, וזו אזהרת ה’ לקין אם תיטיב, אם תרצה לצאת מהבוץ של חטא, שאת תתרומם ותתקדם הלאה, אל תחשוב על העבר, תשכח ממנו ותצא מהבוץ ותמשיך הלאה, ואם לאו, הסכנה עצומה לפתח חטאת רובץ ח”ו.


There is a great weakness in people, to stay inside the mud. That is an enormous danger, and it is Hashem’s warning to Kayin. Im teitiv, if you want out of the mud of the sin, s’eis, lift yourself and move forward, do not think about the past, forget it, get out of the mud and keep going. And if not, the danger is enormous, lapesach chatas roveitz.


Put that next to the Midrash Talpiyos and the two of them are describing one thing. The fall put him in the mud. What keeps him sitting there is being told there is no way out, and believing it.


5. The only thing that disqualifies


The Mashgiach is quoted saying it in a line:


אפשר ליפול אלף פעמים ועדיין נשאר צדיק... אדם שנופל תמיד הוא לא מופרך. מה מופרך? כשמתייאש חס ושלום.


A person can fall a thousand times and still remain a tzaddik. Someone who keeps falling is not disqualified. What disqualifies? Giving up, chas veshalom.


Bottom line: when the thought shows up that there is no tikkun for this, it is worth knowing whose side that thought is on. The baal Shevet Mussar already told us what it does to a person, and what he thought of the people who hand it over. You are not obligated to take a psak on your own future from a footnote, or from a well-meaning person who is not your rov. And what the Steipler and the Mashgiach are both asking for is far smaller than a tikkun: get out of the mud today, and keep going.


Likut Reshimos, printed page קט, quoting R’ Yeruchom Levovitz. The closing line is quoted in Rav Wachtfogel’s name in Bnesivos HaHalacha.
