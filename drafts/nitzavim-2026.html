PARSHAS NITZAVIM-VAYEILECH

You Don’t Stand There Alone

“Atem nitzavim hayom kulchem lifnei Hashem Elokeichem”, “You are standing today, all of you, before Hashem your G-d.” (Devarim 29:9)


Nitzavim is read right before Rosh Hashana, and the Nesivos Shalom notices that the pasuk stresses kulchem, all of you together. Why does it matter that we stand before Hashem as one?


He explains that there are two ways to approach Hashem: through din, strict judgment, or through rachamim, mercy. Rosh Hashana is the day of judgment, so how do we awaken mercy? Through the koach harabbim, the power of the group. A father’s mercy is aroused most when his children come to him together.


And an individual Jew is called Hashem’s child specifically as part of klal Yisrael. When a person binds himself to other Jews, the words “banim atem laHashem,” you are children to Hashem, rest fully on him, and a father’s mercy follows. This, the Nesivos Shalom says, is why Rabbi Akiva called “v’ahavta l’re’acha kamocha” the great principle of the Torah: loving your fellow Jew is how each person joins the group and draws on its strength.


Then he brings the line that speaks straight to our struggle. Quoting the Rebbe of Karlin, he teaches that even a Jew who has fallen in the worst way, and afterward feels he cannot stand and daven, feels he has no face to turn to Hashem, can still reach Him, through the koach harabbim. By joining himself to the tzibbur, he stands before Hashem carried by the whole. Because a Jew is never completely pushed away from Hashem, and that truth becomes available specifically through the group, since when Jews join together, they are as one.


Here is what that means for us.


There are days you feel you have no standing before Hashem. You’ve fallen again, and the shame says that you, of all people, have no right to turn to Him. On your own, you feel unable to stand before the King.


Nitzavim’s answer is that you don’t stand on your own. Atem nitzavim hayom kulchem, you stand as part of all of us, together. And as part of the whole, you are a child, and a father’s mercy reaches you no matter what you’ve done. The one who feels he has no face of his own is carried by the tzibbur.


This is why the connection at the heart of GYE matters so much. It was never only about encouragement. Binding yourself to others is the actual channel through which even a person who has fallen reaches Hashem. When you post on the forum, reach out to a chaver, or refuse to fight alone, you’re joining the kulchem, and through it standing before Hashem as a child, even on a day you feel unworthy.


So as Rosh Hashana comes and the shame says you have no place before the King, answer it with the first word of the parsha: Atem. You. Together with klal Yisrael, you stand as a child before a Father whose mercy was never in question. No Jew is ever pushed away entirely.

7 KEYS TO A STRONGER MARRIAGE · KEY #4

Let Your Spouse Influence You

Adapted from the research of Dr. John Gottman


The last three keys were about knowing your spouse, admiring them, and turning toward them in the small moments. This week’s key is about something harder for many of us, especially for husbands: actually letting your spouse’s opinion move you.


Gottman found one of the most striking predictors of a marriage’s future in a single question: does the husband accept his wife’s influence? In his research, marriages where the husband was unwilling to share power, to let his wife’s views genuinely affect his decisions, were vastly more likely to fail. And the ones where he did let her influence him were far more likely to thrive. Note the direction: Gottman found that wives generally already accept their husbands’ influence; the piece that’s often missing, and that makes the biggest difference, is the husband learning to yield.


Now, “accepting influence” doesn’t mean losing yourself or agreeing with everything. It means treating your spouse as a genuine partner whose perspective has real weight, so that when they raise a concern or want something, your instinct is to consider it and look for the yes, rather than to defend, dismiss, or dig in. It’s the difference between “here’s why you’re wrong” and “help me understand, you might be seeing something I’m not.”


The Torah’s picture of the husband who listens


This is old wisdom. The Gemara teaches, “if your wife is short, bend down and take counsel with her”, lower yourself to hear her perspective, because her insight is worth having. Chazal held up the man who honors his wife’s opinion, not the one who overrides it. In fact, the Gemara famously says a man should be careful with his wife’s honor, because blessing, in wealth and in the home, comes to a household on her account. The Torah’s vision of a strong husband is not the one who wins every argument. It’s the one secure enough to be moved.


And notice the Gemara’s balance, because Gottman found the same thing. The same passage that says “bend down and seek her counsel” also cautions against following blindly and thoughtlessly. Accepting influence isn’t surrendering your judgment; it’s genuinely weighing your spouse’s, so the decision becomes ours rather than a contest of wills. Two partners looking for common ground, not a winner and a loser.


Yielding is strength, not weakness


There’s a reason Chazal praised the one who is ma’avir al midosav, who can let go, bend, and not need to be right. The person who insists on winning every disagreement doesn’t actually have a strong marriage; he has a scoreboard. The one who can say “you make a good point, let’s do it your way” has something far stronger: a partner who feels genuinely heard, and who becomes, in turn, far more eager to hear him. Influence flows both ways once someone is brave enough to let it flow at all.


Your Action Steps This Week


  1. Find the yes. The next time your spouse raises something you’d instinctively push back on, pause and look for the part you can agree with. Start your response with that: “You’re right that…” It changes the whole conversation.
  2. Ask instead of defend. When you feel yourself getting defensive, replace the defense with a question: “Help me understand how you’re seeing this.” Then actually listen for the thing they might be right about.
  3. Yield on one real thing. Pick one genuine disagreement this week and let your spouse’s view carry the day, not as a concession you resent, but as a partner choosing “us.” Notice what it does to the temperature between you.
