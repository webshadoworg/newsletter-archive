Guarding Our Eyes Protects Our Livelihood

A message from the Kalever Rebbe for Parshas Eikev


“The Eyes of Hashem your God are always upon it, from the beginning of the year to the end of the year.” (Devarim 11:12)


When trouble in the world causes people’s parnassah to decline, people make every effort to protect and stimulate their livelihoods. G-d-fearing Jews, however, believe that the source of their livelihood is Heavenly. They therefore make every effort to improve their spiritual level, committing to increase their Torah, mitzvos, and tefillah, thereby drawing down the abundant blessings of parnassah from Heaven.


One of the main barriers to that Divine flow of parnassah is looking at improper things, which brings improper thoughts and ultimately pollutes the mind. Even if the effect is not immediate, it becomes etched into the mind, and in the end it contaminates the person. For this reason Hashem commanded us, “and you shall guard yourself from every evil thing” (Devarim 23:10), which Chazal explain as a command to keep one’s thoughts pure during the day (Kesubos 46a).


The Holy Zohar (Tikkunei Zohar 30b) teaches that poverty chases anyone mired in such impurity, and even one born under a mazel of wealth can lose his fortune. This includes not only financial poverty but poverty of the mind, because this impurity fogs the brain, shortens attention, and drains the clarity a person needs to succeed even in his business dealings.


The reverse is also true, and always greater: one who is careful to guard his eyes is worthy of abundant parnassah, and with ease. All the pain and difficulty a person endures overcoming the tremendous temptations of shmiras einayim stands as a merit for him, and this greatly lessens the difficulty that may have been decreed upon him to earn a living. This tremendous reward is poured down upon him and his children from Heaven, as explained in Sefer Chasidim (495).


We see this in Yosef HaTzaddik. At only seventeen, he suddenly found himself in Egypt, the most immoral place in the world, yet he conquered his desires and refused to look at Potiphar’s wife, running from her. He kept himself holy even as Viceroy, even when the immodest women of Egypt threw their jewelry at him in the street to get him to look. In that merit, Yosef became worthy of fabulous wealth, to the point that during the famine the entire world came to him for food, “and Yosef was the distributor of food” (Bereishis 42:6). Chazal teach that it was in the merit of Yosef’s shmiras einayim that he drew down abundant parnassah for his descendants after him (Zevachim 118b).


But when Yosef passed away and the Israelites lost their role model in holiness, their eyes and hearts were no longer careful, and they began to frequent places of immodest sights. The Midrash (Tanchuma Shmos 6) teaches on the verse “and the land was filled with them” (Shmos 1:7) that they began filling the Egyptian theaters. When that happened, their parnassah shrank, and they became enslaved, working brutally hard just for a bit of food, and the spiritual root of that decline was the breach in their holiness.


So when a time comes in history when there is an epidemic and parnassah becomes difficult, we must be even stronger than usual in these areas. The Gemara (Taanis 8b) teaches that once a famine and an epidemic struck together, and the people didn’t know which to pray about. Rabbi Shmuel bar Nachmani said they should pray for food, because that prayer includes being saved from the plague, since food is not given to the dead.


The Koznitzer Maggid zt”l explained the depth of this. If a Divine decree is written “maves, raav” (death, famine), the tzaddik is able to move the letter ayin out of the word raav (famine) and place it into maves (death), transforming the decree into “maos rav”, abundant money, nullifying both evil decrees at once. This is the meaning of “behold, the Eye (ayin) of Hashem is upon those who fear Him” (Tehillim 33:18): Hashem gave His ayin over to the tzaddikim, to move it from its place in the decree, “to save their soul from death and keep them alive in famine.” The Rebbe Meir of Dzikov zt”l writes in Imrei Noam that one who keeps his ayin, his eye, pure, knowing it belongs to Hashem and it is disrespectful to use it against His Will, causes that letter ayin to be moved to the good place on his account, so that “maos rav” is decreed upon him.


We similarly find (Bava Kama 60b) that Rava would shut his “windows” during an epidemic, and the earlier sources explain that the windows are the eyes, the windows of the body, because in a time of plague he was careful to close his eyes to any evil sight, since improper sights bring tumah that strengthens the destructive forces, from which famine comes, all of which is nullified when one guards his eyes.


Perhaps this is our parsha’s message. Moshe told the Israelites that once they entered Eretz Yisrael they would not have to labor for parnassah as they did in Egypt, because “the Eyes of Hashem are constantly upon it, from the beginning of the year until the end of the year.” The message is that our eyes belong to Hashem, “the Eyes of Hashem,” so that even if a decree of famine was issued at Rosh Hashanah, it can be nullified before the year’s end through strength in shmiras einayim, as Chazal teach (Rosh Hashanah 16b) that this verse shows a decree can be changed in the middle of the year.


May it be Hashem’s Will that through being strong in shmiras einayim, staying far from immodest sights on our devices, Klal Yisrael be worthy of a life of good parnassah.

A Tisha B’Av Kinnah From a Fighter

By @iwannalivereal (Current streak: 1,050 days)


I feel like I have to introduce myself, it’s been so long since I’ve been active. Coming up to three years clean. I sat down this Tisha B’Av to let out some of the emotions that were part of me for so many years. Nowhere safer to save it than here.


Following the teachings of the “Seder Hayom,” for each to write their own version of kinnos of personal tzaros and churban,
to ruminate over destruction that speaks to him strongly, brings forth the pain of lacking the true life of connection.


Just a few short years it’s been, yet the pain never goes. The tears may have dried, yet the emotions live on.


Those fifteen endless years, feelings of failure, of despair, and of grief.
To feel so alone, so utterly helpless. To try once again, and then slide way back down.


To pour out my heart in prayer during those “let’s try again” moments, feels like no one’s listening as the cycle begins again.


Imagine had I not… how my life would be so different. I’d daven and I’d learn, I’d actually be like them.


I saw myself the rasha, the loser, the baal taavah. I’m different than them all, I’m not as it seems.


If only they knew, the shock of close friends, the disgust of rebbeim, the pain of close family.


Living a double life, such a strain to keep up. Smile as if nothing’s wrong, furthest thing from the truth.


The confusion, the klerring, what’s wrong with me? Will this go on forever, will I finally crash?


If only someone would care, someone to share, someone to notice. I can’t do this myself, the burden too much to bear.


Though the chains have long since broken, their echoes linger still. To live a life that’s real is a lifetime of work.


As we cry for our tzaros, let our wails reach the top. O Father in Heaven… Pleeeeeeeease.

Wisdom from the Forum: “I’ve Been Clean a Long Time. How Do I Not Get Complacent?”

This is the veteran’s question, and it fits Eikev perfectly - the parsha that warns us “pen tishkach”, lest you forget Hashem precisely when things are going well and you’re comfortable and full. Long clean streaks bring their own danger, and the chevra has learned this one the hard way. Here’s their wisdom.


The most repeated warning is the simplest: don’t withdraw. Almost every complacency fall starts the same way, quietly drifting from the things that kept you strong. @strugle613 noticed that after nice stretches of being clean, he stopped spending time on GYE and being mechazek himself, and that’s exactly when he fell. @iwtbf613 told the same story: he stopped coming to the website and making GYE a priority, and a relapse followed. The lesson is counterintuitive but crucial - the better you’re doing, the more tempting it is to coast, and the coasting itself is the risk.


The antidote is to stay engaged and keep giving. @serenity put it beautifully to strugle613: “You help other people by posting here, whether you realize it or not.” Once you have some clean time behind you, your presence on the forum stops being only about your recovery - your experience becomes chizuk for the person three days in. Staying to help others is one of the best ways to protect yourself. @gibbor120 suggested to a member who fell after 112 days that he consider joining a 12-step phone call or speaking to a real person about the struggle, and @2restart found enormous help in simply being “out there, in real life, talking to people, and in general being active, doing things.” Connection, online and off, is armor.


Just as important is not letting your spiritual practices quietly erode. @2restart prioritizes daily Torah learning, especially Chassidus, and warns that an “emptiness” creeps in when the learning slides - exactly the emptiness the Yetzer Hara loves to fill. He goes to shul every day, “even, and especially, after a yeridah,” and has found that staying late in shul is sometimes safer than being home and vulnerable. @alexeliezer boils the response to any slip down to three words: “Daven. Move on.” And @iwtbf613 shared a subtle but vital insight: he realized his sobriety had quietly become all about his taphsic (his commitment-with-consequences) and less about actually strengthening his connection with Hashem. When the streak becomes a scoreboard instead of a relationship, complacency isn’t far behind.


The veterans also stay ruthlessly self-aware. @holy yid fell after a long clean streak precisely because of complacency, and his suggestion is to keep a running list of honest questions to ask yourself, such as: Do I think I can use the internet without a filter now? Do I think I can use it late at night? Do I think I can use it when no one’s around? The moment you catch yourself answering “yeah, I’m fine now,” that’s the complacency talking. @2restart names his own danger zones plainly: loneliness at home when the family’s asleep, and, surprisingly, an unchanneled “high” or burst of excitement - both can lead to a fall. And @syatadshmaya sums it up in four words: “Never let your guard down.”


Underneath it all is one hard truth the long-timers accept rather than resist. @colincolin reminded @david26fr that “all of us on here must take things day by day for the rest of our lives. We can never rest on our laurels.” Even a full year clean is not a finish line. So when a slip does happen, @2restart is emphatic: “ALWAYS ALWAYS get right back on track, keep davening, learning, aderabe, that is what helps you get back on track.”


Finally, the healthiest guard against complacency isn’t fear - it’s gratitude. @ur-a-jew finds his strength by reflecting on Hashem’s creation and the simple gift of a working body and mind, which turns guarding his eyes from a burden into an expression of thanks. And @eraygrand drew chizuk from realizing he wasn’t alone - that even “many normal people” fight this same fight. Remembering how far you’ve come, and Who brought you there, keeps the streak humble instead of proud.


The bottom line? Complacency is just forgetting - the exact thing Eikev warns against. Stay connected, keep giving to others, guard your davening and learning, run an honest self-check, know your triggers, and stay grateful. You never graduate from this, but that’s not discouraging. It’s what keeps you awake, and awake is exactly how you stay free.

10 Really Dumb Mistakes Smart Couples Make

Mistake #9: Forgetting That You Are Best Friends, By Rabbi Benzion Shafier (TheShmuz.com)


Over the past weeks we’ve learned to stop trying to change our spouse, to understand how differently they experience the world, and how differently they hear our words. This week we reach the foundation underneath all of it: a husband and wife are, first and foremost, reyim ahuvim, best friends who love each other. Forget that, and everything else falls apart.


“I Married a Drill Sergeant”


Rabbi Shafier paints a scene. It’s six thirty in the evening. A young husband comes home from work, drops onto the couch, and buries himself in the newspaper. In the kitchen, his wife is slaving over dinner while the kids wreck the apartment. The steam rises, and it isn’t from the pot. Finally she’s had it. She marches over, points a finger, and says, “All right, mister, off that couch! You’re either cooking dinner or watching the kids, but you are not just sitting there.”


He gets up and helps. Did she do the right thing? Plenty of people, men and women both, would say absolutely, she’s a hero. And maybe he really was wrong to be sitting there. But here’s what happens next. At nine o’clock, the kids are finally asleep, and she sits down beside him on the couch to enjoy his company. He edges away. She moves closer. He moves further. She can’t understand it. What she doesn’t know is that for the last two hours, his brain has been playing her words on a loop: All right, mister, off that couch… All right, mister… until he arrives at a chilling conclusion: “I don’t believe it. I married a drill sergeant.”


That’s the ninth really dumb mistake smart couples make: forgetting that you are best friends.


Friends First, Then Lovers


Under the chuppah we bless the couple, “Be joyful, be happy, reyim ahuvim.” Rashi (Kesubos 8a) explains that reyim ahuvim means the chassan and kallah are “friends who love each other.” Notice the order: friends first, “who love each other” second. The pasuk in Malachi (2:14) calls a man’s wife chevrascha, your friend, and the Targum Yonasan renders it shutfascha, your partner. A husband and wife are life partners, sharing everything - the good, the bad, and the in-between.


That’s exactly where the wife on the couch went wrong. She may have been completely right about the facts, but she didn’t act like a friend. And you can be 100% correct and your spouse 100% wrong, but the moment you act like a boss instead of a friend, your marriage suffers.


The First Rule of Friendship: Nobody’s the Boss


Who’s in charge in a healthy marriage? Who has the power, the final say, the veto? The answer is: no one. You are equal partners with different roles. You might jointly agree to divide responsibilities - one takes the lead on finances, the other on the home - but that’s a shared decision between partners, not a chain of command. Friends don’t boss friends around. The trouble is that inside each spouse there’s a voice screaming, if I don’t take charge, my needs won’t get met. And so, often with the very best intentions, one spouse takes control to “right the ship,” and in doing so quietly wrecks the relationship.


The Ham Sandwich on Yom Kippur


Rabbi Shafier pushes the idea to its limit. Imagine a woman comes home from shul on Yom Kippur and finds her husband on the couch eating a ham sandwich and smoking a cigar. She’s horrified, “Put that down right now! What is wrong with you?!” Now, what he’s doing is flagrantly, indefensibly wrong. Doesn’t she have the right to demand he stop?


The answer is striking: she would have that right if she were his rabbi, his mentor, or his mother. But that’s not who she is. She’s his wife, his equal partner, his friend - not his boss and not his rav. As long as she’s in the partnership, she has every right to talk to him, discuss it, plead with him, even go together to someone for help. What she doesn’t get to do is demand, command, or dictate. “Putting your foot down” belongs to a teacher and student, a boss and employee, a judge and defendant - not to best friends who love each other. You might win the battle, but you’ll lose the war, and it will be war.


(Rabbi Shafier notes the obvious exceptions: genuine danger - he’s driving ninety-five on the highway - or abuse. There you speak up emphatically. But short of those extremes, everything in a marriage gets negotiated as friends.)


Not a Teacher, Either


It’s not just bossing; it’s teaching, too. Rabbi Shafier shares that early in his marriage he and his wife tried learning Mesillas Yesharim together. The first time went fine. The second time, less so. Afterward his wife told him she’d rather not learn together, “I feel like I’m in school and you’re the teacher.” To this day, he says, he’s uncomfortable when his wife attends his shiurim, because that’s not their relationship. They’re reyim ahuvim, not teacher and student.


The takeaway for this week is simple and searching: in every friction point, ask yourself not “am I right?” but “am I acting like a friend?” Because being right while acting like a boss is precisely how smart, loving couples slowly dismantle the friendship that holds their marriage together.


Your Action Steps This Week


  1. Catch the “drill sergeant” moment before it happens. The next time you’re about to issue an order to your spouse, stop and rephrase it the way you’d say it to a good friend you respect. Same need, different delivery.
  2. Ask your spouse this week, gently and for real: “Are we still friends?” Then listen to the answer without getting defensive.
  3. Notice if you’ve slipped into the role of rebbi, teacher, or boss in some area of your marriage - the frumkeit, the budget, the driving - and consciously step back into the role of partner and friend.
