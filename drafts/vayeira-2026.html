When You Know It’s a Test, It Becomes a Game

This week’s parsha is all about nisyonos and we’re all very familiar with it in our own personal lives. One attitude that greatly reduces the allure of temptation is to see it as a test from above. This is a famous story that illustrates the point, as told by R’ Elimelech Biderman:


A man came to one of the tzaddikim and poured out his heart. He was badly afflicted with anger, he said, and he could not get the better of it.


The tzaddik told him to wait outside the room.


A little later the man heard the Rebbe’s voice through the door, raised and perfectly clear, giving the gabbai his instructions. Bring in a glass of tea. On your way past him, contrive to spill some of it on his clothes, and give him a good shove while you are at it.


The gabbai came out and did exactly that. The man did not get angry. He did not raise his voice, and he did not let the gabbai feel that anything had happened at all. The tzaddik sent him out to do it again, and again, and every time the man answered the same way.


Then the tzaddik called him in. “I do not see that you are a baal kaas at all. The gabbai provoked you several times just now, and every time you answered him with respect and derech eretz.”


“What was I supposed to do,” the man said. “The Rebbe spoke a little too loudly and the door was open a crack. I heard, and I knew the Rebbe was putting me to a nisayon. How was I going to walk into a trap that had been laid in front of me? I would have made a laughingstock of myself if I had gotten angry.”


“Then from now on,” the tzaddik said, “remember this always. Every nisayon you have is prepared and sent to you from Shamayim, every detail of it with wondrous hashgacha, to see how you overcome it and grow stronger. And when you think that way, it will always be very easy for you to overcome.”


HAFTARAH

The Flask of Oil

The haftarah opens with a widow crying to Elisha. Her husband has died, the creditor is coming to seize her two children as slaves, and she has nothing. Elisha asks her, “What do you have in the house?” She answers: nothing at all, except a single small flask of oil.


R’ YY Jacobson, in the name of the Baal HaTanya, hears in this the cry of a soul that has gone numb.


A person comes and says: my fire is dead. “Avdecha ishi meis”, my husband has died, and the word ishi also means my fire. I used to feel something. I used to have passion for Yiddishkeit, warmth in my davening, life in my mitzvos. Now I’m just going through the motions. I’m numb inside, and worse, even my emotions aren’t my own anymore, the creditor is coming to take my children, my loves and my fears have been hijacked by habits and pulls stronger than me.


Elisha asks the one question that matters: is there anything left that is truly yours? And the answer is yes. A small flask of oil.


Oil, the Baal HaTanya explains, is unlike any other liquid. Pour it into any mixture and it refuses to blend in; it always rises back to the top and keeps its own identity. That flask is the essence of your soul, the deepest point of you that stays holy no matter what has happened. It cannot be dissolved by your traumas, captured by your habits, or destroyed by your worst night. You may not be able to see it or feel it. It is still there, and it is still yours, and nothing you have ever done has touched it.


Then Elisha gives strange advice: go borrow empty vessels, many of them, from all your neighbors, and don’t borrow few. Then close the door, and start pouring.


The empty, borrowed vessels, says the Baal HaTanya, are our actions when we feel nothing. The mitzvos that feel robotic. The davening with no heart in it. The things you do only because you know you should, that feel fake, borrowed, not really you. And here is the chiddush that changes everything: Elisha does not tell her those empty vessels are worthless. He tells her to go get more of them. Keep acting. Keep doing the mitzvos, keep showing up, even when it’s empty, even when it feels borrowed, even when you go to sleep sure that none of it was real.


Because here is what happens. Every so often, the flask of oil surfaces on its own. A moment of inspiration hits you out of nowhere, your heart suddenly opens, the fire flickers back for a few seconds. And in that moment, one of two things happens. If your life is empty of vessels, that precious oil has nowhere to go, and it drains away, and you’re left numb again, waiting helplessly for the next flicker. But if your home is full of vessels, all those “empty,” “borrowed,” going-through-the-motions actions, then when the oil comes, it pours into every one of them and fills them with light. Suddenly all those “fake” mitzvos were never fake at all. They were vessels, waiting.


This is why we’re told to place the words of Torah al levavecha, on your heart, and not in it. Because the heart isn’t always open. So you lay the deeds on top of the closed heart, and you keep laying them there, and when the heart finally opens, everything on it falls straight in.


And “close the door.” You cannot do this with the door wide open. Owning yourself means shutting out the forces that drain you, the distractions, the pulls, the things that feed off your energy. Build a closed, protected space, and pour.


So if you feel numb, if your avodas Hashem feels dead and your mitzvos feel empty, do not make the one fatal mistake of concluding that you’re actually dead and giving up. The very fact that it pains you to feel nothing is proof that the oil is still burning somewhere inside. The deadness is real, but it is not the truth about you. Keep filling your life with vessels. The oil is yours, it was never taken, and one day it will flow, and fill every last one of them.

MARRIAGE

Keep Making Her Dear

By Rabbi Yissocher Frand


In this week’s parsha, the angels ask Avraham, “Where is Sarah your wife?” Rashi, quoting the Gemara (Bava Metzia 87a), explains that they knew exactly where she was. They asked only to point out her modesty, to make her more precious to her husband.


Rav Wolbe raises a simple question. At a sheva brachos, we praise the chosson and kallah to cement their bond. That makes sense for a new couple. But Avraham was nearly 100 and Sarah nearly 90, married for decades. By now, either she was endeared to him or nothing would help. So what were the angels accomplishing by making her dearer to her husband?


The answer, says Rav Wolbe, is that endearing a wife to her husband is needed for the entire lifetime of a marriage. A couple can be married thirty, fifty, sixty years, and still a marriage is a living relationship that needs constant renewal. He adds that this is part of the very purpose of the halachos of taharas hamishpacha, so that a wife returning from the mikvah is “as dear to her husband as a bride under the chuppah” (Nidda 31b). One of the greatest dangers to a marriage, he warns, is dull routine. Even Avraham and Sarah needed the work of keeping each other dear.


Rabbi Frand brings a story (from Rav Sholom Meir Wallach, heard through Rav Nosson Friedman) that captures it. Rav Dovid Hirschovitz, an American who had learned in the Mir in Europe before the war, visited Rav Chaim Shmuelevitz in Eretz Yisrael decades later. Rav Chaim brought him home for lunch, and the guest watched in astonishment. The great Rosh Yeshiva, who in his youth had been so absorbed in learning that people had to remind him to eat, now devoured his wife’s chicken and rice, cleaned his plate to the bone, asked which spices she’d used, called it delicious, and asked for seconds.


Afterward, when Mrs. Shmuelevitz had left the room, the guest asked what had happened to him. Rav Chaim explained: “I’m the best maggid shiur in Eretz Yisrael. I worked on my shiurim for forty years. They are gold. And yet when a seventeen-year-old bochur tells me, ‘Rebbe, that was a good shiur,’ it makes my day, even though he barely grasps what went into it. That’s human nature. We all need to be appreciated. This lunch,” he said, “is my wife’s shiur. Taking care of me is what she lives for. So I eat every morsel with gusto, to show her how much I value it.”


That is “making her dear to her husband.” Rav Chaim was an older man, married more than fifty years. And still, every wife, and every person, needs to be appreciated, no matter how long they’ve been married. The compliment, the cleaned plate, the noticing, is not something you outgrow. It’s something a marriage lives on.
