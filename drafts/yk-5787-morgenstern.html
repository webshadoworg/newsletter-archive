Strategies for Successful Teshuva

By Rabbi Chaim Morgenstern

Source: dafyomireview.com, article 198. Pulled Sep 16, 2026. The article’s own typos and spacing are left as they are.

Part I

MAKING A “TOKEN PAYMENT”

“It’s almost a year since last Yom Kippur. I can’t believe it, a year already went by!”

This is what comes to many of our minds when we hear the Shofar on Rosh Chodesh Elul reminding us that the Yomim Ha’nora’im are approaching.

We receive another reminder when we start selichos, and finally we hear again the shofar on Rosh Hashana, this time it’s real.

During the month of Elul and the aseres yemei teshuva we start thinking more seriously about self improvement and my even attend some inspiring shiyurim about teshuva. Finally it’s Yom Kippur! During the ten times that we recited the al chait and ashamnu, we managed to review the past year, regret our misdeeds and make some resolutions for the forthcoming year. After the motzai Yom Kippur meal our minds our now focused on the yom tov preparations - building the sukah, finding a lulav and esrog etc. By the time Sukkos ends, the memories of Yom Kippur start fading and eventually becomes something of the past. As the year starts moving, we slowly start falling back to our old habits. Suddenly, it’s Rosh Chodesh Elul again, and we find ourselves back to square one. For some, this becomes a yearly repetitious cycle causing much frustration and loss of motivation.

When looking at the al chait, we can become overwhelmed just at the thought of all the areas that need improvement and the numerous resolutions that must be implemented. As Rav Avraham Pam writes, “It would seem that teshuva is an impossible task for many people. True, there may be some special people who can fulfill the demanding requirements of teshuva, but what can the average person hope to achieve? This could explain why many people are ”cold" to this teshuva period and let the golden opportunity provided by Elul and the Yomim Ha’nora’im slip through their fingers. They honestly believe that teshuva is an overwhelming task beyond their reach, and despair of ever attaining it. They may go through the motions of saying selichos and the lengthy Yamim Ha’nora’im tefillos, but in their hearts they wait impatiently for this season to pass.

This is a tragic error. Elul is a time when Hashem is especially close with his creations, making it easier for a person to cleanse his soul of all his sins. However, He also recognizes the many temptations that we face in our immoral and corrupt world and how difficult it is for us to live pure Torah lives. What is expected of us?

THE BANKRUPTCY MASHAL · RAV PAM, THE PLEASANT WAY pp. 239-40

The following parable will help us understand what we can do. A businessman, whose company is failing, is in crises with his increasing debts and overdrawn bank account. As things stand, the only way that he can get out of this predicament is to declare bankruptcy thereby legally (but not halachically) freeing himself of his debts. While the bankruptcy courts may protect him from his creditors, they will not be able to quiet the anger and resentment of those to whom he defaulted on large sums of money.

However, an honest and conscientious businessman will not disregard his obligations but will make an effort to avoid this option. He will plead for an extension of the loans, request a reduction of payment and attempt to pay back some of his debts. By making even a token payment, he will convey a message to his creditors that even though at present he cannot fulfill his obligations, when his financial situation improves he will make every effort to repay whatever he owes.

The same principle applies to teshuva. When a person feels overwhelmed by his many sins and character faults, he may think to himself ’Where do I begin to do teshuva?’ One option is to raise his hands in despair and to surrender to the yetzer hora. However, this will certainly anger his creditor, i.e., Hashem.

However, reflecting back to the businessman in crises, there is another option of dealing with this overwhelmed feeling. A person may honestly believe that doing teshuva for every sin is impossibility. But just because he cannot do everything does that mean that he should do nothing? Let him make a “token payment” that will express his desire to make a full payment towards his “debt” to Hashem. Once he takes the first step that shows his desire to improve himself, Hashem will reduce his debt.

Additionally, when a person takes the first step towards teshuva, he will receive siyata dishmaya - heavenly assistance - that will enable him towards the goal of becoming a complete ba’al teshuva. This thought is underscored in Midrash Shir Hashirim (5:3), which says ’If a person makes an opening of teshuva as small as the head of a needle Hashem will open for him a passageway to teshuva wide enough to allow carriages and wagons to pass through." (Adapted from The pleasant way, pp 239-40).

The Pri Megadim, (Psicha part 3 # 10), writes a another important and encouraging principle of the teshuva procedure based on the gemara in Mesechta Kidushin (49b.)

The gemara gives a scenario of a person who proposes to a woman on the condition that he is a tzadik - righteous person - and then gives her a ring. If she accepts the ring she is married even if he is a rasha gamur - totally wicked person - because he may have done teshuva . The obvious question that arises is how can a rasha gamur do teshuva for all of his aveiros in the seconds that it takes for a woman to agree to marry until he gives the ring? Additionally, since teshuva for each aveira requires regret, confession, and acceptance (charata, viduy and kabala) it seems an impossible task for a rasha gamur to accomplish this task in such a short amount of time.

Thus, no matter what a person did during the past year, he should accept upon himself to initiate the teshuva process and begin with at least a small change in towards self-improvement. By making this commitment and small change he will no longer be regarded as a rasha, and will merit siyata dishmaya to continue on the road towards attaining a total teshuva.

Following the sagacious advice of the Pri Megadim and Rav Pam will give us encouragement and motivation in making a sincere effort to give a start in changing our ways.

Part II

DOING PARTIAL TESHUVA

Although the mitzva of doing teshuva is not limited to Elul and the Yamim Ha’nora’im, nevertheless the Rambam and Rabeinu Yona write that there is a special obligation on Yom Kippur to do teshuva because it is Yom Hadin - the Day of Judgment ( Hilchos Teshuva 2:7, Sha’arei Teshuva 4:17).

The Rambam (ibid. #2) lists four necessary parts of proper teshuva:

1) Charata - regret

2) Azivas ha’chait - discontinue doing the aveira

3) Viduy - verbal confession

4) Kabala - making a resolution to cease doing the aveira.

The part that we are most familiar with is the mitzva of viduy, which we recite ten times throughout the Yom Kippur davaning. The reason for this mitzva is that human nature causes us to rationalize our shortcomings as either unavoidable (o’nes) or even a mitzva (to get angry or speak lashon hara). Therefore, if we don’t acknowledge our wrongdoings, we will not be able to do teshuva properly. However, viduy is only one quarter of doing teshuva, and when a person says viduy for any aveira, he must accompany it with the other three parts mentioned in the Rambam.

What, then, should one do if he regrets transgressing an aveira but knows that he cannot discontinue doing it? Let’s say a person knows that he cannot control speaking lashon hara about certain people, or refrain himself from eating certain foods without a proper hechsher. Will his regret have any significance?

The Mabit, in his classic sefer Bais Elokim, writes that regret alone is a significant accomplishment in doing teshuva (quoted in Alei Shur, vol. 1, pp. 45-6). The reason is, through regretting an aveira, a person will feel guilty, at least momentarily, when he is faced with transgressing it again. In time, this guilty feeling will make him more aware that he is continuing to do the aveira and will eventually cause him to do proper teshuva. (However, even if regret only causes a sigh when doing the aveira again, it is still very significant, as will soon be explained).

Moreover, the Mabit writes that even if a person did proper teshuva on Yom Kippur but returned to his old ways afterward, his teshuva was still accepted, (i.e., we don’t consider his teshuva to be insincere after returning to his old ways).

Rav Wolbe writes that we must be grateful to the Mabit for giving us another ray of hope in the difficult task of doing teshuva.

Even if a person feels that he can only refrain from doing an aveira for a short time, for example, not speaking lashon hara or not getting angry for an hour a day, it is an achievement. Rav Yisrael Salanter writes that this person is called a “ba’al teshuva gamur be’chelek mei’ha’aveira” - a complete ba’al teshuva in part of the aveira (Lekach Tov Yamim Ha’nora’im, vol.2 p.110).

Rav Shalom Schwadran gives additional words of encouragement to a person who continues to transgress an aveira after doing teshuva for it (Lekach Tov ibid., pp 108-9). He compares this situation to someone trying to dig a hole in soft ground. Initially, he encounters much difficulty and frustration while removing the earth because as he digs, more dirt keeps caving in from the soft walls. But with each dig, he removes a little more dirt and will eventually succeed in creating a hole.

The same applies to teshuva. Even when a person transgresses an aveira for which he already did teshuva, doing teshuva the second time will be easier. And if he still is not successful, he should continue to make resolutions. The more he tries, the easier it will be to keep the resolutions until he eventually succeeds. Rav Shalom’s parable is kin to the expression, “The winners are the losers who keep trying.”

Rav Itzele Peterburger, a disciple of Rav Yisroel Salanter, gives additional words of encouragement through the following Gemara (quoted in Alei Shur, vol. 2, p. 440). The Torah states that if someone stole an ox or sheep and either sold it or slaughtered it, he must be penalized by paying four times the value of the sheep and five times the value of the ox (Shemos, 21:37). The Gemara explains that the reason why he pays only four times the sheep’s value is that in the process of stealing the sheep, the thief suffered embarrassment by carrying it on his shoulder. Therefore, his punishment is lessened (Bava Kama 79b).

Rav Itzele applies this same reasoning to transgressing an aveira. An aveira committed with a painful feeling or a sigh is recorded in shamayim as, “Ploni did an aveira with a sigh.” The difference between committing an aveira with and without a sigh is enormous.

The story is told that when the Chafetz Chaim was about to print his monumental work on lashon hara, Rav Yisrael Salanter remarked, “Do you think that publishing your sefer will stop people from speaking lashon hara?” The Chafetz Chaim replied, “It is worth publishing the sefer even if it will only cause people to give a sigh when they speak lashon hara.”

This reasoning is clear. The first step toward doing teshuva is being aware that an aveira was committed. Thus, once a person sighs, he becomes aware that he transgressed an aveira and has broken the ice for doing a more successful teshuva the following year.

Therefore, even if one repeated to transgress an aveira for which he already did teshuva, or at least said viduy on Yom Kippur, nevertheless it is considered an achievement if the viduy caused him to sigh when he does the aveira again.

This concept that partial teshuva helps, whether it is simply regretting our aveiros, reverting to our old ways or giving a sigh, fits in perfectly with the words of Rav Pam who says that we must make at least a “token payment” to Hashem to show Him that we are striving to do a complete teshuva (quoted in Part I of this article).

PART III

MAKING IT LAST

THE PRELIMINARIES

The first step toward achieving a successful teshuva is to properly prepare yourself before Yom Kippur by studying the Ashamnu and Al chait sections of the viduy. Rav Chaim Friedlander explains that the reason why the aveiros are not enumerated in the Ashamnu section is because we are repenting for the source (shoresh) of the aveiros-the evil character traits that caused us to sin. Rectifying the source of an aveira will automatically cause a person to refrain from transgressing it (Sifsai Chaim, Mo’adim, Vol. I, p. 346).

Nevertheless, it is customary to specify the sins in alphabetical order, corresponding to the words of the Ashamnu text. For example, when saying “Ashamnu,” we list the aveiros that begin with the letter aleph, such as “Achalti bli bracha” - I have eaten without a bracha. A comprehensive list appears in the Chayei Adam (klal 143) and in most viduy booklets.

When reviewing the list of aveiros in the above sections, you should choose the essential areas that you feel need the most improvement, so that when you recite the viduy, you can focus on specific aveiros for which to do teshuva. If you are uncertain about which aveiros to work on, you should seek advice from a rav to whom you feel close. Rav Pam suggests that a person should choose areas that he feels a natural affinity toward self-improvement (Atara Lemelech, p.241).

Additionally, since we usually become weaker as the fast progresses, we should invest our energy and concentration during the evening and morning tefilos while we can still properly focus on the viduy.

THE RESOLUTION

The following are important guidelines for making and implementing resolutions.

1. MAKE REALISTIC RESOLUTIONS. When Yom Kippur ends, we are usually on a spiritual high with tremendous inspiration to quickly implement our resolutions. However, since changing habits is a gradual process, we must be cautious not to accept upon ourselves anything that may be more than we can handle. Taking on too much is counterproductive, causing frustration and resulting in discontinuing to implement the resolution. Let’s say a person decides to work on improving his concentration (kavana) in davening. If he begins by trying to have kavana in large portions of Shacharis, he will immediately encounter much difficulty and begin to think that kavana in davening is beyond his reach. This will result in a feeling of despair and possibly abandoning to carry out his resolution. Therefore, we must be honest with ourselves about our individual capabilities.

2. BEGIN WITH SOMETHING EASY. Using the example of improving kavana in davening, we should initially focus on small segments, giving preference to the parts of davening where kavana is required. These are the pasuk of “Posayach es yadecha etc.”, in Ashrei, the first pasuk of the Shema and the first bracha of the Shemona Esrei (Avos). During the day, one or two additional b’rachos should also be added, such as: one Asher yatzar, a bracha on food and the first b’racha of Birkas Hamazon.

The same method applies for doing chesed or giving tzedaka. Giving large amounts of tzedaka or doing too much chesed may be too overwhelming and stressful. Doing small daily acts of chesed or tzedaka will be more effective in making lasting changes in how we fulfill these mitzvos (cf commentary of the Rambam on Avos 3:15).

Similarly, if you encounter difficulty in setting aside time for learning, you should start by a making a small seder in an easy time slot, for example, between mincha and ma’ariv, or 10-15 minutes after Shacharis. Once you make a start, it will be easier to increase the learning times.

3. BE CONSISTENT. The key to success is being consistent in your commitment to keep the resolutions. If you skipped a day, you must somehow make it up. For example, if you forgot to have kavana in one of the brachos or parts of the davening of your resolution, then you must make it up by having kavana in an additional bracha or tefilla. Similarly, if you forgot to do chesed on a particular day, you must do an additional chesed the following day; or if you missed your learning seder, you must somehow find the time to make it up. This will ensure continuity of the resolutions.

4. PROGRESS SLOWLY. Rav Wolbe writes that teshuva is a gradual process (Alei Shur, vol. 2 p. 438). Therefore, it is extremely vital not to progress too quickly. Failure to do so can result in becoming stressed out and giving up the resolution. We must realize that we are not meant to become tzaddikim (righteous people) overnight. We can compare working on our midos to trekking up a 20-story building. Walking non-stop will cause muscle strain. On the other hand, if we stop in the middle, we may decide to give up. The way to succeed is to ascend in a slow and steady pace. The same goes for teshuva. Changing habits is a difficult task, as Rav Yisroel Salanter said, “Changing a mida (character trait) is more difficult than completing shas (the entire Talmud).” Since completing shas takes seven years in a daf yomi cycle, we should not be surprised if it takes this long to make a permanent change in our midos.

Therefore, when working on a particular area of improvement, we should not progress until we are able to fulfill our present resolution without feeling strain. For example, when working on davening, one should not advance in increasing his kavana until he is able to be mechavain (concentrate) in what he is already saying.

5. AVOID NO-WIN SITUATIONS. Since our objective is to avoid obstacles that will interfere with the continuity of working on our resolutions, we should avoid situations that will cause us difficulty in succeeding. The Sefer Lekach Tov terms this concept “avoiding confrontation with your emotions” (Yamin Nora’im vol. 2 pp 88-9). For example, if you are working on controlling anger, you may find it extremely difficult to remain calm on erev Shabbos if you leave most of your preparations for the last minute. The resolution must be to avoid the last minute Shabbos rush. Similarly, if you are about to bentch but are disturbed about something, your kavana will be hindered. Instead of trying to struggle with having kavana while bentching, you should take a few moments before bentching to try to calm down and unwind from what’s disturbing him you. Davening from a bentcher is also extremely helpful to facilitate kavana. (In fact, in his later years, Rav E. Shach, referring to Sefer Hachinuch Mitzva # 430, would constantly advocate to recite the benching from a bencher.)

PRESERVING THE INSPIRATION

Rav Avraham Pam writes that just as Chazal say that we should not leave shul in a hurry (B’rachos 6b), similarly we should not “run away” from Yom Kippur. When we leave shul on motzai Yom Kippur tired and exhausted from fasting and davening, we immediately focus on the meal and Yom Tov preparations. Nevertheless, we should take the inspiration and kedushas hayom with us and remember our thoughts, resolutions, regrets and the many prayers for which we entreated Hashem for forgiveness (Atara Lemelech p. 180).

Rav Avigdor Miller said that after Yom Kippur one should avoid leitzanus (frivolous behavior), as Mesilas Yesharim writes (ch.5), “ One act of leitzanus eliminates an enormous amount of inspiration.” Some examples are mocking remarks about the chazan’s voice or the rabbi’s sermon (which is also lashon hara).

After Yom Kippur, we should write our resolutions on a small index card and keep it in a place where we can view it daily, such as in a tallis bag or purse. This will constantly remind us to work on the resolutions.

THE SIGNIFICANCE OF A SMALL ACHIEVEMENT

How should we feel if our progress is only minute after weeks of constantly working on resolutions? The following words of the Chafetz Chaim gives us encouragement to carry on:

A talmid of the yeshiva of the Chafetz Chaim in Radin approached the Chafetz Chaim and asked with great concern, “The Zohar states that any mitzvah that is performed without fear and love of Hashem does not ascend to heaven. If so, what will happen to the people of our generation in the World to Come? Who can say that we perform the mitzvos with the required amount of ahavas and yiras Hashem?”

The Chafetz Chaim replied softly, "Let me tell you about something that happened a while ago. Before the world war, I met a baker whom I knew and asked him how his business was going.

"He replied, ’I wish I could say that business was good, but the truth is I work very hard all night making a thousand loaves of bread and rolls. In the morning, when I go out to sell them, I only hear complaints. One customer says the roll is over-baked, another complains that the bread wasn’t baked enough and many complain that they just don’t taste good. As hard as I try, I manage to sell only about 600 loaves in the morning, and the other 400, I have to sell in the afternoon as animal food for a very cheap price. As a result, I lose a great deal of my profits. Rebbi, can you please give me a bracha for parnassa?’

" I blessed him and then we parted.

"Shortly afterward, the world war broke out and we met once again. This time the baker seemed very happy and content, which surprised me, since everyone else was downcast because of the war. When I asked him how his business was doing, he exclaimed, ’It’s just wonderful!’

" ’But it’s war time! How can it be so good?’ I asked.

" ’That’s just it,’ replied the baker. ’During a war, people will take any bread and rolls that they can get.’

“And that is what Hashem feels about our generation,” replied the Chafetz Chaim to his talmid. “We are all involved with a tremendous war with the yetzer hara, and the temptations and tests for every person are staggering. And yet, people perform mitzvos and manage to study Torah. How wonderful! Hashem is ready to accept any of our mitzvos, even if they have not been fulfilled with the highest level of ahavas and yiras Hashem.”

If this was said almost 100 years ago, how much more true is it in today’s generation? Of the approximately 15 million Jews in the world, how many are affiliated with any type of Judaism? And of those who are affiliated, how many are conscientious about properly fulfilling mitzvos? And of those who go to shul on Rosh Hashana and Yom Kippur, how many make an effort to feel the holiness of the day?

We are living in a “time of war” worse than the times of the Chafetz Chaim. Hashem is desperate to see any Jew go to shul, daven and make any attempt to do teshuva. Although we must always strive toward perfection, nevertheless, when we are confronted with spiritual roadblocks, we should not despair or become depressed. Every accomplishment, however small, is significant in the eyes of Hashem. The yetzer hara is so strong in our generation that one small step toward self-improvement is considered a giant stride in avodas Hashem.

(Adapted from Around the Maggid’s Table, Artscroll, pp 278-80.)

DON’T BE DISCOURAGED BY FAILURES

Even if we feel that we have not been successful, nevertheless we should not get discouraged & still keep trying as the expression goes: “ The winners are the losers who keep trying”.

The following story illustrates this point:

Rosh Hashana had arrived, and Mayer, a 17-year-old talmid of Reb Shimshon Pincus, was disappointed. Reb Pincus’ inspiring shmuessen (Torah lectures) had made a distinct impression on Mayer to understand what the awe of the High Holy Days meant resulting in making commitments and resolutions to improve in his yiras shamayim, davening and learning. However, although he worked very hard to keep these commitments and resolutions, he failed to put them into action and felt that he had not progressed sufficiently for the upcoming Day of Judgment. As a result, he felt like a complete failure.

On Rosh Hashana morning, Rav Pincus stepped outside the Shul for a moment and spotted Mayer in the corner of the hallway sitting on a low chair with his head between his knees. Rav Pincus, with his warm and caring personality, approached Mayer and placed his hand on his shoulder asking him why he seemed so depressed.

Mayer looked up into his rebbis eyes and poured out his frustration. He told him how hard he had tried during this past month to improve but had no success. He had pledged that this Rosh Hashana would be different but it wasn’t. Mayer cried bitterly and hung his head in shame perceiving himself as a total failure.

Rav Pincus sat down next to the boy and related the following incident:

"During the Yom Kippur War, I was sitting in the Emergency Room in the Shaarei Tzedek hospital waiting for one of my children to be seen by a doctor for a cut that apparently needed stitches, when I noticed a commotion taking place. Many doctors had converged in front of the Emergency Room doors and appeared to be discussing an important matter.

"I inquired as to what was the cause of the commotion and I was told a soldier had been shot and had been brought to the hospital. The bullet was lodged in his leg and needed to be taken out. After the doctor had removed the bullet he notified the soldier, who had been awake during the entire procedure that the surgery was complete and he was free to go home. The young man, no older than nineteen years, painfully struggled to slide off the operating table. Beads of sweat formed on his forehead and he looked curiously at the doctor.

’Home? You think I’m heading home? True, I was shot and lost this battle, but there’s a war raging out there and my fellow soldiers need me back on the battlefield.’ The brave young man hobbled out of the room and headed back toward the front lines."

As Rav Pincus concluded this story he looked into Mayer’s eyes and said, “Mayer, I’m not going to convince you that you have not stumbled. But just because you’ve fallen does not mean you can’t get back up. Losing a battle does not mean that you’ve lost the war. We’re fighting a war in there and I don’t want to head back into shul without you. We need you to fight alongside the rest of us!”

A shy smile formed on Mayer’s face and he thanked his Rebbi for the warm words of encouragement. Rav Pincus placed his arm around Mayer’s shoulder and together they walked back into shul to “fight” alongside one another.

TO SUMMERIZE THE 9 STEPS FOR MAKING TESHUVA LAST:

1) Review the viduy beforehand

2) Make realistic resolutions

3) Begin with something easy

4) Be consistent

5) Progress slowly

6) Avoid no-win situations

7) Preserve the inspiration

8) Value small achievements

9) Don’t be discouraged by failures

May we all have a g’mar chasima tova.
