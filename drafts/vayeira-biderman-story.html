The Rebbe Left the Door Open


A man came to one of the tzaddikim and poured out his heart. He was badly afflicted with anger, he said, and he could not get the better of it.


The tzaddik told him to wait outside the room.


A little later the man heard the Rebbe’s voice through the door, raised and perfectly clear, giving the gabbai his instructions. Bring in a glass of tea. On your way past him, contrive to spill some of it on his clothes, and give him a good shove while you are at it.


The gabbai came out and did exactly that. The man did not get angry. He did not raise his voice, and he did not let the gabbai feel that anything had happened at all. The tzaddik sent him out to do it again, and again, and every time the man answered the same way.


Then the tzaddik called him in. “I do not see that you are a baal kaas at all. The gabbai provoked you several times just now, and every time you answered him with respect and derech eretz.”


“What was I supposed to do,” the man said. “The Rebbe spoke a little too loudly and the door was open a crack. I heard, and I knew the Rebbe was putting me to a nisayon. How was I going to walk into a trap that had been laid in front of me? I would have made a laughingstock of myself if I had gotten angry.”


“Then from now on,” the tzaddik said, “remember this always. Every nisayon you have is prepared and sent to you from Shamayim, every detail of it with wondrous hashgacha, to see how you overcome it and grow stronger. And when you think that way, it will always be very easy for you to overcome.”


A well known chasidic story, heard from R’ Elimelech Biderman

NOTES (NOT FOR PRINT)

Where it is from: Printed in Hebrew in a parsha pamphlet, told in the name of R’ Elimelech Biderman: הסיפור הראשון, מפי המשפיע הרה"צ רּ אלימלך בידרמן שליט"א. Transcription and the two parallel printings are in sources-inbox/trick-and-treat-printed-versions.md. The sefer name is cut off the left edge of the screenshot, so the credit line names the teller and no sefer. That is honest and it is how the seforim themselves carry it.

The tzaddik is unnamed and stays unnamed. He is אחד הצדיקים in every printed version. Nobody’s name goes on the closing line.

The English is mine, not supplied copy. It is a rendering of the printed Hebrew, kept close to it. The loshon kodesh the reader already owns is kept rather than glossed: nisayon, hashgacha, baal kaas, derech eretz, gabbai, tzaddik.

Why the last sentence runs whole. An Akeida issue built on nisayon is one step from telling a man who fell that he failed a test Hashem set him. The Hebrew carries its own answer to that and it is in the final clause, וכשתחשוב כן תמיד יהיה לך קל מאוד להתגבר, it becomes very easy. Cutting the clause would leave only the demand. It stays.

Still open. Whether this is the dvar Torah itself or a story inside one, which decides whether an Akeida frame gets written around it. Asked, not answered.
