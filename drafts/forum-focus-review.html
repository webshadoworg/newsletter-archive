Every year this question turns up on the forum a few days after Yom Tov, when it is too late to do anything about it. So we are running it a week early instead.

@poiuytrewq asked:

I just came off of a beautiful Rosh Hashanah. I felt like I was on a spiritual high and then as soon as Rosh Hashanah ended I cracked. Does anyone have any insight?

@HashemHelpMe responded:

This is a very common occurrence. Many of us here do not have the skill of regulating our emotions in a healthy manner. We trained ourselves to take all that built up high/euphoria/dveikus and release it all by masturbating. We have to give ourselves other tools. Secondly, when a yom tov leaves, we find people are generally more anxious (see Aruch Hashulchan who says on Motzai Shabbos, the body senses the loss of the neshomo yeseira and becomes uneasy), and spiritually vulnerable. Prepare yourself, say a tfilah to Hashem and iyh you will be fine.

@grateful4life answered from a 12-step perspective:

Your experience is very common among serious strugglers, and it was especially challenging for me too... better yet it was a demoralizing and hopeless phenomenon for me... feeling that I was so high and so close to Hashem and then BOOM - only to spiral completely out of control as if Elul and Tishrei occurred 10 months ago, not 10 days ago or 10 hours (minutes) ago. This happened to me year after year.

Now being part of a mostly frum live meeting in SA, I've noticed how prevalent it is that people relapse after Yomim Tovim.

So today I avoid getting too high during the Yomim Noraim because I just don't have a good parachute to land safely back down. Your problem is that you are like the cartoons with people attached to a propelled rocket that runs out of fuel mid-air and suddenly everyone is crashing down from the sky. In cartoons they usually end up ok but in real life it hurts! And what do we do when we feel we're missing something or something hurts us - we turn to our drug to numb out the pain, hence the vicious cycle continues...

In a 12 step program we are seriously doing teshuva and growing spiritually on a daily basis so Elul/Tishrei is no different but regardless try to treat these days as "regular" days and don't get too high on the avodas hayom. Hashem wants you to stay clean above anything else so try this method and see how it goes. Maybe in a few years down the road you'll get back to that intensity when you can handle it but for now just keep it even keeled.

A · The column as it is now602 words, 7 paragraphs — this is what is in the issue right now.

For a lot of members, Rosh Hashana arrives with a heavy heart. You're standing before the King as the Day of Judgment, and part of you is sure you're walking in as a failure, undeserving of a good year. The forum knows this feeling intimately, and it has a great deal of hope to offer. Here's the chevra's wisdom.

First, be honest, with yourself and with Hashem. The shame is real, and pretending it away doesn't help, but neither does letting it crush you. The way out isn't to deny the feeling but to bring it, honestly, to Hashem. @unanumun offers a beautifully low bar to start from: "Perhaps for this Rosh Hashana it will be enough to honestly come to the realization that your life isn't manageable and you need to do something concrete to change it." You don't have to walk in transformed. You just have to walk in honest.

Second, don't try to swallow the whole year at once. The image of a perfect year ahead is paralyzing, and it's the wrong focus. @cordnoy gives the sharpest reframe: "I wouldn't focus on next year. Tell Hashem that you'd like to be sober right now, and that itself should lift your spirits." Ask for today. Rosh Hashana isn't a contract you sign for twelve flawless months; it's a chance to turn toward Hashem in this moment.

Third, understand the pattern, so it doesn't break you. Many members know the specific heartbreak of feeling sky-high and close to Hashem over Rosh Hashana, and then crashing right afterward. @grateful4life named it painfully well: "I felt so high and so close to Hashem, and then BOOM, I'd spiral out of control, as if Elul and Tishrei happened ten months ago and not ten days ago. This happened to me year after year." If that's your story too, know that it doesn't erase the height you reached. @i-man offers a way to read it: "Maybe the satan is just fighting back really hard, the more kedusha, the more the tumah pushes. Keep in mind that right now Hashem is very close and listening, so give an extra cry to Him." The crash isn't proof the climb was fake. It's proof it was real enough to be worth attacking.

And there's a deeper idea here that transforms the whole struggle. @battleworn brings Rav Tzadok HaKohen (Tzidkas HaTzadik 111): in the era before Moshiach, the main avodah is to extract the precious from the worthless, and specifically from the place of the yetzer hara and the increase of sins, "from there He will take you, specifically through teshuvah." In other words, the very area of your hardest struggle is precisely where your greatest teshuvah and closeness to Hashem are waiting to be drawn out. Your battlefield is not a disqualification. It's the raw material.

Finally, turn the feeling into action. @markz puts it bluntly: when you face a personal challenge, you can't just say "I tried, I cried, I prayed", you have to do something.

The bottom line? You don't approach Rosh Hashana by pretending you're someone you're not. You approach it honestly: bring the shame to Hashem instead of letting it drive you, ask only for today, know that the highs and crashes are part of a real fight and not a verdict on it, remember that your hardest struggle is exactly where your teshuvah lives, and take one concrete step with the chevra behind you. You're not standing there as a failure. You're standing there as a Yid who's still fighting, and that is precisely the kind of person Hashem is listening for.
