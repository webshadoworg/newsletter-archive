Yitzchak’s Own Lech Lecha


The parsha opens with Hashem telling Avraham, לֶךְ לְךָ מֵאַרְצְךָ וּמִמּוֹלַדְתְּךָ וּמִבֵּית אָבִיךָ, “Go for yourself from your land, from your birthplace and from your father’s house” (Bereishis 12:1). Avraham leaves Terach’s avodah zarah behind for a road nobody had walked before.


Interestingly, Yitzchak also made a lech lecha of his own, even though his father’s house was the best there was. Avraham’s middah was chesed, and Yitzchak’s was gevurah, its opposite. He didn’t reject anything Avraham taught him, he found his own way of serving the same Hashem.


Why did Yitzchak need his own derech? Rav Dessler answers that otherwise his avodah would have been “a mere repetition of his father’s avodah.” Hashem wanted each of the Avos to bring a new aspect of avodas Hashem into the world (Michtav MeEliyahu vol. 2 p. 164).


A tzaddik the son of a tzaddik, Rav Dessler explains, has a danger of his own: what he was handed can stay a habit. His maalah comes when he works to turn it into an inner kinyan, “like a man who discovers it all afresh” (ibid. vol. 2 p. 205, vol. 3 p. 124).


Many of us grew up in good homes, full of Torah and mitzvos, and still find ourselves struggling. This might be the reason why. There is a purpose in the struggle: it pushes us to dig deeper and take our connection with Hashem beyond anything we learned or knew before.


As many of our members can attest, breaking free from lust takes more than what we were handed. It sends a man deep inside himself, and his connection with Hashem goes to a whole new level, more real than what he had to begin with. He goes into a mitzvah further than anyone taught him and finds a depth he never knew was there.


This week, pick one bracha you’ve said every morning since cheder. Say it slowly, word by word, as a man standing before Hashem and discovering it afresh.
