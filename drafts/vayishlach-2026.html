Why Do You Ask My Name?

Based on a post by @LivingInspiration


After wrestling with the angel throughout the night, Yaakov asked him for his name. The angel responded, “Why do you ask my name?” What lies behind this seemingly unusual exchange?


Rav Chaim Shmulevitz explains that Yaakov was confronting the yetzer hara, represented by the angel of Eisav. By asking for its name, Yaakov sought to understand its nature and how it manages to entice people to sin.


The angel did in fact answer the question. His response, “Why do you ask my name?” reveals something fundamental about the yetzer hara: it does not want us to stop and examine what we are doing. Its strength lies in keeping us constantly occupied, rushing from one thing to the next without pausing to consider our actions or their consequences. The yetzer hara’s motto is “Don’t think.” The moment we pause to examine our actions and consider their significance, its hold over us begins to weaken. (Mussarei Shaarei Chaim, Vayishlach)


A person who never takes time to think can stay stuck in the same place for years. Stepping back to think does not require much time. A few quiet minutes, with nothing else running and nobody else in the room, are enough to look honestly at where we stand: what we should be doing, what we could be doing, and what might be worth doing differently.


“Thinking deeply about yourself for 15 minutes will get you further in avodas Hashem than fasting from sunrise to sunset.”

— R’ Shmuel of Lubavitch


FROM OUR HEBREW EDITION

Gib Him!

Rabbi Yitzchak Dovid Grossman rang the bell of the big villa a second time. Nothing. He looked at his watch. The meeting with Mister Moses had been set for nine, and nine was a while ago now.


Then the buzzer went and the door opened. Mister Moses was in an armchair with his back to the door, staring at a large screen, so far inside it that he never heard anyone come in.


Two boxers were going at each other. Mister Moses was tense. He badly wanted one of them to win. Every time his man landed a punch he shouted him on, and every time his man took one he protested out loud.


Then his boxer took a bad hit and went down, with the fight gone out of his face. Mister Moses was on his feet, screaming with everything he had: “Gib him! Gib him!”


When it finished he turned around and saw the Rav waiting behind him. “Rabbi Grossman, I am so sorry. I have a lot of money riding on this.”


“Don’t apologize,” said Rabbi Grossman. “I learned something tonight. How many people are watching this fight?”


“Millions.”


“And if only one boxer had come to the ring and the other one had stayed home, would millions have watched that?”


“Of course not,” said Mister Moses. “Nobody watches one man in a ring. It is only a fight because there are two of them.”


“That is exactly it,” said Rabbi Grossman. “The Ribbono shel Olam has endless malachim. What interests Him is the fight inside a person, between the yetzer tov and the yetzer hara. And when He sees a Yid fighting his yetzer, He is standing there shouting, Gib him!” (Ohr HaShabbos p. 71)


In this week’s parsha, Yaakov is in a long, hard fight with an angel. When the angel sees he is not going to win it, he strikes Yaakov in the socket of his thigh, and the Radak says that is the place that stands for the power of having children.


That fight did not end with Yaakov. We are people who want to live right, and the yetzer usually cannot take us head-on, so he goes for the one place he knows is tender.


It does not always look the same, though. The baalei mussar write that there are two different yetzer haras. One is the ordinary one all of us know, sitting inside our own heart, the middos every person has to work on his whole life.


The other is a different order of thing altogether. An angel with enormous strength, from outside the man, working on him with tactics, until it “sometimes succeeds in bringing a person to sins far below his usual level” (Michtav MeEliyahu vol. 5 p. 23).


Two Levels of Struggle


At GYE we see both of these every day. There is the regular level of struggle, and there is true addiction, and each one needs a different kind of help.


For the regular struggle, the GYE tools work: motivation, planning and connection. Connection means the forum, a mentor, or one person who knows where you are holding and notices when you go quiet. Most people who take themselves and the program seriously succeed.


True addiction is a different story. If it is running your life and not getting easier, even though you have been using the GYE tools and working with a mentor, you might need a different class of help. That alone does not prove you are an addict. It could be that you just need to do a better job with the tools. But it is a sign worth taking seriously.


How do you know which one you are facing? That is what GYE’s free consultation is for. For true addiction, what we have found is that the 12-step program is the most effective path, and Efraim can help you work out whether that is where you are holding and what your next step should be. Write to efraim@guardyoureyes.org.


Either way, whichever of the two you are fighting this week, Hashem is watching, and He is on His feet. Gib him!


FROM A MEMBER

“It did not make any sense to work this time”

Based on a forum post by @thistimeillwin


One year ago, on 12 Kislev, I started another of the hundreds of attempts in the past 30+ years. An attempt to rid myself once and for all of the P & M which was dragging me down and destroying my life (ruchniyus and gashmiyus). Somehow, as the song goes ‘this time it will be was different.’ and I soared to heights beyond belief, with now one full year free of the garbage I have been dealing with for most of my life.


As I’ve mentioned before, it did not make any sense to work this time. One of my biggest triggers, stress, was at an all time high. Financial stress was at a peak, and was dealing also with a personal crisis. Family life was also facing huge challenges, as I’ve detailed in the BB Forum. Yet, with all that, I was able to reach goals I never have before, and not for lack of trying. It is truly a gift from Hashem, who has seen the number and level of attempts and decided that it was time. He also eased one trigger for me, which was my Shalom Bayis, which suddenly took an about-face and got much better (now when the bickering starts, I try to control myself saying my year of sobriety depends on this). I also met and spoke with a few mentors, though not as much as I should, but it made the fight not as lonely.


Since my initial 90-day journey, I have been mostly in cruise-control, with white-knuckle days lessening, and getting used to a life without P & M. I know I have much more to work on, in my battle against lust and in my general ruchniyus wellbeing, but the challenges of my day-to-day life have left those mostly unattended. Today will hopefully be a wakeup call for me that I can’t sit on my laurels and declare victory; the clock is ticking and I need to get to the next phase of the battle.


I have been largely absent from the Forum, for a few reasons. The Forum had become an obsession (maybe taking the place of my P & M obsession - better, but not healthy). I was checking on it multiple times a day and reading every post, and it was affecting my work. And work got very busy, so I dropped off for a while, just logging in to update my count.


In my personal life, things have eased a bit. My financial issues peaked sometime over the last year, but have pulled back from the brink for the time being. I have come to terms with some personal challenges. And family life has also regulated a bit, though not fully to where I would like it. In summary, it’s normal living.


Hashem should help me continue on the path to וטהר לבנו לעבדך באמת, and should help all of the chevra here who are trying. Remember, I was at it for close to 35 years, was falling multiple times a day, tried stopping hundreds of times. If I can do it, you can too! Hashem will see all your failed attempts and at a certain point say אפתח לו פתח כפתחו של אולם.

He Climbed Over the Garbage Bag

Based on a shiur by Rabbi YY Jacobson


A woman at a marriage workshop told this story about her husband. He leaves for shul before anyone else in the house is awake, and every night she asks him to take the garbage out on his way. Every night he forgets. So one night she set the bag right up against the front door, where he could not possibly miss it. In the morning the door was open, the house was quiet, and the bag was still standing there. He had climbed over it and gone to shul.


She told it laughing. He is a professor, she said. That is how absent-minded he is.


A woman sitting across the room was not laughing. She said it was not funny. She said it was abusive.


Same bag, same door, same tired husband stepping over it in the dark. Two women heard two different marriages in it.


The difference was not in the husbands. The woman who laughed knew, without needing to check, that her husband loved her, so a forgotten bag was a forgotten bag. The other woman did not have that. In a marriage where you are not sure you are loved, nothing is ever only itself. A bag by the door becomes a message. A quiet evening becomes a verdict. “When are you coming home?” becomes an accusation.


Dan l’kaf zechus, judging a person favorably, is one of the first things a Jewish child is taught. What is strange is how hard it is to do at home, for the one person who has earned it more than anybody. It comes easier for a stranger in shul than at your own kitchen table.


The reason is uncomfortable. You cannot accept from your wife a better opinion of you than the one you hold of yourself. A man who has quietly decided that he is failing her does not hear it when she says otherwise. The words go past him. What he listens for is the criticism, and he finds it, because a man who is looking for it will find it in anything.


This gets heavier when you are carrying something you are ashamed of. You are certain that if she knew all of it she would think less of you, so her ordinary sentences arrive in the voice of the judgment you have already passed on yourself. You spend the evening defending yourself against a charge she never made.


So try this. The next time something she says stings, do not answer it yet. Say back to yourself only the words she actually used. Not the tone you heard in them, not what you decided they meant about you. Just the words. Most of the time you will find the sting was not in them.


It works the same way with Hashem. In a week when you have fallen and you do not think much of yourself, a quiet stretch feels like He has finally had enough of you. He has not. You are hearing your own opinion of yourself and putting it in His mouth.


The bag by the door is only a bag by the door. Everything else you heard in it, you brought.
