Bringing Kedusha Into Our Homes

Just in Time for Rosh Hashana, GYE Launches Project Kedusha

It is Sunday night in Flatbush, and a mother is sitting at her kitchen table with a list.

Her daughter’s bas mitzvah is in two months, and she has been asking to upgrade her phone. The question can no longer be pushed off. Which phone? With which filter? At what age, and with what rules? Her husband heard of a few options, but he is not sure which one is best, and the school sent home a letter that answered none of it. She is a capable woman who has organized simchos, budgets, and carpools. Tonight she has a simple, universal question, and no idea where the answer lives.

She is not in crisis. She is every one of us.

Questions like hers reach Guard Your Eyes every single day, from every kind of family:

A rebbi in Flatbush: “I need help with a Talmid.” A mother in Yerushalayim: “We can’t afford a filter.” A father in Monsey: “Is there a kosher way for my kids to use AI for school?” A mother in Boro Park: “There are so many kosher phones. What’s the difference between them, and which one is most recommended?” Another father: “When and how should I speak to my boys about Kedusha?” A bochur, alone, at two in the morning: “I gave my word to my Rav and broke it again.”

Some of these questions are painful. Most are simply practical. There is the work laptop the children sometimes borrow, which cannot be locked down without blocking the sites you need for parnassa. There are parental controls that are switched on, and a twelve-year-old who worked around them in an afternoon. There is a Motzaei Shabbos with bored children and nothing kosher to put on for them. There is the father with a kosher smartphone who reaches for it every free minute and realizes he is not fully present for his own children. There is even the family with no internet at all, who still has to pay a bill and order a ride.

Kedusha and technology touch every single Jewish home today. There is no family that is spared, because these are no longer questions for “people who struggle.” They are questions for people who have children, jobs, and devices, which is to say, everyone. And until now, every family answered them the same way: alone, quickly, and hoping for the best.

Every Answer Exists. Nobody Could Find It.

Here is the strange part. Klal Yisroel is blessed with remarkable resources for every one of these questions. Filters, kosher phones, curriculums, chizuk programs, therapists, hotlines. Nothing is missing except a way to find them, and to know which one fits your specific situation.

Centuries ago, a Yid with a practical question in halacha faced something similar. The answers existed, scattered across Bavli, Yerushalmi and the Rishonim, but most people had no way to find them, until Rav Yosef Karo organized the practical halacha into the Shulchan Aruch. Lehavdil, that is precisely what has been missing in this generation’s greatest challenge: one address where any Yid can bring any question about technology and Kedusha, and walk away with a clear, practical answer suited to his situation.

At GYE, we stopped asking what else we should build. Instead the question became: why does that address not exist?

This Elul, it does. Project Kedusha launches in three parts:

The Kedusha Guide, in print. Kedusha.com, the same library alive online, powered by AI. And 866-KEDUSHA, all the answers just a phone call away; perfect for anyone, but especially for those without internet.

One address. All of Klal Yisroel’s solutions in one place. Free, anonymous, any hour of the day or night.

What’s Actually in It

The Kedusha Guide runs close to 100 full-color pages, the first of its kind: close to 200 vetted resources from dozens of organizations, organized into six sections.

Education runs by age, from a book for a four-year-old to pre-marriage material, with school curriculums and resources for mechanchim. Chizuk offers videos, WhatsApp groups, seforim and shul-wide programs. Struggles offers GYE’s full range of tools for those who need them, including mentoring, spousal support and frum therapy referrals.

The largest section, and perhaps the most practical, is Kedusha Safeguards. Every filter in America and Israel, compared side by side. All popular kosher phones, by region and community standard. Routers, accountability software, the new kosher AI tools. All the worldwide TAG offices, plus Project STEP for anyone who needs help setting up a filter or covering its cost. Even the devices people forget: the Xbox, the smart TV, the Tesla. And call-in lines for rides, bills and email, for families with no internet at all.

Screen Addiction is the section nobody expects, because it is not about content at all. It offers community initiatives, apps that help people cut down, lock boxes and timers to put some healthy distance between a person and his screen. And Kosher Aisle gives your bored children safe alternatives: kosher music, kosher entertainment, phone hotlines, and protected media devices.

Wherever you see the GYE badge, it is ours. Everything else is the work of others, and that is the point. This is not about one organization. It is all of Klal Yisroel’s solutions under one roof.

Rav Yosef Elefant of the Mir called the Kedusha Guide a “game changer.” The goal is thousands of copies in shuls, schools and homes across America and Eretz Yisroel. Because when this book sits openly on a table, it normalizes the conversation. No searching. No shame.

You Can Just Ask

A printed book is fixed the day it leaves the press, but the solutions themselves are constantly changing. Filters update, kosher phone models come and go, new tools appear every month. That is why the same library lives at Kedusha.com and on the 866-KEDUSHA hotline, where Rabbi Leizerson of Mishor in Lakewood personally oversees the work of keeping every solution current. When a new filter launches, a kosher phone model changes, or a new resource becomes available, Kedusha.com already knows.

And the website & hotline do something no book has ever done. It answers you.

Remember our mother at the kitchen table? She types one sentence: “My daughter wants to upgrade her phone for her bas mitzvah.” What comes back is not a page of search results. The site, powered by GYE’s proprietary AI database, responds the way a wise mentor would. It asks her age and the family’s community standards. It narrows two hundred resources down to the three that fit this home, and it even suggests how the conversation with her daughter should sound.

The mother in Yerushalayim gets offered Israeli filters, not American ones. The father with the work laptop gets a different answer than the father with a smartphone. Anonymously, instantly, and without judgment. Every conversation is reviewed by our team, so the site keeps learning and shapes the next edition of the Guide.

Harav Simcha Bunim Cohen, Rav of Kahal Ateres Yeshaya in Lakewood, put it memorably:

“Now that AI came up, which is another tool of the Yetzer Hara, Project Kedusha is teaching us how to use it.”

Yaakov Nadel, GYE’s founder, explains the philosophy:

“A man will type at two in the morning what he could never say out loud at two in the afternoon. Our only job is to make sure he gets the best resources possible, in the most empathetic and Torahdik way, and that a real human is standing right behind it.”

Nineteen Years to Get Here

Guard Your Eyes is nineteen years old, founded by Yaakov Nadel and Yechezkel Stelzer with the guidance of Rabbi Dr. Abraham Twerski zt”l and, yibadel lechaim, Rav Aharon Feldman shlita. Tens of thousands have broken free using GYE’s three pillars of Motivation, Planning and Connection. And behind every number stand marriages rebuilt, self-respect restored, and fathers who are present again for their families.

But prevention, helping people before they fall, was never an afterthought. Back in 2013 GYE helped build the VCF, the precursor to GenTech, built Webshadow for parent-child accountability, launched community Filterthon events, and published filter guides and prevention tips for parents. That work continues today through Project STEP, which finds a sponsor for a filter when cost is the obstacle; the 30-day Kedusha Challenge, which Rabbanim have run in more than forty shuls this past year; and the Esa Einai program, that has helped transform the winter z’man for thousands of teens during the weeks of Shovavim.

Harav Uri Deutsch, Mara D’asra of Forest Park, Lakewood:

“I was very moved and extremely impressed by this new effort, both the printed and the electronic resources, to be mechazek what we know is the nisayon of klal yisrael in our time.”

Removing the Shame

For years, GYE was identified with crisis, and crisis is hard to speak about. Many people never reached out at all because of stigma. Project Kedusha changes the conversation, because the Guide belongs on every Jewish bookshelf. Not because something is wrong in the house, but because every family is navigating technology today.

Elul is the season of kabbalos, but our kabbalos rarely survive Cheshvan. Here is one that will. Pick one thing in your house: a conversation you keep putting off, a device with no real filter, or a new chizuk program. The Guide will tell you exactly how.

Somewhere tonight, that mother is back at her kitchen table. This time she is reading an answer, written for her daughter, her community, her home. That is the news of this Elul: for the first time, every family has a place to ask.
