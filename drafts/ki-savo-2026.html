PARSHAS KI SAVO

The Big Bikkurim Speech

The parsha opens with bikkurim, and it’s easy to picture it as a farming mitzvah: the first figs ripen, you carry them up to Yerushalayim. But bikkurim is really two mitzvos. There’s bringing the fruit, and there’s mikra bikkurim, the declaration you make out loud while you’re standing there holding it. The fruit by itself isn’t the mitzvah. The fruit plus the speech is the mitzvah. And it isn’t a quick thank you. Look at what the declaration actually says. Not “thank You for the fruit,” but the reason: we went down to Mitzrayim, we were afflicted, we cried out, He took us out with a strong hand and brought us to this place, and this is what grew here. The mitzvah is saying why.


While real bikkurim is only done in the Beis Hamikdosh, the idea behind it – Hakaras Hatov and gratitude, and making a ceremony out of it – is an amazing tool that is especially useful for those of us who are struggling: writing down, every day, a list of things you’re grateful for. Open each line however you like. “I’m grateful for…” “Thank You for…” “Modeh ani lefanecha that…” The wording matters very little. Doing it daily matters a lot. And it works even when it feels forced at the start, which for most people it does.


Researchers at the University of Pennsylvania asked people to write down three things that had gone well that day, and why they went well – who gets the credit for it – every night for one week. One week, and then nothing. A month later they were happier than when they started. Six months later they still were.


Notice what they were asked to write: three things, and who gets credit for them so that it’s more detailed. That’s also an idea we see with bikkurim. It wasn’t just “Thank you Hashem for these figs.” It included the full story going back to Lavan and Mitzrayim, all the suffering we went through, so that it’s clear why having figs from our very own Eretz Yisroel is such a big deal.


Birkos Hashachar already includes brachos for opening our eyes, for standing up straight, for the ground under our feet. But when we add our own original thank yous, it makes the gratitude feel more real. And when you elaborate on how you’re grateful to Hashem for this gift, it forces you to think, and deepens your appreciation.


A gratitude list does nothing for your willpower. What it changes is your mood, and your mood is where the pull actually comes from. Most of us don’t go looking for taavah when we feel good. We go looking when we feel bad, and taavah is what’s offering comfort. Feeling bad is the opening it needs.


Kvetching is a small announcement that things are not good, and makes us feel like we need relief. And that invites acting out. Tilt the day the other way and it runs the other way with it: gratitude leaves a person happier, and a happier person is much harder to catch.


And that is where the fight really is. Sit inside gratitude and you are standing close to Hashem, where “oz v’chedvah bimkomo,” strength and joy are in His place.


Bikkurim isn’t brought at the end of the harvest, it’s the reishis, the very first fruit. So write your list in the morning, that way the day starts on a good note instead of only ending on one.


A member of a 12-step group wrote his list every day for months without missing, including the nights he remembered only at two in the morning and fell asleep partway through it. “After seven months like that I felt an enormous change. Before, no matter how good things were around me, there was a point inside me that always felt bad, always unsatisfied. Now it’s the reverse. Even when things around me are very hard, that inner point feels good, and it gives me the strength to get through everything.”

7 KEYS TO A STRONGER MARRIAGE · KEY #3

Turn Toward Each Other, Not Away

Adapted from the research of Dr. John Gottman


The first two keys were about knowing your spouse and nurturing admiration for them. This week’s key is about the small, ordinary moments where a marriage is actually built, or quietly eroded.


Gottman discovered something that surprised even him. The strength of a marriage isn’t decided mainly in the big, dramatic conversations. It’s decided in the tiny, almost invisible moments throughout an ordinary day, moments he called “bids for connection.” A bid is any small gesture that says, in effect, “notice me, be with me for a second.” Your wife points out a bird at the window. Your husband sighs over a headline. One of them says, “look at this,” or “how was your day?” These look like nothing. They’re everything.


In each of these moments, you have a choice: to turn toward the bid, by responding, engaging, showing you’re there, or to turn away, by ignoring it, brushing it off, or staying buried in your phone. Gottman found that in the marriages that thrived, spouses turned toward each other’s bids the overwhelming majority of the time. In the marriages that fell apart, they turned away far more often. And it usually wasn’t the huge betrayals that did the damage, it was the steady accumulation of small moments where one partner reached out and the other wasn’t there.


The Torah’s “emotional bank account”


Gottman describes this as building up an “emotional bank account”, every time you turn toward a bid, you make a small deposit, and over years those deposits become a reserve of goodwill and trust that carries a couple through hard times. This is a deeply Torah idea. The Sefer HaChinuch writes that a person is shaped by the accumulation of small actions far more than by grand gestures, “acharei hapeulos nimshachim halevavos,” the heart is drawn after the deeds, and the deeds that matter most are the repeated small ones. A marriage, like a person, is built from the little things done again and again.


There’s a beautiful hint in how the Torah describes the ideal marriage: reyim ahuvim, beloved friends. Think about what actually makes a friendship close. It’s rarely one dramatic event. It’s the hundred small moments of showing up, of being interested, of turning toward. The same is true, even more so, of the friendship at the heart of a marriage.


Small and often beats big and rare


Here’s the freeing part of Gottman’s finding: you don’t need grand romantic gestures to build a strong marriage. In fact, couples who rely only on the occasional big gesture, the anniversary dinner, the expensive gift, while turning away in the daily moments, tend to struggle. What builds lasting closeness is the small stuff, done consistently. A two-second response. Looking up when they walk in. Asking a follow-up question. Putting the phone down when they start to talk. These cost almost nothing, and they are the actual currency of a marriage.


Your Action Steps This Week


  1. Notice the bids. This week, just pay attention to how often your spouse makes a small bid for your attention, a comment, a question, a “look at this.” You’ll likely be surprised how many there are once you’re watching for them.
  2. Turn toward, especially when it’s inconvenient. Pick the moments you’d normally brush off, when you’re tired, mid-task, on your phone, and turn toward instead. Look up. Respond. Give the two seconds. Those are the deposits that count most.
  3. Put the phone down first. The single biggest source of “turning away” today is a screen. When your spouse starts to talk, make it your habit to physically put the phone down and face them. Nothing says “you matter to me” faster.

JOYFUL TESHUVA · PART 3

How Teshuva Can Backfire

Last week in this series we quoted Rabbeinu Yonah that when doing Teshuva for addictive behaviors, the first step should be how to break free. Charata (remorse) over the past can wait until we’ve reached stability with avoiding the old patterns.


Now suppose you’re already clean for 3-6 months? Is it time now to focus on the past? And if yes, how should it be done?


In this week’s newsletter we bring you some surprising guidance on this topic from Gedolei Yisrael.


1. Thinking about the past should be done on rare occasions


Rav Nosson Meir Wachtfogel zt’l, the Lakewood Mashgiach, quoting R’ Yeruchom Levovitz zt’l (Leket Reshimos, Elul):


כל השנה אפילו שהאדם נכשל צריך לחשוב הלאה על העתיד, ולהתקדם ולא לחשוב על העבר, רק יום אחד בשנה מותר לאדם לחשוב על העבר - ביום הכפורים... וביום זה מזכירים בוידויים כ”כ הרבה נפילות עם פרטים פרטים, ואלמלי קדושת היום ששומרת על האדם היה נהפך למושחת. וגם זה מותר רק מחמת החידוש של תשובה, אבל כל השנה המהלך הוא להמשיך הלאה ולהתקדם, ולא להתעסק עם העבר, וזה חולשה של בני אדם לחיות עם העבר.


All year long, even when a person has fallen, he has to think forward, about the future, and keep moving ahead, and not think about the past. Only one day a year is a person permitted to think about the past, and that is Yom Kippur... And on that day we list in the vidui so many falls, in detail after detail, and were it not for the holiness of the day protecting him, a person would be harmed by it. Even that is only permitted because of the special power of teshuva. But all year long, the way is to keep going forward and to keep moving ahead, and not to occupy yourself with the past. Living with the past is a human weakness.


The Baal HaTanya (Tanya Ch 26) teaches that feeling bitter about the past needs to be confined to scheduled slots of time, when you have a clear head to do proper Teshuva. Because if the feelings of bitterness come up at random times, like during learning Torah, davening or even neutral times like work – it will only drag you down, and serve the Yetzer Hara’s agenda. Even the scheduled slots should be focused: You sit down, feel remorse as best you can, and then have full Bitachon that Hashem has forgiven you, and there’s nothing left to be bitter about.


2. When it comes to Kedusha-related behaviors, there are even more restrictions


The Steipler zt’l wrote:


מכתבך קבלתי והנני משתתף בצערך ומקוה שהשי”ת יעזרך במשך הזמן להנצל מחטאות נעורים ר”ל. במה שנוגע על העבר אין לחשוב לעת עתה כלל, ועיקר העיקרים הוא לעסוק בתורה... עוד עיקר גדול שלא להיות עצב ולא לחשוב אודות העבר כלל עד אחר נשואין שאז יש ליתן לב לתקן העבר.


I received your letter, and I share in your pain, and I hope that Hashem will help you over time to be saved from the sins of youth. As for what concerns the past, there is no need to think about it at all for now, and the most essential thing of all is to be occupied with Torah... Another great fundamental: not to be sad, and not to think about the past at all until after marriage, at which point it is worth turning your attention to setting the past right.


Why wait so long? Because for a bochur, thinking about what we did in the past, even in the context of Teshuva, has a risk of getting us stimulated and triggered, which then puts us at risk of relapsing!


Similarly, the Lubavitcher Rebbe zt’l wrote in two separate letters:


...ובהנוגע לתיקון חטא הידוע, הנה צריך להרבות באותיות התורה ובאותיות התפלה... ולהסיח דעתו מהנ”ל, אפילו מתיקונו, עד לאחר הנשואין בשעה טובה ומוצלחת.


...And with regard to the tikkun for the known sin: he should increase in Torah and tefillah... and take his mind off the above, even off its tikkun, until after his marriage, in a good and successful hour.


יסיח דעתו מענין זה לע”ע, היינו לא רק מהענין בעצמו אלא גם מהתבוננות בגודל החטא ובנחיצות התיקון. ויסיח דעתו מכ”ז לגמרי. ויתקע דעתו בחוזק בלימוד ובתפלה. מובן שכל הנ”ל צ”ל בשמחה (אף שמבואר בספרים באופן אחר, הנה דרך זה יניח כעבור איזה שנים, ועתה יסיח דעתו מכל הענין ויתחזק לעבוד את ה’ מתוך שמחה דוקא).


He should take his mind off this matter for now. That means not only off the matter itself, but also off contemplating the gravity of the sin and the urgency of putting it right. He should take his mind off all of it completely, and fix his mind firmly on learning and on tefillah. It goes without saying that all of the above has to be done with simcha. (Even though the sefarim explain it differently, he will set this approach aside after some years; for now he should take his mind off the whole matter and strengthen himself to serve Hashem specifically out of joy.)


Igros Kodesh vol. 8 pp. 284-5 and vol. 4 p. 364.



Bottom line: Focusing on the past needs to wait until you’re sure you can do it without getting triggered by it, and even then, it should only be done on Yom Kippur or other special occasions. Teshuva itself is covered by working on breaking free, and doing other positive actions, especially an increase in Torah learning.


(Just to be clear, working on stopping does require thinking about recent slips so that you can learn lessons from them. But that type of thinking about the past is more technical. It’s about the circumstances that led to the fall, and a down to earth problem-solving approach: what time did I slip? What device was it? What triggered it? What mistakes led to it? How can I improve my plan? The focus is not on evoking deep remorse, and not on replaying exactly what the slip entailed.)
