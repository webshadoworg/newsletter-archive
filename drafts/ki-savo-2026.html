PARSHAS KI SAVO

Bikkurim Comes With a Speech

The parsha opens with bikkurim, and it’s easy to picture it as a farming mitzvah: the first figs ripen, you carry them up to Yerushalayim. But bikkurim is really two mitzvos. There’s bringing the fruit, and there’s mikra bikkurim, the declaration you make out loud while you’re standing there holding it. The fruit by itself isn’t the mitzvah. The fruit plus the speech is the mitzvah. And it isn’t a quick thank you. Look at what the declaration actually says. Not “thank You for the fruit,” but the reason: we went down to Mitzrayim, we were afflicted, we cried out, He took us out with a strong hand and brought us to this place, and this is what grew here. The mitzvah is saying why.


That’s worth more to us than it looks. One of the strongest tools anyone in this fight has is gratitude on purpose: writing down, every day, a list of things you’re grateful for. Open each line however you like. “I’m grateful for…” “Thank You for…” “Modeh ani lefanecha that…” The wording matters very little. Doing it daily matters a lot. And it works even when it feels forced at the start, which for most people it does.


It’s also one of the very few things in this territory that has actually been measured. Researchers at the University of Pennsylvania asked people to write down three things that had gone well that day, and why, every night for one week. One week, and then nothing. A month later they were happier and less depressed than when they started. Six months later they still were.


Notice what they were asked to write: three things, and why. That’s the same point bikkurim is making. The general thank yous are covered for us already; the whole morning is built out of them, brachos for opening our eyes, for standing up straight, for the ground under our feet. What the siddur can’t supply is your own line, about something you noticed today, with one word attached to it: because. Not “I’m grateful for my home.” “I’m grateful for my home, because…” and then finish the sentence. What is it about this one that you appreciate? Answering that takes a minute of actual thinking. A list you rattle off is a routine. A list you have to think about does something to you.


Why should a list on a piece of paper do anything at all? Because of where the pull actually comes from. Most of us don’t go looking for taavah when we feel good. We go looking when we feel bad, and taavah is what’s offering comfort. The bad feeling is the delivery mechanism.


Which puts kvetching in a rather different light. A running complaint about the traffic, the job, the tone somebody used with us, feels like nothing. It’s a mood, not an aveirah. But every kvetch is a small announcement that things are not good, and a man who has been making those announcements since morning is a man already looking for some relief. That is the mood the whole thing waits for, because the relief on offer is giving in. Tilt the day the other way and it runs the other way with it: gratitude leaves a person happier, and a happier person is much harder to catch.


And that is where the fight really is. Sit inside gratitude and you are standing close to Hashem, where “oz v’chedvah bimkomo,” strength and joy are in His place.


And notice when bikkurim happens. Not at the end of the harvest, out of the surplus. From the reishis, the very first fruit, before you have any idea how the rest of the season will go. So write the list in the morning, before the day has told you anything, so the day opens on a good note instead of only closing on one. A second list at night, for the good that actually turned up, is better still.


A member of a 12-step group wrote his list every day for months without missing, including the nights he remembered only at two in the morning and fell asleep partway through it. “After seven months like that I felt an enormous change. Before, no matter how good things were around me, there was a point inside me that always felt bad, always unsatisfied. Now it’s the reverse. Even when things around me are very hard, that inner point feels good, and it gives me the strength to get through everything.”


V’samachta b’chol hatov. And you shall rejoice in all the good. It sits in the parsha a few pesukim after the bikkurim declaration, and it reads less like a blessing than an instruction.

WISDOM FROM THE FORUM

“How Do I Deal With the
Shame After a Fall?”

Few things in this struggle hurt like the hours after a fall. The depression, the self-loathing, the quiet voice saying you’re hopeless, it’s one of the most common experiences members bring to the forum, and one of the most dangerous, because the shame itself is often what sets up the next fall. Here’s the hard-won wisdom of the chevra.


Start by understanding what the shame actually is. It feels like conscience, but it functions like a weapon, and it belongs to the other side. @skeptical says it plainly: “Feelings of shame and depression don’t help the situation at all. In fact, in most cases, these feelings make things worse.” The Yetzer Hara uses shame and depression to keep you down, because a person crushed by self-hatred is a person who’s easier to knock over again. Recognizing that the shame is part of the trap, not part of the teshuva, is the first step to refusing it.


So the central move is simple, if not easy: get back up immediately, and don’t let the fall become a spiral. @puremind names the exact lie to watch for, the voice that says, “You’re already down, just enjoy till the end, and then get back up.” That “might as well finish” mindset turns one slip into a binge, and it has to be rejected on the spot. Instead, @tzitzis dude applies a principle from Mesillas Yesharim: act like a zariz, a diligent person, and you’ll become one. Get right back into your normal routine and live as if the fall didn’t derail you. Or, as @bardichev put it in his unmistakable style: “Fell shmell. Depressed? No way! KOT”, keep on trucking.


Crucially, don’t hand over your progress. A fall does not erase what came before it. @momo reminds a struggling member, “You haven’t lost your two clean weeks.” Every clean day before the fall still counts, still happened, still proves what you’re capable of. @hashemssoldier chooses to be happy for the clean days Hashem gave him rather than letting the fall poison them, and @momo adds a staggering thought: “Think of how many angels you created fighting the Yetzer Hara for that hour!” The clean time wasn’t wasted. It was a victory that still stands.


Then get your eyes off the past and onto the only thing you can control. @skeptical advises, “Forget the past, you can’t change what happened,” and as @cordnoy says it: “Today!” The past is closed; the only choice you actually have is the one in front of you right now. That’s why @learning committed to posting every single day as a fresh start, one day at a time.


Don’t carry it alone, because isolation is where shame does its worst work. @2restart describes sleeping for days and being unable to face his wife or anyone after a fall, and that withdrawal only deepens the shame. The antidote is to open up. @some-guy found real relief the first time he shared his struggle instead of hiding it, and @tzitzis dude reassured him, “Your reaction is mamesh normal, so don’t fret too much.” Knowing others have stood exactly where you’re standing takes a surprising amount of the shame’s power away.


It also helps to turn the fall from a verdict into a lesson. @yiraishamaim asks the productive question: “Can you identify what really went wrong, so that with Hashem’s help you can prevent it next time?”, and in his own case he realized he’d gotten lax on his gedarim. @mr.clean takes the same stance: “I’m going to take this as a lesson, and learn how to better protect myself in the future.” And @inastruggle turns it into a concrete step: “Just break that pattern. You know what makes the fall, make a taphsic or kabbalah to keep away from it.” That’s regret aimed at building, not at self-destruction.


Finally, hold onto the bigger picture, because it’s far more hopeful than shame will let you believe. When @dd felt hopeless after falling once in five months, @gevura shebyesod shot back: “You fell once in five months and you call that ‘no hope’? I’d call that major progress!” The fall you’re ashamed of may actually be sitting on top of real growth. As @syatadshmaya says after his own dark stretches, there is always hope.


The bottom line? The shame after a fall feels like the truth, but it’s really the Yetzer Hara’s last move, an attempt to turn one slip into surrender. Don’t take the bait. Get up right away, keep the clean days you earned, focus only on today, reach out instead of hiding, mine the fall for one lesson, and remember that a single fall on top of real progress is still real progress. The goal was never to never fall. It’s to get back up, every single time.

7 KEYS TO A STRONGER MARRIAGE · KEY #3

Turn Toward Each Other, Not Away

Adapted from the research of Dr. John Gottman


The first two keys were about knowing your spouse and nurturing admiration for them. This week’s key is about the small, ordinary moments where a marriage is actually built, or quietly eroded.


Gottman discovered something that surprised even him. The strength of a marriage isn’t decided mainly in the big, dramatic conversations. It’s decided in the tiny, almost invisible moments throughout an ordinary day, moments he called “bids for connection.” A bid is any small gesture that says, in effect, “notice me, be with me for a second.” Your wife points out a bird at the window. Your husband sighs over a headline. One of them says, “look at this,” or “how was your day?” These look like nothing. They’re everything.


In each of these moments, you have a choice: to turn toward the bid, by responding, engaging, showing you’re there, or to turn away, by ignoring it, brushing it off, or staying buried in your phone. Gottman found that in the marriages that thrived, spouses turned toward each other’s bids the overwhelming majority of the time. In the marriages that fell apart, they turned away far more often. And it usually wasn’t the huge betrayals that did the damage, it was the steady accumulation of small moments where one partner reached out and the other wasn’t there.


The Torah’s “emotional bank account”


Gottman describes this as building up an “emotional bank account”, every time you turn toward a bid, you make a small deposit, and over years those deposits become a reserve of goodwill and trust that carries a couple through hard times. This is a deeply Torah idea. The Sefer HaChinuch writes that a person is shaped by the accumulation of small actions far more than by grand gestures, “acharei hapeulos nimshachim halevavos,” the heart is drawn after the deeds, and the deeds that matter most are the repeated small ones. A marriage, like a person, is built from the little things done again and again.


There’s a beautiful hint in how the Torah describes the ideal marriage: reyim ahuvim, beloved friends. Think about what actually makes a friendship close. It’s rarely one dramatic event. It’s the hundred small moments of showing up, of being interested, of turning toward. The same is true, even more so, of the friendship at the heart of a marriage.


Small and often beats big and rare


Here’s the freeing part of Gottman’s finding: you don’t need grand romantic gestures to build a strong marriage. In fact, couples who rely only on the occasional big gesture, the anniversary dinner, the expensive gift, while turning away in the daily moments, tend to struggle. What builds lasting closeness is the small stuff, done consistently. A two-second response. Looking up when they walk in. Asking a follow-up question. Putting the phone down when they start to talk. These cost almost nothing, and they are the actual currency of a marriage.


Your Action Steps This Week


  1. Notice the bids. This week, just pay attention to how often your spouse makes a small bid for your attention, a comment, a question, a “look at this.” You’ll likely be surprised how many there are once you’re watching for them.
  2. Turn toward, especially when it’s inconvenient. Pick the moments you’d normally brush off, when you’re tired, mid-task, on your phone, and turn toward instead. Look up. Respond. Give the two seconds. Those are the deposits that count most.
  3. Put the phone down first. The single biggest source of “turning away” today is a screen. When your spouse starts to talk, make it your habit to physically put the phone down and face them. Nothing says “you matter to me” faster.

JOYFUL TESHUVA · PART 3

Why Going Over the Past Keeps Pulling You Back

Last time we said that for an ingrained behavior the order of teshuva is reversed: leaving the behavior comes first, and the regret comes after. We also promised to come back and explain why leaning hard on the past tends to backfire on this particular struggle. This is that article.


Ask it honestly, because it sounds like the frum answer is the opposite. Is going over your aveiros not exactly what a person is supposed to be doing in Elul? How could that be the thing holding you back?


The Lakewood Mashgiach, Rav Nosson Meir Wachtfogel, brings a yesod in the name of Rav Yeruchom Levovitz that answers it directly, and he states it as a rule with a number attached (לקט רשימות, p. קט):


אמר בשם רבי ירוחם, כל השנה אפילו שהאדם נכשל צריך לחשוב הלאה על העתיד, ולהתקדם ולא לחשוב על העבר, רק יום אחד בשנה מותר לאדם לחשוב על העבר - ביום הכפורים.


All year long, even when a person stumbles, he has to think forward, about what is ahead, and keep moving. Not about the past. There is exactly one day a year on which a person is permitted to think about the past, and it is Yom Kippur.


Rav Yeruchom is not saying the past does not matter. He is saying it has a time, the time is on the calendar, and today is almost certainly not it. And he closes by naming what the rest of it is:


אבל כל השנה המהלך הוא להמשיך הלאה ולהתקדם, ולא להתעסק עם העבר, וזה חולשה של בני אדם לחיות עם העבר.


The whole year, the derech is to keep going and to move ahead, and not to busy yourself with the past. Living in the past is a human weakness. Not avodah. A weakness.


Why it backfires here specifically


For most things a person works on, replaying the failure costs him an afternoon. For this one it is not neutral, and a reader who does not know why will keep doing it and keep wondering why he is losing ground.


First, and GYE has said this in its own material for years: going over past falls in this area can itself stir up the very thing you are trying to get away from. The review is not happening at a safe distance. You are putting the images back in front of yourself and calling it teshuva.


Second, it quietly changes the subject. An hour spent on what you did last Thursday is an hour not spent on what is different about next Thursday. The past feels like the more serious topic, which is exactly what makes it such an effective way to avoid doing anything.


Third, and this is the one that does the damage, it is the fastest route to yiush. Rav Wolbe wrote to a bochur in exactly this matzav, in אגרות וכתבים (ח״א, letter כד): “תלמד הלאה, ובשמחה, כאילו לא קרה כלום” - keep learning, and with simcha, as though nothing happened. Despair is what the Yetzer Hara is after. Do not hand it to him.


The Mashgiach’s mashal, and it is the whole article


On the same page, someone asks the Mashgiach an ordinary question: a fellow wasted an entire seder. What should he do when the next one starts?


ענה המשגיח שישכח מסדר א׳ שלא יחשוב עליו כלל, ויתחיל ללמוד סדר זה, עם מרץ ושמחה ורציפות...! מוכרחים להתרומם!


He should forget about first seder. Not think about it at all. And start this seder, with energy, with simcha, and without interruption. We are obligated to lift ourselves up.


Notice how little heat there is in that. Nobody is ashamed of a wasted seder, so the answer comes through clean, and you can hear that it is a strategy and not a comfort. Not think about it at all. Then take the same instruction and put it where it is hard to take. You had a bad night. Second seder starts in the morning.


A few lines earlier on that page he puts it on a pasuk, and it sharpens what the danger actually is. There is a great weakness in people, he says, to stay inside the mud. That is the סכנה, and that is Hashem’s warning to Kayin: “אם תיטיב”, if you want out of the mud of the cheit, “שאת”, lift yourself and move forward. So the danger is not the fall. It is the sitting there afterwards, going over it.


For now, not forever


None of this cancels anything. The Steipler, in קריינא דאגרתא, writes to someone in the middle of this struggle: “במה שנוגע על העבר אין לחשוב לעת עתה כלל”, as far as the past is concerned, do not think about it at all לעת עתה, for now. Two words doing a great deal of work. He does not say never. He says not now, and in the same breath he says what to do instead, which is to be occupied with Torah. It lines up with where Rabbeinu Yonah left us last time: the charata comes, just afterward. Nothing is written off in either place. The regret is real, it is yours, and it is being put where it can do its work instead of where it will be turned against you.


So the answer to when is Yom Kippur, and it is the same answer for everybody. If you want more than one appointment in the year, take one, whether that is erev Rosh Chodesh or a set time with a rebbi or a sponsor. The point of putting it on the calendar is not to be strict. It is that a cheshbon you chose, at a time you chose, with your head clear, is a completely different act from the one that ambushes you at one in the morning twenty minutes after a fall. Same words, opposite result. And the rest of the year, when it comes up on its own in the middle of a seder or in the car or in bed, you are allowed to put it down. Not because you are letting yourself off, but because you have a time for it and this is not it.


HOMEWORK

This week, notice the difference between the two things, because from the inside they feel identical and they are not. One is working on the problem: what is different about tonight, what is going in the gap, who you are calling. The other is going over the last time.

When you catch yourself doing the second one, you are not being asked to feel nothing. You are being asked to say: I have a time for this, and it is not now. Then get up and go do the first one.

And pick your time. Yom Kippur is the one everybody has. If you want a second one before then, put it in your calendar this week.
