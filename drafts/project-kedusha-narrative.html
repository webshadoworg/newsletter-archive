Kedusha in Every Home

Three new resources that put every answer in one place, for the family that has been making these decisions alone.

It is eleven at night in Lakewood, and a father is standing in his kitchen holding a phone that isn’t his.

He has just seen what his son saw. He doesn’t know how long it has been going on, or whether to take the phone away tonight or say nothing until morning. What he does know is that there is nobody to ask. Not the neighbor, whose son sits next to his in class. Not his Rav, at this hour, about this.

So he does what everybody does. He opens a browser and types the sentence he cannot say out loud. A thousand answers come back, not one of them written for a father with one question about one child.

Until this year, that is where the story ended.

He is not the only one asking. Questions like these reach Guard Your Eyes every day, from around the world.

A father in Lakewood: “My son saw something on his phone, what do I do?”
A rebbi in Flatbush: “I need help with a Talmid”
A mother in Yerushalayim: “We can’t afford a filter”
A bochur, alone, at two in the morning: “I gave my word to my Rav and broke it again”...

Most of It Isn’t an Emergency

That father’s night is the extreme case. Most of what a family needs is quieter, and far more common.

A work laptop the children sometimes borrow, which cannot be locked down without breaking the software you earn a living with.

A house where filtering every device properly costs more than the family can spend, and no way to know which cheaper option is good enough.

Parental controls that are switched on, and a twelve-year-old who worked around them in an afternoon.

A bar mitzvah bochur getting his first phone next month. What do you say, at what age, in which words?

A Motzaei Shabbos with bored children and nothing kosher to put on.

An adult with a perfectly kosher phone who reaches for it every free minute, which is not a kedusha problem at all.

Not one of those is a crisis. All of them are decisions, and every one has an answer somebody has already worked out. Most of us make them alone anyway, quickly, and hope for the best. That is what was missing all along. Not answers. An address.

Every Answer Exists. Nobody Could Find It.

Centuries ago a Yid with a practical question in halacha faced something of the same kind. The answer existed, scattered across Bavli, Yerushalmi and the Rishonim, and most people had no way to find it, until Rav Yosef Karo organized it. Lehavdil, that is our challenge.

“We stopped asking what else GYE should build. The question became: why is there no single place a Yid can go with any question in this area and simply get an answer?”

Finding them was not GYE’s expertise either. Nineteen years of helping a Yid who is already struggling is one thing. Knowing which filter is genuinely the best one this month, on which device, at what price, in which kehilla and in which language, is research rather than chizuk, and it changes weekly. So GYE went and found the people who do it. Project Kedusha is a partnership with Mishor in Lakewood, whose business is exactly that.

It reaches Klal Yisroel this Elul in three parts. The Kedusha Guide, in print. Kedusha.com, the same library alive online, answering in your own words. And the Kedusha Hotline, 866-KEDUSHA, AI-powered and answering now, for anyone without internet. One address, free, anonymous, at any hour of the night.

What Is Actually in It

The Kedusha Guide runs close to 100 full-color pages, the first of its kind: 230 vetted resources from dozens of organizations across six sections. Kedusha Safeguards. Kedusha Education. Kedusha Chizuk. Kedusha Struggles. Kosher Isle. Screen Addiction.

Safeguards is the largest section and the most practical. Every filter in America and in Eretz Yisroel, compared. Every kosher phone, by region and community standard. Routers. Accountability software for the man who must keep open internet. Kosher AI tools. All 70-plus TAG offices plus Project STEP, for a family that would rather somebody came and did it. The devices people forget, the Xbox, the smart TV, the Tesla. And call-in lines for rides and bills, for those with no internet at all.

Education runs by age, from a book for a four-year-old to pre-marriage material, with school curriculums and resources for a rebbi with one struggling talmid. Chizuk is video, seforim and shul-wide programs. Struggles is mentoring, spouse support and frum therapy referrals. Kosher Isle is what you put on instead. And Screen Addiction is the section nobody expects, because it isn’t about content at all: lock boxes, timers, whole communities unplugging together.

Wherever you see the GYE badge, that resource is ours. Everything else is somebody else’s work, printed with their name on it.

Rav Yosef Elefent of the Mir called it a game changer. Rav Elya Brudny shlita his own words and exact title: need from you. And the goal is not a print run. The goal is a copy in every Jewish home. Shuls, schools and events across America and Eretz Yisroel are how it gets there, because when a book sits on a shelf in the house, where a child can see it and a guest can pick it up, it normalizes the conversation. No searching. No shame.

You Can Just Ask

The Guide is a yearly edition, rebuilt each year against what actually changed, and it can be printed at home. But a year is a long time in technology, which is why the same library is alive at Kedusha.com, corrected week by week with Mishor. When a new filter launches, Kedusha.com already knows. A recommendation that stops being right stops being given.

Then it does something no book has ever done.

The father in the kitchen types one sentence: “I found my son watching things he shouldn’t.” What answers him is not a page of search results. It does what a wise mentor would. It asks his son’s age, the device, what the family can spend, which community, which language. Only then does it narrow 230 resources to the few that fit him, and tell him how the conversation in the morning should sound.

So the mother in Yerushalayim gets Israeli filters, not American ones. Anonymously, instantly, without judgment. Every conversation is reviewed by our team, so the site learns and shapes next year’s edition.

“A man will type at two in the morning what he could never say out loud at two in the afternoon. Our only job is to make sure that what answers him is Torah, and that a human being is standing right behind it.”

Nineteen Years to Get Here

Guard Your Eyes is nineteen years old, founded by Yaakov Nadel and Yechezkel Stelzer with the guidance of Rabbi Dr. Abraham Twerski zt”l and, yibadel lechaim, Rav Aharon Feldman shlita. Tens of thousands have broken free using GYE’s three pillars: motivation, planning, connection. Behind every number: marriages rebuilt, self-respect restored, fathers present again.

Prevention was never an afterthought either. GYE helped build the VCF filter in 2013, which became GenTech, built Webshadow for parent-child accountability, and launched Project STEP for families who cannot pay. The Kedusha Challenge has run in more than forty shuls, thirty days led by a Rav in his own kehilla, and Esa Einai has done the same for thousands of teens.

Every Jewish Bookshelf

Remember those questions? The father has an address. The rebbi has a library. The mother who couldn’t afford a filter has Project STEP. And the bochur, alone at two in the morning, is not alone anymore.

GYE has been identified with crisis, and crisis is hard to speak about, so many never reach out at all. Project Kedusha changes the subject. The Guide belongs on a shelf in the house not because something went wrong, but because every family is already making these decisions, and until this year most of us made them alone.

Elul is the season of kabbalos that rarely survive Cheshvan. Here is one that will. Pick one thing in the house: a conversation you keep putting off, a device with no real filter, one evening with something kosher on it. The Guide will tell you how.

Every family deserves a trusted place to turn. Project Kedusha. One small step for countless individuals. One giant upgrade to Klal Yisroel’s Kedusha.

How to get the Kedusha Guide

A new edition is published every year; the current one is available now. Shuls, schools and organizations can request copies in quantity; an individual copy can be ordered link or number: need from you, and a printable edition is available. Everything in it, kept current with Mishor, lives at kedusha.com. The phone line is 866-KEDUSHA.

None of it runs on its own. GYE’s annual campaign, dates and goal: need from you, puts a Guide on the shelf of a home that would never have thought to ask.
