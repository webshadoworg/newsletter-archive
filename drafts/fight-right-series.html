FIGHT RIGHT · POST 1

The First Three Minutes

Based on Fight Right by Drs. John and Julie Gottman


Most of us were never taught how to fight with the person we love. We just do it, and then we live with whatever is left in the room. A hard conversation at home can leave a man raw, and that kind of stress is a common place for the yetzer hara to show up.


This series is about fighting well. The happiest couples fight too. The Gottmans found that the difference is how they start, how they stop, and how they come back to each other afterward.


You walk in from a long day and the kitchen is still a mess from lunch. The words are out before you’ve put down your keys.


“Do you ever clean up around here? I can’t come home to this every night.” She stiffens. The rest of the evening is decided in that first breath. Nobody is talking about the dishes anymore.


The Gottmans call this a harsh start-up. In Fight Right they name it the Bomb Drop: you open with blame or criticism, often with no warning, and the other person has nowhere to go but defense.


In a study of 124 newlywed couples, they found that the first three minutes of a disagreement already said a great deal about how the marriage would be doing six years later. The couples who later divorced had all started those talks with more negativity and less warmth.


The way out is what they call a softened start-up. John Gottman puts it simply: you point the finger at yourself, not at your partner.


The formula is plain. I feel this, about this situation, and I need this.


So “You never clean up” becomes: “I feel wiped out when I walk in and the kitchen is still messy from lunch. Can we take ten minutes after supper and do it together?”


You are naming a feeling, describing a situation, and asking for a specific kind of help. She can hear that. A character attack she cannot.


Leave last week’s complaints out of it. One issue, said cleanly, is enough.


You will not get the first line perfect every time. If it comes out hard, you can still stop and start over. “Let me try that again” already changes the temperature.


If she is the one who starts hard, you still have a move. Don’t meet a hard opening with a harder one. A calm response in those first minutes can keep the conversation from sliding.


This week, before you raise something that’s been sitting on you, write the opening line down. Check that it starts with what you feel, not what’s wrong with her. Then say that line, and stop.


FIGHT RIGHT · POST 2

When You’re Flooded

Based on Fight Right by Drs. John and Julie Gottman


The conversation started small, something about who was supposed to call the landlord. Ten minutes later your jaw is tight, your heart is going, and her words are arriving as noise.


You hear yourself getting sharper, or you go quiet and stare at the wall. Either way, you are not solving anything.


The Gottmans call this flooding. It is not just being upset. When you feel cornered, the body moves into fight or flight, and stress hormones take over.


In that state, they say, you cannot think straight and you cannot hear well. It is a terrible place to keep talking from, and a hard place to stay steady.


A lot of us grew up with the idea that you should never go to sleep angry, so we push through. The Gottmans say the opposite, if one of you is flooded.


Pause the fight, and say when you’ll come back.


The break can be as short as twenty minutes or as long as a day. Stay long enough for both of you to cool down, and not so long that the thing just sits there. Agree on a time, then leave the room.


The hard part is what you do with those minutes. Don’t replay what she said, and don’t draft your next argument.


Don’t sit there feeling like the injured party. Go for a walk. Give your body a chance to let the stress drain off, then come back at the time you said.


You may feel rude cutting a conversation off. Say it as care, not as a slam of the door: “I want to get this right, and I can’t do that while I’m this heated.”


If she is the one who asks for the break, honor it the same way. Don’t follow her down the hall to finish your point.


A break that becomes a disappearance is just another way of closing the door. Coming back, even if the conversation is still hard, is what makes the pause an act of care.


This week, agree on a simple line you can both use: “I’m getting overwhelmed. Can we take twenty minutes and pick this up at eight?” Then use the time to calm down, and be there when you said you would.

What You Can’t Give Up

Based on Fight Right by Drs. John and Julie Gottman


You’ve had this conversation before. She wants her parents for the second days of Yom Tov. You want to stay home, sleep in your own bed, daven in your own shul.


You’ve each explained your side more than once. Now you’re just defending it. Somebody has to win, and neither of you is going to.


The Gottmans call this the Standoff: a fight in which both people feel they have to win. The longer it runs, the more the original question disappears, and all that’s left is who will give in.


Their way out is a simple exercise they named the Bagel Method, after its shape. Each of you takes a paper and draws two rings, a smaller one inside a larger one.


In the inner ring, write what you truly cannot give up. In the outer ring, write what you could flex on, if the center were honored.


Your inner ring might say: I need a bit of rest, and I want to daven in the place I feel at home. Your outer ring might say: we could go for part of Yom Tov, or we could host her parents here, or we could switch off years.


Her inner ring might be: I don’t want my parents to feel dropped. Her outer ring may have more room than you think.


Then you trade papers. Ask why the inner ring matters so much. Look at where the outer rings overlap, and build a plan from there.


If everything lands in the center, nothing is actually protected. Be honest about what is truly core, and leave the rest in the outer ring, where there is room to move.


Couples are often surprised by how much flexibility was there once the core was named and respected. You’re not looking for a clone of yourself. You’re looking for a way that both of you can live with, without anyone having to lose.


This week, pick one stuck disagreement and actually draw the two rings. Don’t do it in your head in the middle of a fight. Do it at the table, when you’re both calm, and read each other’s paper out loud.

The Fight After the Fight

Based on Fight Right by Drs. John and Julie Gottman


Shabbos is over. The fight was Thursday night, about something small that got big.


You made up, kind of. You both said “fine.”


But the air in the house is still off. She is polite. You are careful.


The thing you fought about is sitting in the room with you, and nobody is touching it.


The Gottmans call this the Chasm in the Room. The mistake is not that you fought. It is that you never went back to what happened, so the distance just stays.


A fight that isn’t processed doesn’t disappear. It waits.


Their advice is to return to it later, when you’re both calm, almost like watching a replay together. Don’t try this in the same hour as the argument.


There is no official transcript. Each of you only has your own version.


The point is not to agree on a single story. The point is to hear the other one.


They walk couples through five steps. First, share what you felt, not the case you were building. Second, describe the fight as you experienced it, and listen while she describes hers, without jumping in to correct her.


Third, name what set you off, the old nerve the moment touched. Fourth, own your part, even if it’s small. Fifth, make one concrete plan for how you’ll handle it next time.


You don’t have to agree that her version is the official one. You do have to let it be real for her.


This is not reopening a wound for its own sake. It’s closer to teshuva between two people: you look at what happened, you take responsibility, and you choose a different way next time. Then the next hard moment doesn’t have to start on top of the last one.


If the conversation starts to get hot again, stop. This is a debrief, not a second fight.


This week, pick one recent fight that still feels unfinished. Ask her, “Can we talk about the other night for a few minutes? I want to understand how it landed for you.” Then go through the five steps, and let her go first.
