Like New Every Single Day

This week we read Ki Savo, "when you will enter the Land," which begins with the commandment to bring the first fruits to the Beis Hamikdash in Yerushalayim, the mitzvah of bikkurim. But there's a pasuk later in the parsha, after bikkurim and a number of other mitzvos, that hits on something I think plagues all of us, to varying degrees, at one time or another.

"Hayom hazeh Hashem Elokecha m'tzavcha la'asos es hachukim v'es hamishpatim," "On this day, Hashem your G-d commands you to perform these decrees and statutes." (Devarim 26:16)

Why does the pasuk say Hashem is commanding us "on this day"? Hadn't most of the mitzvos already been given at Har Sinai?

The Ramban explains that Moshe had just now completed teaching Bnei Yisrael all of the mitzvos, so it was only from that day that they were fully commanded to keep all of the laws and statutes.

Rashi, though, takes a different approach. Quoting the Midrash, he explains: every day, the mitzvos should be k'chadashim, like new in your eyes, as if you had just been commanded them today.

Think about how powerful that is. Imagine Hashem came forth and spoke to you directly, giving you clear instructions about exactly what actions are in your best interest. Imagine the motivation, the determination you'd feel to carry them out. According to Rashi, the pasuk is asking us to feel that way every single day, k'chadashim, like new.

But how do you maintain that freshness? We human beings have a tremendous capacity to get used to things.

Rav Volbe, in Alei Shur, deals with exactly this, the cycle of spiritual ups and downs we're all subject to. How at times avodas Hashem feels fresh and alive, and at other times we feel like robots going through the motions. And how, in those flat stretches, we start to question ourselves: Is this really me? Where did the feelings go? Maybe this whole thing just isn't for me.

He quotes the Sefer HaYashar, that a person has to understand from the very start that this is part and parcel of spiritual growth. The ups and downs, the swings between closeness and distance, are an intrinsic part of the process. In fact, having those feelings is as clear a sign as you could ask for that this is exactly where you belong. This is for you.

It would be like giving up baseball because you struck out once. "Well, I guess baseball isn't for me." Or quitting because you missed a foul shot. Of course not, it's part of the game.

But if that's true, the pasuk gets even harder. If the ups and downs are inevitable and built into growth, how can I be commanded to feel as though it's all new? I've done it so many times, and right now I feel distant and detached.

The Sefer HaYashar writes further that the single factor determining whether a person feels close in his avodas Hashem or detached from it is chidush, newness. You keep your service from going stale by constantly searching for new insight, new understanding, finding chidush in yourself and in what you do. The chidush is what makes everything k'chadashim.

And perhaps that's the answer to our pasuk. We're commanded to see the Torah as newly given each day. How can we do that, and how can we be commanded to do something that seems beyond our nature? Perhaps the command is in fact very tangible: work at finding and infusing freshness into the mitzvos you do. Study. Search. Open your eyes. Open your heart. Do that, and you'll minimize the downs and maximize the ups, short bouts of detachment surrounded by long stretches of closeness.

For those of us in this struggle, this is everything. The fight gets old. The same battle, the same triggers, the same setbacks, day after day, and the flatness itself becomes dangerous, because a heart that feels nothing is a heart that's easier to catch off guard. Rashi's answer is that the flatness isn't proof you're failing, and it isn't proof this isn't for you. It's part of the process, the surest sign that you're exactly where you belong. And the way out of the flat stretch isn't to wait for the feeling to come back on its own. It's to actively seek chidush, one new insight, one fresh reason, one small thing that makes today's fight today's, and not a stale rerun of yesterday's. Find the chidush, and even a battle you've fought a thousand times can feel k'chadashim.

Rashi, Devarim 26:16; Rav Shlomo Wolbe, Alei Shur, citing the Sefer HaYashar.

When the Fight Goes Flat

There's a stretch nobody warns you about. You're not falling. You're not white-knuckling either. You're just doing it. Same fences, same davening, same fight, and none of it moves you anymore. Then the thought arrives: maybe I've outgrown this. Maybe it was never real. Maybe this isn't for me.

This week's parsha has a pasuk for exactly that. "Hayom hazeh Hashem Elokecha m'tzavcha la'asos es hachukim v'es hamishpatim," "On this day, Hashem your G-d commands you to perform these decrees and statutes." (Devarim 26:16)

Why "on this day"? The mitzvos were given at Har Sinai forty years earlier. The Ramban answers plainly: Moshe had only now finished teaching them, so that was the day the obligation was complete. Rashi, quoting the Midrash, says something else. Every day the mitzvos should be k'chadashim, new in your eyes, as if you had been commanded them today.

Which sounds impossible. You've done this thousands of times, and nobody can decide to feel something new about a thing he's done a thousand times.

But notice what kind of sentence Rashi is explaining. The word is m'tzavcha, commands you. Whatever k'chadashim is, it sits inside a command, and a person can't be commanded to have a feeling. He can only be commanded to do something.

Perhaps that's the whole idea, and it turns the problem inside out. You are not waiting for the fight to feel new. Making it new is the assignment.

Rav Volbe, in Alei Shur, takes up this exact cycle: how avodas Hashem is alive some weeks and mechanical others, and how in the flat stretches a person begins to suspect himself. He brings the Sefer HaYashar, that the swings are not a malfunction but the design, and that feeling them is the clearest evidence a person could ask for that he is in the right place. Then comes the operative line. The single factor that decides whether a person feels close or detached is chidush, newness. Not newness that arrives on its own. Newness he goes and finds.

Which needs a size, because "find chidush" on its own is advice nobody can act on. So: before you walk into today's fight, get one thing you didn't have yesterday. One line of a sefer you'd never seen. One reason this matters to you that you'd never put into words. One detail about your own trigger you'd never noticed, what time it starts, what feeling comes right before it. One new thing, and today's battle is today's, not a rerun of yesterday's.

For us this isn't a nice thought. A heart that feels nothing is the easiest heart in the world to catch off guard, so the flatness is more dangerous than the urge. And Rashi isn't asking you to summon a feeling you don't have. He's handing you a job small enough to do this morning. Do it, and a fight you've fought a thousand times becomes one you've never fought before.

Rashi, Devarim 26:16; Rav Shlomo Wolbe, Alei Shur, citing the Sefer HaYashar.

Nobody Can Fake Freshness

This isn't about the day after a fall. It's about month fourteen. You haven't fallen. The fences are up, you're doing everything you were doing, and none of it registers anymore. The fight has turned into a formality. That isn't failure and it isn't a fall. It's what happens to anything a person has done a thousand times.

"Hayom hazeh Hashem Elokecha m'tzavcha la'asos es hachukim v'es hamishpatim," "On this day, Hashem your G-d commands you to perform these decrees and statutes." (Devarim 26:16) Why "on this day," when the mitzvos had been given forty years earlier? The Ramban answers plainly: Moshe had only now finished teaching them. Rashi, quoting the Midrash, says something else. Every day the mitzvos should be k'chadashim, new in your eyes, as though you had been commanded them today.

Which sounds like being commanded to have a feeling, and nobody can do that. You can't decide to be moved by something you've done a thousand times, any more than you can decide to be surprised by a joke you already know.

But look at what freshness is actually made of. The reason something stops moving you isn't that time passed. It's that you ran out of new information about it. Nothing you know about this fight is any newer than it was last year: the same reason to fight, the same understanding of what's at stake, the same picture of yourself. Of course it's gone flat. You're running on last year's material.

And that you can be commanded. You can't decide to feel something new. You can decide to know something new. Rashi is commanding the second one, and the first one follows it.

Which gives the thing a size, and the size is small. Before you walk into today, get one piece of this you didn't have yesterday. One line of a sefer you'd never seen. One reason it matters to you that you'd never actually put into words. One detail about your own trigger you'd never noticed, what time it starts, what feeling shows up right before it. The Big Bikkurim Speech

The parsha opens with bikkurim, and it's easy to picture it as a farming mitzvah: the first figs ripen, you carry them up to Yerushalayim. But bikkurim is really two mitzvos. There's bringing the fruit, and there's mikra bikkurim, the declaration you make out loud while you're standing there holding it. The fruit by itself isn't the mitzvah. The fruit plus the speech is the mitzvah. And it isn't a quick thank you. Look at what the declaration actually says. Not "thank You for the fruit," but the reason: we went down to Mitzrayim, we were afflicted, we cried out, He took us out with a strong hand and brought us to this place, and this is what grew here. The mitzvah is saying why.

While real bikkurim is only done in the Beis Hamikdosh, the idea behind it – Hakaras Hatov and gratitude, and making a ceremony out of it – is an amazing tool that is especially useful for those of us who are struggling: writing down, every day, a list of things you're grateful for. Open each line however you like. "I'm grateful for…" "Thank You for…" "Modeh ani lefanecha that…" The wording matters very little. Doing it daily matters a lot. And it works even when it feels forced at the start, which for most people it does.

Researchers at the University of Pennsylvania asked people to write down three things that had gone well that day, and why, every night for one week. One week, and then nothing. A month later they were happier than when they started. Six months later they still were.

Notice what they were asked to write: three things, and why. That's the same point bikkurim is making. The general thank yous are covered for us already; the whole morning is built out of them, brachos for opening our eyes, for standing up straight, for the ground under our feet. What the siddur can't supply is a line of your own, about something in your own day, with one word attached to it: because. Not "I'm grateful for my home." "I'm grateful for my home, because…" and then finish the sentence. What is it about this one that you appreciate? Answering that takes a minute of actual thinking. A list you rattle off is a routine. A list you have to think about does something to you.

Why should a list on a piece of paper do anything at all? Because of where the pull actually comes from. Most of us don't go looking for taavah when we feel good. We go looking when we feel bad, and taavah is what's offering comfort. The bad feeling is the delivery mechanism.

Which puts kvetching in a rather different light. A running complaint about the traffic, the job, the tone somebody used with us, feels like nothing. It's a mood, not an aveirah. But every kvetch is a small announcement that things are not good, and a man who has been making those announcements since morning is a man already looking for some relief. That is the mood the whole thing waits for, because the relief on offer is giving in. Tilt the day the other way and it runs the other way with it: gratitude leaves a person happier, and a happier person is much harder to catch.

And that is where the fight really is. Sit inside gratitude and you are standing close to Hashem, where "oz v'chedvah bimkomo," strength and joy are in His place.

And notice when bikkurim happens. Not at the end of the harvest, out of the surplus. From the reishis, the very first fruit, before you have any idea how the rest of the season will go. So write the list in the morning, before the day has told you anything, so the day opens on a good note instead of only closing on one. A second list at night, for the good that actually turned up, is better still.

A member of a 12-step group wrote his list every day for months without missing, including the nights he remembered only at two in the morning and fell asleep partway through it. "After seven months like that I felt an enormous change. Before, no matter how good things were around me, there was a point inside me that always felt bad, always unsatisfied. Now it's the reverse. Even when things around me are very hard, that inner point feels good, and it gives me the strength to get through everything."

V'samachta b'chol hatov. And you shall rejoice in all the good. It sits in the parsha a few pesukim after the bikkurim declaration, and it reads less like a blessing than an instruction.
