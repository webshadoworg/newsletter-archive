How the Green Circle Could Have Prevented the Chet Eitz Hadaas

The Meshech Chochmah (Bereishis 2:16) says Hashem gave Adam two mitzvos in Gan Eden, not one. “Of every tree of the garden you shall surely eat” is a mitzvah in its own right, he writes, to keep body and soul going and to enjoy the peiros of the Gan. Which is why, he says, the cheit could have been avoided:


Had Adam told Chava that Hashem commanded them to eat from the Gan, then even without kavanah for the mitzvah, doing it would have protected her from the Eitz HaDaas. But Adam never told her that eating was a mitzvah from Hashem, so she never had the mitzvah. All Chava had in her head was the issur, and an issur alone did not protect her.


She had the no. Nobody told her the yes. What would have held her was not a better answer to the nachash. What could have helped was focusing on the good, on the fruit she had a mitzvah to eat all along.


We often assume that first you break free from the bad, sur mera, and only then can you start building the good, asei tov. Clean the house, then furnish it.


R’ Aharon of Karlin, the Beis Aharon, taught the opposite, “Teshuvah, for people on our level, is sur mera through aseh tov,” turning from evil by doing good.



Why not simply fight the bad head-on? Because, the Beis Aharon explained, wherever your thoughts go, that is where you end up. A person is where he thinks. Someone who spends his days wrestling with a sin spends his mind inside that sin, even as he tries to escape it.


The Ohr HaChaim (Vayikra 18:2) makes the same point on the verse “Do not follow the practices of the land of Egypt.” The way to overcome a desire, he writes, is to distance it from your mind entirely and treat it as nothing, rather than wrestling with it. Even prolonged thought aimed at rejecting a desire keeps it in the mind and strengthens it. He reads Shlomo HaMelech’s advice the same way: “My son, if sinners entice you, al toveh” (Mishlei 1:10). Al toveh means more than “say no.” It means don’t enter into argument with it.


So what does a person do with a desire he doesn’t want? Not a battle. A turn. He doesn’t stare at the darkness and try to push it away. He turns toward something good, a page of learning, a mitzvah, a kind word, a real task, and lets the good fill the space the struggle used to occupy.

This is what the green circle is (in the GYE 3-Circle Plan tool). It's not a nice extra next to the real work, it is the real work. A day filled, on purpose, with good things, so that the mind has somewhere positive to be. Lust doesn’t leave because we defeated it in debate. It leaves because we stopped giving it our minds and gave them to something better.



FROM A MEMBER · DAY 121

How Bookending with a Mentor Saved the Day
(and Got My Phone into a Lake!)

by @winningdaybyday, on the forum, chol hamoed Sukkos


Day 121 of my streak.


So last night I was heading for a fall. Plain and simple. The whole plan was reviewed in my head.


Now I had prepared for this scenario with my mentor. Made up with him that if I ever head to Walmart to do this I would text him.


As I was packing up earlier in the day to head back to lakewood I called my mentor to tell him that I felt I was heading into dangerous territory (long chol hamoed, no schedule, frequent for me to fall in past chol hamoeds). He didn’t answer and I figured he’d call me back. Got back to Lakewood at night and he still hadn’t called. Been reviewing this in my mind the whole way back home and it seemed like the whole plan would work. I’d finally have that well-deserved treat I’ve held off for so long!


I’d go to Walmart instead of maariv. As I was nearing the time I knew I could leave home, I texted my mentor “SOS”, as a last cry of help. And that was all I had the strength to do.


Still no response. I almost felt validated, like this was my chance..


I drove to Walmart shaking the whole way there, knowing how bad of a decision I was making, but felt i had no choice. I bought the device.


I headed home and on the way back my mentor finally called. I didn’t answer because I really wasn’t interested in talking to him now. then he called again. Still didn’t answer.


Then I called him back.


The plan worked!!


I told him where I was at but didn’t actually use the phone yet, or [acting out]. Was just gearing up for my evening of ruin (bliss).


We talked a little and made up what I’d do.


I drove to the nearest lake and we made up I will chuck it in the water. I did it. And I emerged victorious!


Kinda felt weird, like this wasn’t me doing it. I felt like a baby. “All wired up and ready but my mentor caught me” But after talking it thru he reminded me that it was me and him that set up these barriers for that weak moment. And it was my strength that allowed me to text him and got saved.


This is the first time that I went this close to falling in 120 days and I am very grateful I did not.


This was a powerful night for me. Had a hard time calming myself down and facing reality that i would be actually clocking into day 121! I am still trying to stay heavily aware of this battle for the rest of chol hamoed so I don’t end up there tonight again.


Last night was just another proof that there is a mightier higher chance of beating this battle with a mentor at my side. After all, a war is won over many small battles, and some big ones too, and sometimes we need to call in backup reinforcements in order to win a battle.


This can be done by anyone!

MEMBER STORY

How Cutting Fantasy
Stopped My Falls

by @theboywholived, on the forum


It’s been some time since I’ve had the peace of mind necessary to post here.


I’m currently in the high 70s [days clean]. I last posted at 50. It was a rather inspirational post, at least to myself. I’m still here. I’m just not feeling the inspiration.


I am still motivated. I am done with porn. I am done with [acting out]. These are things that I do not do.


However, I’ve been noticing a lack of concentration.


For me, the only way I’ve gotten here has been with an ironclad grip on my eyes and mind. NEVER allowing myself to keep looking. NEVER allowing myself to fantasize.


I’d never realized. I thought my issue was porn. Whether visual, or audio, or words on a page, I’d thought that I just needed to stop the porn. I didn’t realize that a far bigger issue was fantasy. I would go to sleep every single night fantasizing. Whether it was things I’d seen or read, or things I wanted to do or had done, I literally fell asleep to sex every night. I didn’t even realize this. But once I decided that I have to cut out fantasy, suddenly I’ve noticed how much it had previously been a driver of my bad habits.


And in order to keep fantasy under control, I needed to keep my eyes under control. And again, I never realized how much I stared at women in the street. Frum Jewish women dressed tzniusly (or relatively tznius). Non Jews in tank tops. Everyone. I stared at every girl that I passed. As a relatively observant guy, I could probably tell you the underwear brand of most of the people whom I passed. And I cut it out. Now I can’t tell you that I don’t notice people or details that are inappropriate. I can’t tell you I never take another look or a third look. But I now have a mindset of looking away from women. All women even those who aren’t specifically attractive.


Despite all that, and its relative success, I have not been nearly as good lately. It started with looking, of course. Just being a little less careful. But a few times recently I’ve caught myself fantasizing. Not even something bad, just about my wife. But I know that I’m getting close to a slippery slope.


I don’t want to fall off.


I gotta get back to what’s worked. But it’s hard, holding on with such a good grip for so long. But relaxing even a bit could be the beginning of the end.


7 KEYS TO A STRONGER MARRIAGE · KEY #7

Build Something Holy Together

Adapted from the research of Dr. John Gottman


We’ve reached the last of the seven keys. The first six were about the daily work of a marriage, knowing your spouse, admiring them, turning toward them, letting them influence you, solving what’s solvable, and honoring the dreams behind what isn’t. This final key is about what all of that is for.


Gottman’s research found that the very strongest marriages share something beyond good communication and smooth conflict. They have what he called shared meaning, a sense that the two of you aren’t just running a household together, but building something, a shared life with shared purpose, values you hold in common, rituals that are yours, a story you’re writing together. Couples with it describe their marriage as meaningful, almost sacred. Couples without it can get along fine and still feel, underneath, that they’re just two people managing logistics side by side.


Gottman meant this in a secular way, rituals like how you do Friday night, shared goals, a sense of “this is who we are as a family.” But for us, this key isn’t a nice addition at the end. It’s the entire point, and the Torah said so long before the research.


The Shechina in the home


Rabbi Akiva taught (Sotah 17a) that the words ish, man, and isha, woman, each contain a letter of Hashem’s name, the yud and the heh. When a husband and wife merit it, those letters join and the Shechina dwells between them. When they don’t, remove Hashem’s name from the two words and all that’s left in each is aleph-shin, esh, fire, a fire that consumes.


Sit with that. A Jewish marriage was never meant to be two people simply coexisting. It’s meant to be a mishkan, a dwelling place for the Shechina, two people whose shared life is holy enough that Hashem’s presence rests inside it. That is the shared meaning the Torah is after. Not just “this is who we are as a family,” but “this home is a place where the Shechina wants to be.” And the warning in Rabbi Akiva’s words is just as real: without that shared holiness, the very same fire that could have warmed the home consumes it instead.


That’s where all seven keys have been leading. You know your spouse so that you can build with them. You admire them, turn toward them, yield to them, and honor their dreams so that the home becomes a place of peace, and a place of peace is a place the Shechina can live. The goal was never just a marriage without fighting. The goal is a marriage so full of shared purpose that Hashem’s presence dwells in it.


Your Action Steps This Week


  1. Name what you’re building. Have one conversation with your spouse that isn’t about logistics, but about meaning: what kind of home do we want this to be? What do we want our family to stand for? You may be surprised how rarely it gets said out loud.
  2. Protect one ritual that’s yours. Identify one thing the two of you do that makes your home yours, the Friday night table, a Motzaei Shabbos routine, a walk you take. Guard it. Shared rituals are the bricks of shared meaning.
  3. Make the home a place the Shechina would want to be. This week, put peace between you above being right, above the mess, above the small irritations. Every act of shalom bayis is literally an invitation to the Shechina. Send it.


This concludes our series, 7 Keys to a Stronger Marriage. May our homes be true mishkanos, full of peace, purpose, and the presence of Hashem.
