The first conversation in the Torah is a question the nachash already knew the answer to. He was not trying to convince Chava of anything. He was trying to get her to answer him, because once she was in the conversation with him, the rest took care of itself.


The Ohr HaChaim turns that into a rule, and it is the piece we lead with this week. The engagement itself is the damage, even when you are arguing the right side of it. The Yetzer Hara does not need to win the argument with you. He needs you to have it.

You Don’t Talk to a Snake

The first conversation in the Torah opens with a question the nachash already knows the answer to. “Did Hashem really say you may not eat from any tree of the garden?” He had watched them eat from the other trees. Rashi says it plainly: he knew it was false and he said it anyway, so that she would answer him.


The lie was never the point. Getting her to answer was the point.


The Ohr HaChaim (Vayikra 18:2) writes it out as a rule, on the pasuk in Mishlei, “My son, if sinners entice you, do not consent”:


“If sinners entice you,” meaning the aspect of sin. “Do not consent,” meaning do not go into claim and counter-claim with your enticements, because the drawn out engagement of the mind, even in the mode of denial, sets the desire growing and turns the will toward it.


The words he uses for the argument, toein v’nitan, are beis din words, the back and forth of a claimant and a defendant. And the Baal HaTanya uses the same vocabulary in the same place (Likkutei Amarim 28): when a machshava zara comes, “he should not answer it anything at all, no claim and no reply,” because “one who wrestles with a filthy man gets filthy himself.” Make yourself like a man who did not hear it, he says, put it out of your mind, and pour the strength into whatever you were already doing.


Even in the mode of denial


That is the line worth holding on to. The Ohr HaChaim is not warning us about entertaining the thought. He is warning us about fighting it in our heads. Twenty minutes spent proving to yourself why you are not going to do this is twenty minutes you spent on it, and you come out of them with the pull stronger than it was going in. The Yetzer Hara does not need to win the argument. He needs you to have it.


Which is exactly what happened at the tree. Chava did not agree with the nachash. She corrected him. She was on the right side of that conversation the whole way through, and it still ended with the fruit in her hand.


The research lands in the same place. Ask someone not to think about a white bear and the bear is all he can think about, because to check whether he is obeying he has to call the bear up. And people who argue hard against a position tend to come away holding a little of it, because the only way to answer a claim is to carry it around in your head first.


What to do instead


Name it cold. “A thought of taava just came up.” Not I am disgusting, not why is this happening again, not I will never. Just the flat observation, said from the outside. That sentence is already most of the work, because a thought you are standing next to is not a thought you are inside.


Give it nothing. No reasons, no vows, no negotiating over how far is too far, no inventory of what it would cost you. Not one word. You are not required to defeat it. You are required not to join it.


Move. Stand up, change rooms, call somebody, start the next thing. Not as a counter attack, which is just the argument in another form. Simply as the next thing.


Hashem told Kayin that he could rule over him. A good part of what ruling over him looks like is this: not winning the argument. Not having it.


MEMBER STORY

“I Didn’t Realize the Bigger
Issue Was Fantasy”

by @theboywholived, on the forum


It’s been some time since I’ve had the peace of mind necessary to post here.


I’m currently in the high 70s. I last posted at 50. It was a rather inspirational post, at least to myself. I’m still here. I’m just not feeling the inspiration.


I am still motivated. I am done with porn. I am done with [acting out]. These are things that I do not do.


However, I’ve been noticing a lack of concentration.


For me, the only way I’ve gotten here has been with an ironclad grip on my eyes and mind. NEVER allowing myself to keep looking. NEVER allowing myself to fantasize.


I’d never realized. I thought my issue was porn. Whether visual, or audio, or words on a page, I’d thought that I just needed to stop the porn. I didn’t realize that a far bigger issue was fantasy. I would go to sleep every single night fantasizing. Whether it was things I’d seen or read, or things I wanted to do or had done, I literally fell asleep to sex every night. I didn’t even realize this. But once I decided that I have to cut out fantasy, suddenly I’ve noticed how much it had previously been a driver of my bad habits.


And in order to keep fantasy under control, I needed to keep my eyes under control. And again, I never realized how much I stared at women in the street. Frum Jewish women dressed tzniusly (or relatively tznius.) Non Jews in tank tops. Everyone. I stared at every girl that I passed. As a relatively observant guy, I could probably tell you the underwear brand of most of the people whom I passed. And I cut it out. Now I can’t tell you that I don’t notice people or details that are inappropriate. I can’t tell you I never take another look or a third look. But I now have a mindset of looking away from women. All women even those who aren’t specifically attractive.


Despite all that, and its relative success, I have not been nearly as good lately. It started with looking, of course. Just being a little less careful. But a few times recently I’ve caught myself fantasizing. Not even something bad, just about my wife. But I know that I’m getting close to a slippery slope.


I don’t want to fall off.


I gotta get back to what’s worked. But it’s hard, holding on with such a good grip for so long. But relaxing even a bit could be the beginning of the end.


7 KEYS TO A STRONGER MARRIAGE · KEY #7

Build Something Holy Together

Adapted from the research of Dr. John Gottman


We’ve reached the last of the seven keys. The first six were about the daily work of a marriage, knowing your spouse, admiring them, turning toward them, letting them influence you, solving what’s solvable, and honoring the dreams behind what isn’t. This final key is about what all of that is for.


Gottman’s research found that the very strongest marriages share something beyond good communication and smooth conflict. They have what he called shared meaning, a sense that the two of you aren’t just running a household together, but building something, a shared life with shared purpose, values you hold in common, rituals that are yours, a story you’re writing together. Couples with it describe their marriage as meaningful, almost sacred. Couples without it can get along fine and still feel, underneath, that they’re just two people managing logistics side by side.


Gottman meant this in a secular way, rituals like how you do Friday night, shared goals, a sense of “this is who we are as a family.” But for us, this key isn’t a nice addition at the end. It’s the entire point, and the Torah said so long before the research.


The Shechina in the home


Rabbi Akiva taught (Sotah 17a) that the words ish, man, and isha, woman, each contain a letter of Hashem’s name, the yud and the heh. When a husband and wife merit it, those letters join and the Shechina dwells between them. When they don’t, remove Hashem’s name from the two words and all that’s left in each is aleph-shin, esh, fire, a fire that consumes.


Sit with that. A Jewish marriage was never meant to be two people simply coexisting. It’s meant to be a mishkan, a dwelling place for the Shechina, two people whose shared life is holy enough that Hashem’s presence rests inside it. That is the shared meaning the Torah is after. Not just “this is who we are as a family,” but “this home is a place where the Shechina wants to be.” And the warning in Rabbi Akiva’s words is just as real: without that shared holiness, the very same fire that could have warmed the home consumes it instead.


What all seven keys were for


That’s where all seven keys have been leading. You know your spouse so that you can build with them. You admire them, turn toward them, yield to them, and honor their dreams so that the home becomes a place of peace, and a place of peace is a place the Shechina can live. The goal was never just a marriage without fighting. The goal is a marriage so full of shared purpose that Hashem’s presence dwells in it.


Your Action Steps This Week


  1. Name what you’re building. Have one conversation with your spouse that isn’t about logistics, but about meaning: what kind of home do we want this to be? What do we want our family to stand for? You may be surprised how rarely it gets said out loud.
  2. Protect one ritual that’s yours. Identify one thing the two of you do that makes your home yours, the Friday night table, a Motzaei Shabbos routine, a walk you take. Guard it. Shared rituals are the bricks of shared meaning.
  3. Make the home a place the Shechina would want to be. This week, put peace between you above being right, above the mess, above the small irritations. Every act of shalom bayis is literally an invitation to the Shechina. Send it.


This concludes our series, 7 Keys to a Stronger Marriage. May our homes be true mishkanos, full of peace, purpose, and the presence of Hashem.
