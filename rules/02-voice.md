# 02 Voice

How the copy sounds. Applies to anything I write. **Never to copy a human supplied** (see
[08-working.md](08-working.md)).

## Length

- A dvar Torah is **~300 words**, not 580.
- The opening letter is **one short paragraph, 50 to 75 words**, a hook in the first line. It does
  not enumerate the articles; that is the TOC's job.
- **Two or three sentences a paragraph, not five.**
- Baseline: when Mendy rewrites my copy it comes out at **about half the length with the same
  content**. Write the half version first.
- Test before sending any section: can I delete this paragraph and lose an actual idea? If not,
  delete it.

## The AI tells, by name

1. **Hype hinges.** A sentence whose only job is to announce that the next one matters. Banned:
   "the point that changes everything", "one of the most liberating ideas in all of recovery",
   "one of the most honest questions a person can ask", "Read that again."
2. **Restating instead of advancing.** Make the point once, in its strongest form, then apply it.
   Every paragraph adds a move, not a new phrasing.
3. **Escalating triplets.** "battered, used-up, worn-down". Anaphora and rule-of-three cadence is the
   loudest tell. Once per piece at most.
4. **Manufactured objections.** "Why should a list on a piece of paper do anything at all?" invents
   the reader's scepticism. A plain question he is actually asking is fine; the manufacturing is the
   tell, not the question mark.
5. **Commentary on my own writing.** "That's worth more than it looks", "From here we see that".
6. **Em dashes as a habitual connector.** One or two in a whole piece, maximum. Ask what each is
   standing in for: a colon, a comma, or a full stop. Recasting the sentence is allowed.
7. **Any device used twice in one piece is a tic.** Before publishing, list my own paragraph openers
   and closers and count them.

## Cuts that are always right

- **The sentence whose job is to introduce the next sentence.** Open the paragraph on its content.
- **The closer that sums up.** Let the piece end on a member's own words or on the concrete rule.
  A real voice beats a neat line.
- **One example, not three. One contrast, not a rhythm.** Drop the not-X-but-Y cadence after its
  first use.
- **Whole units that repeat.** Redundancy at the paragraph and source level costs more than any
  sentence. Two sources making the same point: cut one.

## Cuts that are wrong

- **Never cut information, only texture.** Before cutting, ask what the words are carrying, not how
  they sound.
- **Comma splices are speech, not errors.** In Mendy's register, print them. Do not formalise his
  voice.

## Word choice

- **Plain words. No vocabulary from another field.** "The bad feeling is the delivery mechanism"
  became "Feeling bad is the opening it needs."
- **Name the actor.** "much harder to catch" becomes "much harder for the yetzer hara to catch."
- **Only expressions Americans actually say.** When a Hebrew source leans on an idiom, swap the whole
  image, not the wording. Same test for anything British, Israeli, or yeshivish-literal.
- **American spelling:** honor, color, behavior, realize, organize, recognize, defense, center.
  My default drifts British. The word list guard lives in `rosh-hashana-2026/arush-copy.mjs`; copy it
  into any new copy module.
- **No doubled words when patching prose.** "the hour after a fall", not "the hour of a fall".
- **"Reb Nachman", never "Rebbe Nachman".** "Reb Noson" likewise.
- **Say "every morning", not "every day"** where the point is a renewal. One is a moment you can
  picture.

## Emphasis

- **One bold run per section**, on the mechanism the piece rests on, so a skimmer who reads one thing
  gets the argument.
- **Italics on the load-bearing word.** Do not add italics on house convention; ask.
- **Title Case for every bold subtitle.** "The Ten-Point Home Improvement Plan", not "The ten-point
  home improvement plan".

## Shape of a dvar Torah

Problem first, pasuk second. The pasuk is the relief, not the premise.

1. Open cold on the problem, in a concrete scene.
2. Show what it costs.
3. Concede the honest half of the mekor. Do not argue with the reader's experience.
4. Then the pasuk, as the encouragement.
5. Anchor it in a moment he can picture.
6. One concrete rule.

The title and the opening letter follow the same order: lead with the fall, not with the verse.
**One problem per piece**, and the answer cannot be the opening question restated.

## Never write against a previous draft

The reader arrives cold and does not know what the last version said. "Not just X", "more than a
quick Y", "this isn't about Z" is dead weight and sounds defensive. Delete the old thing; do not
negate it in the text.
